### 1. Relaxation exercises may make some talk therapies work less well, huge patient-level analysis finds

**Journal:** *The Lancet Psychiatry* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 3 Aug 2026)
**PMID:** 42546730 | **DOI:** 10.1016/S2215-0366(26)00193-8
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 9/10 — individual participant data from more than 10,000 people, and the headline result is that two staples of therapy look actively unhelpful.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Researchers pooled individual participant data from 30 randomized trials (10,612 adults; 72% women, mean age 36.7) of psychosocial interventions for depression and anxiety delivered by non-specialists, then dismantled each therapy into its constituent ingredients and ran a Bayesian component network meta-analysis. Strengthening social support was by far the most beneficial component (incremental mean difference −9.48), followed by behavioral activation and problem management. Relaxation training was associated with a worse outcome (+7.97), and cognitive reframing was also on the detrimental side (+5.17). Component effects varied by baseline severity and demographics, and the team built a web app that generates personalized estimates.

**Why it matters:** If replicated, this challenges the assumption that adding more therapeutic ingredients makes a package better — and suggests the "active drug" in scalable global mental health programs is social connection, not the relaxation and thought-challenging exercises many people associate with therapy.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "The bit of therapy that might be holding you back." Open with someone doing progressive muscle relaxation homework from a self-help app, then reveal that in the largest component-level analysis to date, relaxation was the one ingredient linked to worse symptom outcomes. Call Davide Papola (University of Verona/Harvard Medical School) and co-author Pim Cuijpers; ask a UK CBT trialist for pushback, since cognitive reframing is a core CBT component and the trials here were task-shared, often in humanitarian settings.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a global health or policy outlet on the "dismantling" movement in psychotherapy research — the push to treat therapies like drug formularies with identifiable active components and dosages, and what a WHO-funded personalization web app means for delivering mental health care in countries with almost no psychiatrists.

**Caveats:** Components are inferred from a purpose-built taxonomy rather than randomized head-to-head, so "detrimental" components may be confounded by which trials used them; ethnicity data unavailable; trials were largely in low-resource and humanitarian settings, so generalization to specialist Western care is uncertain; funded by the European Commission; one author declares pharmaceutical and app-related interests.

### 2. Psychological first aid after trauma performs worse than doing nothing in a randomized trial

**Journal:** *Behaviour Research and Therapy* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 30 Jul 2026)
**PMID:** 42561812 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.brat.2026.105124
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 8/10 — a randomized test of one of the world's most widely promoted post-disaster interventions, with a genuinely uncomfortable result; limited by the analogue stress paradigm.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Eighty-four participants watched a validated distressing video, then were randomized to group Psychological First Aid (n=47) or natural recovery (n=37), with 74 followed up two weeks later. The control group showed a greater reduction in stress immediately after the intervention (medium effect size), and by two weeks the groups were indistinguishable overall — except that participants who had been most distressed at the outset reported significantly more trauma symptoms at follow-up if they had received PFA.

**Why it matters:** PFA has been rolled out globally as the safe, evidence-neutral replacement for critical incident stress debriefing; this trial suggests it may share debriefing's central flaw of interfering with natural recovery in exactly the people it targets.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "The trauma intervention we replaced debriefing with may have the same problem." Open with the history — debriefing was standard practice for two decades before trials showed it could entrench symptoms — and then show PFA repeating the pattern. Grant Devilly at Griffith University is the obvious call; he has been arguing this for years and will be quotable. Be explicit that this is a lab stress-video analogue, not a disaster zone.
- **Wider angle:** A workplace and institutional story for a general-interest outlet: employers, schools and emergency services routinely deploy group debrief-style sessions after critical incidents. What does "stepped, individualized care" actually look like in an HR policy, and who is liable if the mandatory group session makes things worse?

**Caveats:** Small N (84 randomized, 74 completed); analogue distress-video paradigm rather than real trauma; self-reported outcomes only; two-week follow-up; single center; the harmful subgroup finding is an interaction in a small sample and needs replication.

### 3. Nearly one in ten UK therapy clients says they recovered a memory of abuse they had not previously known about

**Journal:** *Memory* | **Published:** 28 August 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42663614 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09658211.2026.2714175
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 8/10 — a nationally representative estimate of a practice most people assume died out in the 1990s, from clients rather than therapists.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Lawrence Patihis surveyed a representative UK sample of 1,507 adults, 40% of whom had received therapy. Among the full sample, 4.9% said a therapist had discussed repressed memories of abuse with them (half the time the therapist raised it first), and 3.6% reported recovering memories during therapy of abuse they had not previously known about — 8.9% of everyone who had had therapy. Rates were highest for primal therapy, internal family systems and psychodynamic therapy, and were not confined to the 1980s: for therapy started between 2006 and 2024 the rate was 8–12%.

**Why it matters:** The recovered-memory debate is usually discussed in the past tense, and previous prevalence estimates came from therapists, who have obvious reasons to under-report. Asking clients directly suggests the practice is ongoing and affects millions of people in the UK.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Repressed memory therapy never went away." Lead with the number — 8.9% of British therapy-goers — and the fact that rates are as high for therapy started in the last two decades as during the memory wars. Call Patihis (University of Portsmouth), and get a response from the UKCP or BACP on what current training says about memory construction. Handle carefully: the story is about how memory can be constructed under suggestion, not a claim that any individual's memories are false.
- **Wider angle:** A legal and forensic feature for a specialist or long-read outlet on how courts in the UK now treat testimony based on memories recovered in therapy, and how the science of reconsolidation and imagination inflation has and hasn't reached the justice system.

**Caveats:** Self-reported and retrospective — clients' accounts of what a therapist said may be imprecise; no verification of memory accuracy in either direction; therapy modality labels are client-reported; cross-sectional survey.

### 4. People can track an object that has no visible features at all — a tiger defined only by the grass moving around it

**Journal:** *Cognition* | **Published:** November 2026 (online 31 Jul 2026)
**PMID:** 42537447 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cognition.2026.106657
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — vivid, demonstrable, and it undercuts a core assumption of object-tracking models; it is basic vision science rather than health news.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Across four experiments, observers watched grids of hundreds of small elements (for instance, randomly oriented crosses) in which an invisible "object" was defined purely by a propagating wave of change — each element switching to a new random orientation, brightness or shape as the object passed. Participants not only detected this second-order motion, they perceived it as a persisting object and could perform multiple-object tracking on several such objects at once, even when the type of change was haphazardly interleaved from element to element.

**Why it matters:** Standard models assume tracking works by continuously re-identifying an object's surface features across time; here there are no enduring features to re-identify, implying the visual system can bind "change itself" into an object.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "You can watch a tiger you cannot see." Lead with the demo — New Scientist could embed or describe the display — and the evolutionary framing the authors use: the most important objects are camouflaged ones. Brian Scholl's lab at Yale is the call, and Scholl is an unusually good explainer of phenomenological demonstrations.
- **Wider angle:** A technology feature for a computing or defense-adjacent outlet: machine vision systems track by feature re-identification and are systematically fooled by exactly this class of stimulus. What would a change-based tracker look like, and does biological vision have a trick worth stealing for camouflage detection?

**Caveats:** Sample sizes per experiment not reported in the abstract; laboratory display stimuli whose relation to real-world camouflage is an interpretation, not a test; no clinical or applied outcome.

### 5. Working memory is not the main thing helping you understand speech in a noisy room — reasoning ability is

**Journal:** *Cognition* | **Published:** 27 August 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42659749 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cognition.2026.106692
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — directly challenges a dominant assumption in hearing research, with a clean multivariate design; N is modest and the study is online.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)

**The study:** Eighty-nine healthy adults listened to sentences degraded in five different ways and reported the words they heard, and also completed Reading Span and N-back tests of working memory plus a short form of Raven's Advanced Progressive Matrices for fluid reasoning. All cognitive measures correlated with intelligibility on their own — but in multivariate and dominance analyses, fluid reasoning was clearly the strongest predictor, with working memory contributing little unique variance.

**Why it matters:** Decades of "listening effort" research and hearing-aid outcome prediction have leaned on working memory span, particularly the Reading Span test, as the cognitive resource that lets people decode degraded speech. This suggests the relevant ability is more general problem solving.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Why some people cope in a noisy bar — it isn't memory." Open on the cocktail-party problem everyone recognizes, then explain that the standard cognitive explanation may have been an artifact of testing one construct at a time. Call Joseph Rovetti and Ingrid Johnsrude at Western University; Johnsrude is a central figure in the working-memory-and-hearing literature, which makes the result more interesting, not less.
- **Wider angle:** A specialist audiology/health-technology piece on what clinics actually measure when they predict who will benefit from hearing aids or cochlear implants, and whether a widely used cognitive screening battery is measuring the wrong thing.

**Caveats:** Small N (89) for an individual-differences study; online testing with uncontrolled listening conditions; healthy adults, not people with hearing loss; cross-sectional and correlational; short-form Raven's.

### 6. The slowness of depression may be a timing prediction problem, not a movement problem

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychiatric Research* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 30 Jul 2026)
**PMID:** 42542090 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2026.07.024
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a specific, testable reframing of psychomotor retardation with a striking frequency-specific signature; modest clinical sample.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)

**The study:** Thirty-eight people with major depressive disorder and 65 healthy controls tapped in time with rhythmic tone sequences at a range of tempos. Basic motor function — tapping speed, stability, interval production — was intact in the depressed group. What was disrupted was synchronization specifically below 1 Hz, the range corresponding to the motor system's natural delta rhythm, and the size of that desynchronization tracked depression severity, psychomotor anhedonia and vegetative symptoms.

**Why it matters:** Psychomotor retardation is one of the oldest observed features of depression and has been treated as a motor output deficit; this locates it instead in the predictive timing signals the motor system sends to sensory areas, which is a cognitive mechanism with different treatment implications.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Depression may slow you down by breaking your internal metronome." Open on the clinical observation — the slowed speech and movement clinicians have described for a century — then show the frequency-specific finding: only the slow rhythms break. Georg Northoff (University of Ottawa) is an excellent, quotable source on temporal structure in psychiatric disorders; Nai Ding at Zhejiang University for the auditory-motor rhythm science.
- **Wider angle:** A music-and-brain feature for a general-interest outlet on rhythm as a clinical tool: why rhythmic auditory cueing helps in Parkinson's, whether slow-tempo synchronization could ever serve as an objective marker of mood state, and what a "biomarker you can tap out with your finger" would mean for a field short of objective measures.

**Caveats:** Small N (38 patients); cross-sectional and correlational — cannot establish whether timing deficits cause or follow depressive symptoms; medication status not described in the abstract; unequal group sizes; single site.

### 7. Insomnia, not oxygen starvation, explains the link between disturbed sleep and depression

**Journal:** *Journal of Sleep Research* | **Published:** 28 August 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42663413 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jsr.70431
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — two harmonized population cohorts with objective sleep measurement, and a clean null for the mechanism most people assume; cross-sectional.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Researchers harmonized two Australian and Swedish community cohorts (n=1,238; 68% male, mean age 57) with objective sleep recordings. Neither apnea-hypopnea index nor oxygen desaturation index was associated with depressive symptoms. Reduced sleep efficiency and increased wake after sleep onset were — but those associations disappeared once insomnia symptoms were added to the model.

**Why it matters:** Sleep apnea is routinely described as a cause of depression via intermittent hypoxia; in samples free of clinical referral bias, the depression signal seems to come from the subjective experience of broken sleep rather than from oxygen dips.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Your CPAP machine may not be the thing standing between you and a better mood." Lead with the null on hypoxia, which is the mechanism most sleep-clinic patients are told about. Jenny Theorell-Haglöw at Uppsala and Nathaniel Marshall at the Woolcock Institute are the calls. Be careful to frame this as observational and cross-sectional — no advice on treatment decisions.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a health outlet on the widening gulf between "objective" and "felt" sleep: people whose polysomnography looks fine but who feel wrecked, the growing recognition of insomnia as a distinct disorder rather than a symptom, and why the two are treated by different specialties.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design — cannot establish causation or direction; depressive and insomnia symptoms both self-reported, raising shared-method concerns; two cohorts differ by sex composition (one all-women, one all-men) and were harmonized post hoc; middle-aged and older community samples may not generalize to younger people or clinical apnea populations.

### 8. Reminding older people of the "memory decline" stereotype doesn't wreck their recall — it makes them study for longer

**Journal:** *Memory* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 5 Aug 2026)
**PMID:** 42554651 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09658211.2026.2711903
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a nuanced, partially null result in a literature under replication pressure; small sample keeps the score down.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)

**The study:** Ninety-two older adults were randomized to a stereotype threat condition or a control condition and completed a self-paced word-pair learning task with judgments of learning. Threat had no significant effect on how well people predicted their own learning, on the accuracy of those predictions, or on how they distributed study time across easy and hard items. What it did change was total study time: threatened participants spent longer studying and had to mobilize extra effort to reach the same performance as controls.

**Why it matters:** Stereotype threat in aging is usually framed as a performance-destroying phenomenon; here the cost shows up as reduced processing efficiency — the same result achieved for more effort — which is a subtler and arguably more realistic account of what negative age stereotypes do.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Ageism doesn't make you forget — it makes you work harder for the same result." Open on the moment of being handed a memory test after being told memory declines with age. Lucie Guerrero and Badiâa Bouazzaoui are the calls, and they can speak to why the metamemory effects everyone expected didn't appear. Situate it in the broader replication debate around stereotype threat.
- **Wider angle:** A workplace or economics angle for a general-interest outlet: if age stereotypes tax cognitive resources rather than degrading output, the cost is invisible in performance metrics and shows up instead as fatigue — with implications for how older workers are assessed and how "cognitive load" is measured at work.

**Caveats:** Small N (92); laboratory word-pair task with uncertain relevance to everyday memory; single center; several key results are null, so the positive study-time finding needs replication.

### 9. Most children in mental health services report feeling watched or followed — and no one is asking about it

**Journal:** *Psychopathology* | **Published:** 27 August 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42658756 | **DOI:** 10.1159/psp/aelag004
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — first data on "felt presence" in young people, with a striking prevalence figure; exploratory, and the authors say their own measure was inadequate.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Within the ChUSE feasibility RCT in NHS Child and Adolescent Mental Health Services, 50 young people aged 8–16 reported on distressing sensory experiences at baseline. Feeling watched or followed was reported by 88%, and 28% said it was the experience that distressed them most. Among those who went on to receive therapy in the trial, 63.8% described regularly experiencing a felt presence and being distressed by it.

**Why it matters:** Felt presence — the sense that someone unseen is nearby — is a well-documented phenomenon in adults with psychosis, Parkinson's and sleep paralysis, but has never been studied in children in clinical care; if these rates hold up, routine assessment is missing something common and highly distressing.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "The invisible person in the room: the childhood experience clinicians never ask about." Open on a young person describing a presence at the end of the bed, then the number: 88%. Ben Alderson-Day at Durham has written a book on felt presence and is a strong interviewee; Sarah Parry can speak to the CAMHS side. The honest framing is that this opens a line of enquiry, not that it changes practice yet.
- **Wider angle:** A cultural-science feature for a magazine or podcast on felt presence across contexts — sleep paralysis, mountaineers' "third man", bereavement, Parkinson's — and what a single phenomenon appearing under so many conditions tells us about how the brain models other minds and bodies in space.

**Caveats:** Small N (50); no control group of non-clinical young people, so the 88% figure cannot be interpreted as elevated; the authors explicitly flag that their assessment of felt presence was not a validated measure; sample not diverse; single trial embedded in a feasibility study.

### 10. Sitting through a criminal trial did not make bereaved relatives' grief worse — or better

**Journal:** *European Journal of Psychotraumatology* | **Published:** December 2026 (online 24 Aug 2026)
**PMID:** 42635331 | **DOI:** 10.1080/20008066.2026.2713263
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a well-powered longitudinal null in an extraordinary natural experiment; null results are harder to sell but this one has a strong hook.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers followed 237 people bereaved by the shooting down of flight MH17 across the criminal trial of the perpetrators, using longitudinal confirmatory factor analysis. Prolonged grief and mental well-being emerged as distinct, negatively related constructs whose relationship stayed stable over time — and neither predicted the other over time. Crucially, no aspect of criminal justice system involvement was associated with grief or well-being levels after the trial, once pre-trial levels were accounted for.

**Why it matters:** Both the "therapeutic jurisprudence" literature and the popular idea of "closure" assume that participation in a trial materially changes bereaved people's mental health. In this cohort it did neither, and grief and well-being turn out to be two separate dials rather than opposite ends of one.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Closure is a myth — a landmark trial changed nothing about relatives' grief." Open on the MH17 courtroom in the Netherlands, then the data. Lonneke Lenferink at Twente/Groningen is the call. The second finding is the more useful one for readers: reducing grief and building well-being appear to be separate therapeutic tasks.
- **Wider angle:** A justice-system feature for a legal or general-interest outlet on victim impact statements and the promises courts make to bereaved families — what victims actually say they want from a trial, and whether the mental health rationale for expanded victim participation is supported by evidence.

**Caveats:** Observational; a single, highly unusual mass-casualty event with unusually strong state support for the bereaved, which limits generalization; the perpetrators were tried in absentia; self-reported measures; null findings on CJS involvement need replication before informing policy.

### 11. How much you'd be blamed for *not* doing something predicts your behavior better than how much you'd be praised for doing it

**Journal:** *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* | **Published:** 26 August 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42655904 | **DOI:** 10.1177/01461672261472937
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a simple conceptual reframe that predicts real behavior including actual voting, across six studies; the moral-psychology framing needs work for a general audience.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Across six studies, the authors argue that moral acts carry two separable evaluations — praise for doing them (virtue) and blame for not doing them (duty) — and that these are not systematically related to each other. Measuring both improved predictions of intended blood donation and of actual voting behavior, and helped explain two puzzles: why Republicans masked less than Democrats during COVID-19, and why paying people for blood reduces donation.

**Why it matters:** Public health and civic campaigns routinely choose between praising good behavior and shaming its absence; this suggests those are independent levers, and that the wrong one can be pulled — paying for blood, for example, appears to strip an act of its duty status.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Why paying people to give blood makes them give less." Lead with the blood donation paradox, which readers find genuinely strange, then unpack the virtue/duty distinction and the mask-wearing result. Julian Zlatev at Harvard Business School and Dale Miller at Stanford are the calls; ask whether the framework predicts anything about recycling, tax compliance or vaccination.
- **Wider angle:** A behavioral-policy feature for a business or politics outlet on why "nudge" campaigns keep misfiring on politically coded behaviors, and what happens when a government tries to convert a virtue into a duty (or vice versa) — mask mandates, mandatory voting, organ donation opt-out.

**Caveats:** Multiple studies but sample sizes and populations not specified in the abstract; likely online panel samples with limited generalization; some outcomes are intentions rather than behavior; the COVID masking explanation is post hoc.

### 12. Brain waves recorded a week after a stroke predict who will still be recovering thinking skills six months later

**Journal:** *Clinical EEG and Neuroscience* | **Published:** 28 August 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42663449 | **DOI:** 10.1177/15500594261480203
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — prospective design and a good discrimination statistic, but a single-center cohort under 100 with a surprising direction of effect that needs external validation.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)

**The study:** Ninety-six patients with a first mild-to-moderate ischemic stroke had resting EEG recorded around eight days after the event, then MoCA cognitive testing at three and six months. Just over half (55.2%) showed meaningful cognitive recovery. Counterintuitively, *lower* frontal peak alpha frequency and *reduced* frontal slow-to-fast wave ratio predicted recovery, alongside elevated frontal frequency variability, together discriminating recoverers with an AUC of 0.831. Recoverers looked worse at three months but caught up by six.

**Why it matters:** Post-stroke cognitive impairment affects 30–60% of survivors and rehabilitation is often withdrawn early on the basis of a poor three-month assessment; if early EEG can flag late bloomers, some patients are being written off too soon.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "The stroke patients who look worst at three months are often the ones still improving." Lead with the clinical decision point — when do you stop rehab? — and the fact that the EEG markers predicted recovery in the opposite direction to what a "healthier brain recovers better" intuition suggests. Tran Cong Thang and Nguyen Van Tuan at Ho Chi Minh City are the calls; a Western stroke rehabilitation researcher for independent assessment.
- **Wider angle:** A health-technology piece for a specialist or general outlet on the quiet revival of EEG — cheap, portable, decades old — as a prognostic tool in an era of expensive imaging, and what it would take for a low-cost bedside test to change rehabilitation funding decisions.

**Caveats:** Small N (96) and single center; observational cohort — no test of whether acting on the prediction improves outcomes; recovery defined by a 2-point MoCA change, a fairly coarse threshold; predictors selected by stepwise regression in the same sample, risking overfitting; no external validation cohort; Vietnamese sample may not generalize.

### 13. Men and women's prolonged grief hangs together differently — identity loss is more central for men

**Journal:** *European Journal of Psychotraumatology* | **Published:** December 2026 (online 24 Aug 2026)
**PMID:** 42635333 | **DOI:** 10.1080/20008066.2026.2711483
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — large sample and a clear, humanly recognizable result, but network analysis findings are notoriously unstable and this is cross-sectional.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)

**The study:** A total of 1,093 bereaved adults in Hong Kong (274 men; mean age 37) completed the PG-13-Revised, and the authors compared symptom networks by gender. Women reported more severe symptoms overall, but the overall connectivity of the networks was similar. - **Pitch angle:** "Men's grief has a different shape." Open on a widower describing not knowing who he is anymore, then the network finding. ### 14. The brain's waste-clearance system looks more impaired in people on two antipsychotics than one

**Journal:** *Psychiatry Research* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 29 Jul 2026)
**PMID:** 42551238 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117348
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a novel and clinically pointed observation about a very common prescribing pattern, but small, cross-sectional, and using a proxy measure of glymphatic function.

**Story angles:**

### 15. Antipsychotic polypharmacy is rising again, 21 years of prescribing records show

**Journal:** *Psychiatry Research* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 29 Jul 2026)
**PMID:** 42551239 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117346
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — territory-wide data over two decades is unusually solid, but it is a descriptive trend paper without an individual-level hook.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Using electronic health records for every adult diagnosed with schizophrenia in Hong Kong from 2004 to 2024, researchers tracked prescribing. **Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Best used as the data spine of the combination-therapy story with entry 14: guidelines say one drug, and clozapine when one fails; real-world practice is drifting the other way. Esther Chan at the University of Hong Kong is the call, plus a UK or Australian prescribing researcher to say whether the pattern holds outside Hong Kong.
- **Wider angle:** A health-systems piece for a policy or medical outlet on why clozapine — the only drug with strong evidence in treatment-resistant schizophrenia — remains stubbornly underused worldwide, and what its monitoring requirements cost patients in outcomes.

**Caveats:** Descriptive prescribing data with no outcome measures — says nothing about whether polypharmacy helped or harmed; single jurisdiction; an erratum has already been published for this paper; several authors report consulting and research funding from industry (declared as unrelated), including funding from Amgen and GSK to one author.

### 16. Insecure attachment predicts avoiding conversations about sexual consent

**Journal:** *Archives of Sexual Behavior* | **Published:** 27 August 2026 (online ahead of print)

**The study:** The authors propose the Integrative Cognitive-Affective Model of Sexual Consent Communication, in which attachment insecurity undermines consent communication through two routes: reduced mentalizing (reading the other person's mind) and poor emotion regulation, which makes people perceive consent conversations as costly. ### 17. Autistic adults say talking to non-autistic people costs more effort — and noise makes them lose words

**Journal:** *Autism* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 3 Aug 2026)
**PMID:** 42547992 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13623613261469909
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a decent-sized survey putting numbers and first-person description behind the double empathy problem; self-report and retrospective.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Using the Neurotype Communication and Assistive Technology Survey, researchers collected quantitative ratings and written descriptions from 154 autistic adults aged 18–35 about past conversations. **Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Autistic adults find it easier to talk to each other — and here's what the room has to do with it." Lead with the specific, vivid detail of losing words in a noisy café. Zachary Miller (Michigan State) and Carla Mazefsky (Pittsburgh) are the calls. The practical angle — quiet rooms, predictable settings — is concrete without overclaiming.
- **Wider angle:** A design and architecture feature for a general-interest outlet on sensory design in offices, hospitals and schools: open-plan environments were built on a theory of collaboration that appears to disable a substantial minority of workers.

**Caveats:** Self-reported and retrospective reflections rather than observed conversations; convenience sample recruited online, likely skewed toward diagnosed, verbally fluent adults aged 18–35; no non-autistic comparison group; cross-sectional.

### 18. Mental fatigue and physical fatigue push you off the sofa in completely different ways

**Journal:** *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* | **Published:** November 2026 (online 2 Aug 2026)
**PMID:** 42543096 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychsport.2026.103240
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a three-month longitudinal design with a clean dissociation, though half the paper is a scale validation and outcomes are self-reported.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** In a two-part study, researchers validated an Italian version of the Physical Effort Scale (675 participants), then followed 424 adults (mean age 25) over three months. Physical fatigue predicted lower physical activity indirectly, by reducing the tendency to approach physical effort and increasing the tendency to avoid it — an effect strongest for vigorous activity. Mental fatigue had no relationship with those approach/avoidance tendencies at all, but directly predicted less light and moderate activity.

**Why it matters:** Public health messaging treats "too tired to exercise" as one thing; these data suggest tired body and tired brain suppress activity through separate routes, meaning the same motivational intervention would not work for both.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Two kinds of tired, two reasons you skipped the gym." Open on the after-work decision point, then the dissociation: physical fatigue changes how you feel about effort, mental fatigue just quietly removes your walking and light activity. Boris Cheval (ENS Rennes) is the standout call — he works on the theory that humans are wired to avoid unnecessary effort.
- **Wider angle:** A workplace-health feature for a business or general outlet on the cognitive cost of desk work: if mental fatigue selectively erodes light and moderate activity — walking, stairs, chores — then knowledge work may be depleting exactly the movement that matters most for long-term health.

**Caveats:** Self-reported physical activity throughout; young sample (mean age 25) recruited in Italy, limiting generalization; observational and mediational — the causal ordering is assumed, not tested experimentally; Study 1 is a scale validation with limited news value on its own.

### 19. Precision psychiatry audit: only a handful of tools are ready for the clinic

**Journal:** *International Review of Psychiatry* | **Published:** 27 August 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42657684 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09540261.2026.2722686
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a narrative review rather than new data, but the readiness triage is a useful spine for a skeptical trend piece.

**The study:** The authors applied a structured framework — validity, external replication, incremental utility, actionability, patient benefit, feasibility, equity, governance — to the major approaches under the banner of precision psychiatry. Only structured longitudinal measurement-based care, therapeutic drug monitoring for selected drugs, and a few pharmacogenetic gene-drug pairs qualified as clinically actionable. **Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** A reported piece rather than a study write-up: use the readiness ladder as the structure and go to the companies selling the experimental tier — particularly pharmacogenetic panels and automated suicide risk prediction — for their evidence. ### 20. Working memory chunks the world differently depending on what marks the boundaries

**Journal:** *Memory* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 4 Aug 2026)
**PMID:** 42552609 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09658211.2026.2712599
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — solid four-experiment work on a mechanism everyone has heard of (chunking), but the representational-geometry framing is hard to make vivid.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Across four behavioral experiments, participants encoded nine-item sequences and later reported an item's original position. Sequences varied in how strongly chunk boundaries were signaled: structured linguistic sequences produced a near-orthogonal representation, with clearly separable "which chunk" and "where in the chunk" codes. Color-segmented shapes gave a weaker, partially collapsed version. Scrambled sequences and uniformly colored shapes collapsed entirely to a single dimension.

**Why it matters:** Chunking is textbook — the reason phone numbers are grouped — but this shows it does not produce one uniform format; whether you get a genuinely two-level positional code depends on the strength of the boundary cue.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Why grouping a phone number only helps if the gaps are obvious enough." Lead with the everyday memory trick, then the finding that weak boundary cues give you the appearance of chunking without the underlying structure. Lin Zhan at Beijing Language and Culture University is the call. This works best as a short front-of-book piece.
- **Wider angle:** An interface-design story for a technology outlet on how information is grouped in passwords, one-time codes, addresses and form fields — and whether current conventions supply strong enough boundary cues to actually help memory.

**Caveats:** Sample sizes not given in the abstract; laboratory sequence-memory task; the group-level "intermediate" result partly reflects individual heterogeneity, which the authors acknowledge; language-specific stimuli may limit generalization.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Popular psychological interventions keep failing when properly tested.** The new batch's "Relaxation exercises may make some talk therapies work less well" (PMID 42546730) and "Psychological first aid after trauma performs worse than doing nothing" (PMID 42561812) extend a pattern already strong in the previous digest: mindfulness did not buffer discrimination in Black mothers (PMID 42622844), mindfulness failed to protect tweens against problematic social media use (PMID 42612528), symptom reframing produced no effect in somatic symptom disorder (PMID 42629541), and intensive self-monitoring changed nothing in alcohol recovery (PMID 42606857). Two runs in a row, the null and negative results are landing on exactly the interventions most heavily promoted to the public.
- **Real-world antipsychotic prescribing keeps drifting away from guidelines.** "Antipsychotic polypharmacy is rising again" (PMID 42551239) and the glymphatic imaging study of dual antipsychotics (PMID 42551238) sit directly alongside the previous digest's finding that one in five clozapine prescriptions worldwide is off-label (PMID 42628494). Both runs describe whole-system prescribing data showing guidelines and practice diverging, with clozapine underused in one and misapplied in the other.
- **Cheap EEG as a prognostic tool, with counterintuitive directions of effect.** "Brain waves recorded a week after a stroke predict who will still be recovering" (PMID 42663449) mirrors the previous run's gamma-band signature in early-onset Alzheimer's (PMID 42617666): both are small, single-center cohorts, both report an inverted expectation (worse-looking signal predicts better outcome; more gamma, not less), and both land near an AUC of 0.83. The recurring caution is the same — no external validation.

**Advances**

- **From whole packages to active ingredients.** The previous digest's mediation analysis of video-feedback parenting (PMID 42613957) asked which mechanism carried an intervention's effect; the new component network meta-analysis of 10,612 patients (PMID 42546730) does this at scale and across 30 trials, and finds some ingredients pulling in the wrong direction. This is the same research program a generation on in ambition.
- **Autism coverage moves from counting to lived experience.** The previous run's England-wide identification data (PMID 42613816) documented a doubling in prevalence and a narrowing sex gap; the new survey of 154 autistic adults on communication effort and noise (PMID 42547992) speaks to what that larger, more diverse identified population actually needs from rooms, workplaces and conversations.

**Counterpoints**

- **Algorithmic suicide risk prediction.** The previous digest's narrative review proposed a connectome-based suicide risk calculator (PMID 42612815) and reported clinicians warming to the probability-based OxSATS score (PMID 42618280). The new precision psychiatry readiness audit (PMID 42657684) classifies automated suicide prediction, neuroimaging diagnostics and polygenic scores as experimental, and flags an unresolved accountability gap — a direct brake on the optimism of both earlier items.
- **Oxygen as a lever on the brain.** The previous run tested low-oxygen air as a circadian intervention and found a non-significant shift (PMID 42630089); the new cohort analysis finds oxygen desaturation unrelated to depressive symptoms once insomnia is accounted for (PMID 42663413). Same journal, opposite ends of the same assumption that hypoxia is a meaningful lever on mood and rhythm.

**New themes**

- **Grief as a structured, measurable thing rather than a mood.** Two European Journal of Psychotraumatology papers — the MH17 trial cohort showing no effect of criminal justice involvement (PMID 42635331) and the gendered symptom network in Hong Kong (PMID 42635333) — open a topic absent from the previous digest.
- **Iatrogenic risk inside therapy itself.** "Nearly one in ten UK therapy clients says they recovered a memory of abuse" (PMID 42663614) has no analogue in the previous run and shifts the frame from "does therapy work" to "what does therapy do to people."
- **Basic cognition returns.** Tracking a featureless object (PMID 42537447), reasoning rather than working memory predicting speech-in-noise (PMID 42659749), and chunk-boundary geometry in working memory (PMID 42552609) restore a strand of pure cognitive science that the previous, health-heavy batch lacked.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch converge on the idea that psychological interventions have active ingredients, inert ingredients and adverse effects — and that hardly anyone measures the last two. A component analysis of more than 10,000 patients finds relaxation training and cognitive reframing associated with worse outcomes while social support does the heavy lifting (PMID 42546730); a randomized test of psychological first aid finds it slowed recovery, with the most distressed participants worse off two weeks later (PMID 42561812); nearly one in ten UK therapy clients reports recovering a previously unknown memory of abuse in the consulting room (PMID 42663614); the "closure" that courts promise bereaved families did nothing measurable across the MH17 trial (PMID 42635331); and a structured readiness audit finds only a handful of precision psychiatry tools clinically actionable (PMID 42657684).

**Why pitch this now:** Task-shared therapy, app-delivered relaxation modules and mandatory post-incident debriefs are being scaled globally on the assumption that psychological help is at worst neutral — and this batch is the first time the evidence against that assumption has arrived from several independent directions at once.

**Angle:** A reported feature on psychology's missing side-effect register: what it would take to treat therapies like drug formularies, with named components, doses and adverse event reporting, written for readers who have sat through a workplace debrief, downloaded a breathing app or been handed a therapy referral and assumed the worst case was that it wouldn't help.

**Potential headlines:**
- Some parts of talk therapy make patients worse, and trials are starting to say which
- The most soothing bits of therapy are the ones failing their tests
- Psychology has never kept a side-effect register, and patients are paying for it

**Potential outlets:**
- **New Scientist Features** — the Mind section already runs component-level and "does therapy work" pieces, and the Lancet Psychiatry dataset gives the feature a quantitative spine rather than a therapy-culture essay.
- **The Atlantic** — has an established readership for critical writing about therapy's cultural authority; the recovered-memory prevalence figure and the debrief history give it narrative stakes.
- **STAT** — the prescribing, readiness-audit and task-shared delivery threads are squarely clinical-policy territory, and STAT's audience will care about who owns adverse-event reporting for a psychosocial intervention.
- **Undark** — well suited to the accountability angle: interventions rolled out worldwide on weak evidence, and the institutions that never asked for harm data.
