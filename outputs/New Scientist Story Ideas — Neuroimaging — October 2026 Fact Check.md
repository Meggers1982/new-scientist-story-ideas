### Study 1: Most children who feel lonely at 10 are still lonely at 14
**PMID:** 42186177 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Persistence overstated — the hook misstates the outcome** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Most children who feel lonely at 10 are still lonely at 14" / "Of the 12% who reported loneliness at baseline, 71.6% reported it again over the next three years" / "seven in ten lonely 10-year-olds are still lonely three years later"
- **Abstract says:** "12% (n = 1,158) reported loneliness at baseline, and 71.6% (n = 829) of those re-experienced loneliness over 3 years." The primary outcome was "prospective loneliness reported from ages 10-14."
- **Problem:** "Re-experienced loneliness over 3 years" means loneliness was reported at some point during follow-up — not that those children were still lonely at the endpoint. The digest body sentence is accurate, but the headline and the pitch angle both convert an "at any point during follow-up" figure into a "still lonely at 14 / three years later" persistence claim. The entire pitch hook rests on the stronger version.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Seven in ten lonely 10-year-olds report loneliness again before 14". Pitch: "seven in ten lonely 10-year-olds reported being lonely again at least once over the next three years."

**Issue B. Unsupported head-to-head comparison of imaging vs environmental predictors** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the slightly deflating result that the brain scans added less than the household income question did"
- **Abstract says:** Environmental and health variables were tested with linear mixed-effects models (|d| = 0.069–0.388 and 0.061–0.390); neuroimaging features were entered into a linear discriminant analysis to distinguish children with and without prospective loneliness. No comparative effect-size statistic is given for imaging.
- **Problem:** The two sets of analyses used different methods and are not reported on a common metric, so the abstract does not support a claim that imaging contributed less than family income. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "the slightly deflating result that the strongest associations were environmental — parental psychopathology, developmental history and family income — while the imaging analysis was a separate classification exercise with no comparable effect size reported."

**Issue C. "Largest prospective test" claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this is the largest prospective test of that assumption"
- **Abstract says:** Describes a "population-based longitudinal cohort study" of 9,602 ABCD children; makes no priority or size claim.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "this is among the largest prospective tests of that assumption" — or drop the superlative pending verification.

**NS fit:** 7/10 is defensible on cohort size and prospective design, but it is partly earned by the overstated persistence claim. With the corrected framing I would score **6/10**.

### Study 2: Brain scans during a failed night's sleep show what insomnia actually looks like
**PMID:** 42458639 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Direct physiological evidence" for hyperarousal overstates the conclusion** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It is direct physiological evidence for the long-argued 'hyperarousal' account of insomnia"
- **Abstract says:** "insomnia was associated with altered nighttime relative cerebral perfusion, including increased activity in self-referential, sensory and affective regions, alongside reduced or dysregulated perfusion in frontal cortex and basal ganglia, suggesting altered arousal regulation, sleep drive and inhibitory control."
- **Problem:** The abstract never uses "hyperarousal" and reports a mixed picture (higher posteriorly, lower frontally) in *relative* perfusion maps from 28 people. "Direct physiological evidence" implies a confirmatory result the design cannot deliver.
- **Suggested fix:** "It is consistent with the long-argued 'hyperarousal' account of insomnia — though with relative, not absolute, perfusion in 28 people, it is suggestive rather than confirmatory."

**Issue B. Sleep state determination described incompletely** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "with polysomnography confirming sleep state"
- **Abstract says:** "Sleep state was determined using self-report and polysomnography."
- **Problem:** Sleep state was classified using both self-report and PSG; the digest presents it as objectively confirmed. This matters because the wider angle pitches the study as a tool for separating paradoxical insomnia from objective short sleep.
- **Suggested fix:** "with sleep state determined from both self-report and polysomnography."

**NS fit:** 6/10 with the small-N limitation explicitly stated is defensible.

### Study 3: Lab experiments missed a brain network that decides where you look
**PMID:** 42772940 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Overturns prior research" novelty tag** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** Title: "The orbitofrontal-anterior temporal network **complements** the attention network for where to look in social situations"; "we could precisely replicate the role of the attention networks in a more natural viewing environment."
- **Problem:** The study explicitly replicates the dorsal and ventral attention networks and adds a complementary network. Nothing is overturned; prior work is described as incomplete. The digest's own body text ("reproduced the classic dorsal and ventral attention networks") contradicts the tag.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** Extends/complicates prior research."

**Issue B. "Decides" implies causal control from a similarity analysis** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a brain network that decides where you look"
- **Abstract says:** Activation patterns in orbitofrontal and anterior temporal cortex were found "supporting individual decisions where to look"; the method compared "similarity relationships between eye fixation patterns ... and brain activity patterns."
- **Problem:** A representational-similarity correspondence cannot show the network decides anything; the digest's own caveat says so.
- **Suggested fix:** "Lab experiments missed a brain network tied to where you choose to look."

### Study 4: Impulsive suicidal thoughts leave a different mark on the brain than planned ones
**PMID:** 42710718 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline implies the ideation caused the brain difference** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Impulsive suicidal thoughts **leave a different mark on the brain** than planned ones"
- **Abstract says:** "Hot suicidal ideation was associated with reduced thalamo-pallidal volume compared with no suicidal ideation"; cross-sectional structural MRI in 263 people with MDD.
- **Problem:** "Leave a mark" asserts a direction of effect (thoughts → brain change) that a single-timepoint volumetric association cannot support. The body text and caveats are correctly hedged, so only the headline needs fixing.
- **Suggested fix:** "Impulsive suicidal thoughts are linked to a different brain signature than planned ones."

**Issue B. Single-site description not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "single-site clinical sample of people with MDD"
- **Abstract says:** Reports 263 individuals with MDD; recruitment site(s) not stated (all authors are Poitiers-affiliated).
- **Problem:** Plausible but unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "clinical sample of people with MDD, apparently from a single French centre (confirm with authors)."

Otherwise accurate: the composite (bilateral thalamus, right pallidum), the MADRS-derived subtype, the adjustment set, and the null DLPFC result all match. The pitch's explicit "avoid any suggestion that a scan could identify risk in an individual" is good practice.

### Study 5: Catatonia may be the ancient freeze response jammed on
**PMID:** 42815707 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Systematically cross-referenced" contradicts the review type flagged in Caveats** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The authors **systematically** cross-referenced human neuroimaging of normal threat-induced freezing against imaging of catatonia across its many causes."
- **Abstract says:** "In this critical review, we cross-referenced available neuroimaging studies in human freezing and catatonia studies across etiologies."
- **Problem:** "Systematically" signals systematic-review methodology (prespecified search, screening, selection criteria) that the abstract does not claim; the digest's own caveat correctly calls it a "narrative/critical review."
- **Suggested fix:** "The authors cross-referenced human neuroimaging of normal threat-induced freezing against imaging of catatonia across its many causes in a critical, non-systematic review."

**Issue B. "New mechanism" novelty tag** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** New mechanism"
- **Abstract says:** "It has been suggested that catatonia may be the maladaptive counterpart of the freeze-to-tonic immobility response. While similar brain structures are implicated in both entities, no theory-driven synthesis of neuroimaging evidence has been conducted..."
- **Problem:** The freeze hypothesis pre-dates this paper; what is new is the imaging synthesis and the formalized FAMC model.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** New synthesis of an existing hypothesis."

Note the digest drops "structurally mediated" from the "mechanistically distinct category" list — harmless as an abridgement, but worth restoring if the sentence survives into copy.

### Study 6: Feeling rejected makes you lonely through a specific psychological step — and it looks different in older brains
**PMID:** 42091836 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Causal headline for a cross-sectional mediation** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Feeling rejected **makes you lonely** through a specific psychological step"
- **Abstract says:** "withdrawal-related threat sensitivity partially mediated the association between perceived rejection and loneliness"; "These findings provide **preliminary evidence** that withdrawal-related threat sensitivity **may explain the association**..."
- **Problem:** All measures were collected at one timepoint; the digest's own caveat says mediation "cannot establish causal order," which the headline then asserts.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rejection and loneliness are linked by a specific psychological step — and the brain correlates differ by age."

**Issue B. "Not directly" misstates partial mediation** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the proposed mechanism: rejection breeds loneliness by ratcheting up a habit of withdrawal, **not directly**"
- **Abstract says:** "withdrawal-related threat sensitivity **partially** mediated the association between perceived rejection and loneliness in both age groups."
- **Problem:** Partial mediation means a direct rejection–loneliness association remains after accounting for threat sensitivity. The pitch tells a writer the opposite — that the route is wholly indirect.
- **Suggested fix:** "the proposed mechanism: part of the link between rejection and loneliness runs through heightened withdrawal-related threat sensitivity, rather than all of it being direct."

**Issue C. Age difference in the pathway overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "it looks different in older brains" / "the brain structure associated with the rejection-to-loneliness route was detectable mainly in the young"
- **Abstract says:** "the indirect behavioral association did not differ reliably by age group"; age-sensitive indirect effects were found "in the left precuneus and left planum temporale" only, with the other three regions (left angular gyrus, left subparietal sulcus, right superior temporal sulcus) not described as age-divergent; all imaging analyses were "exploratory."
- **Problem:** Two of five regions showed an age-restricted exploratory effect; the behavioral pathway itself did not differ by age. "Mainly in the young" overgeneralizes.
- **Suggested fix:** "the behavioural pathway was similar in both age groups, but in exploratory analyses two of the five brain regions — left precuneus and left planum temporale — showed the indirect effect only in younger adults."

### Study 7: People who fear punishment are better at waiting for a reward
**PMID:** 42800541 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Better at waiting" frames a preference measure as an ability** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "People who fear punishment are **better at** waiting for a reward"
- **Abstract says:** "Behaviorally, punishment sensitivity negatively correlated with delay discounting." Punishment sensitivity is defined as "an individual's tendency to perceive and respond to punishment and other negative outcomes."
- **Problem:** Delay discounting indexes how steeply a person devalues delayed rewards — a preference, measured correlationally — not a demonstrated capacity to wait. "Fear punishment" also narrows the trait definition to fear. Minor, but the headline is the generalization a reader will carry away.
- **Suggested fix:** "People more sensitive to punishment devalue future rewards less."

Everything else checks out: the dmPFC_L GMV association, the dmPFC_L–PCG_R connectivity, the partial mediation with suppressor effect, and the digest's correct note that N is absent from the abstract.

### Study 8: Your sense of numbers runs through the part of the brain that moves your body
**PMID:** 42764054 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline asserts routing from decoding evidence** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Your sense of numbers **runs through** the part of the brain that moves your body"
- **Abstract says:** "we revealed significant cross-task decoding between the two semantic modalities within the sensorimotor cortex"; "these findings **suggest** a deep functional coupling between numerical evaluation and motor preparation."
- **Problem:** Decodable information in sensorimotor cortex shows the signal is present there, not that number processing is routed through it; the digest's own caveat says the results "cannot show sensorimotor cortex is necessary."
- **Suggested fix:** "Your sense of numbers shows up in the part of the brain that moves your body."

The behaviour–brain dissociation (SNARC behaviourally only in the implicit parity task; decoding predominantly in the explicit magnitude task) is reported accurately, as is the absence of N from the abstract.

### Study 9: For Māori, the brain is sacred — and MRI practice has no rules for that
**PMID:** 42503436 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline says MRI; participants had EEG** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "For Māori, the brain is sacred — and **MRI** practice has no rules for that"
- **Abstract says:** "Semi-structured uiuinga (interviews) with three Māori men ... who had previously participated in an **electroencephalography** session"; "there are currently no national-level guidelines for conducting **neuroimaging** in Aotearoa."
- **Problem:** The participants' lived experience was of EEG, and the guideline gap described is for neuroimaging generally. Naming MRI in the headline attributes experience the participants did not report — and the pitch's own imagery ("gelling and electrode-capping the head") is EEG, not MRI.
- **Suggested fix:** "For Māori, the brain is sacred — and neuroimaging practice has no rules for that."

**Issue B. Dataset-representation inference goes beyond the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "assumptions that affect who consents to be scanned, and therefore who is represented in brain datasets"
- **Abstract says:** Addresses culturally safe care, relational engagement, shared decision-making and whānau confidence; says nothing about recruitment rates or dataset composition.
- **Problem:** A reasonable hypothesis, but presented as a finding of this study. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "assumptions that researchers in this area argue may shape who feels able to take part — a link this study does not itself measure."

**NS fit:** 5/10 for a three-participant qualitative study is appropriately cautious, and the score note already explains why.

### Study 10: In gaming disorder, the brain's reward circuit reacts oddly to rewards that have nothing to do with games
**PMID:** 42335494 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. Group sizes (21 IGD vs 23 recreational gaming users), the four cue types, the faster responses and stronger craving to gaming cues, the altered intrinsic-reward evaluation, the left thalamus → right caudate hyper-synchronization during intrinsic reward processing, and the DMN–reward-network coupling under gaming cues all match the abstract. The digest notably avoids the paper title's own causal phrasing ("Intrinsic reward sensitivity **drives** internet gaming disorder") and correctly labels the "self-efficacy-sensory drive" model as speculative. Framing is consistent with the abstract.

### Study 11: Depressed people who are sleepy by day lose pleasure in physical things
**PMID:** 42746369 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Matched" implies a design feature the abstract does not report** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The two groups were **matched** on depression and anxiety severity *and* on objective sleep parameters"
- **Abstract says:** "The two groups were comparable for demographic and clinical characteristics, with no differences in Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) or Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA) scores or polysomnographic parameters."
- **Problem:** Groups were observed to be comparable, not matched by design — a distinction that matters because the "identical sleep studies" pitch leans on it.
- **Suggested fix:** "The two groups did not differ on depression or anxiety severity, or on polysomnographic sleep parameters."

**Issue B. "Anticipatory" anhedonia vs the instrument used** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "higher specifically on physical anhedonia — not social or **anticipatory** anhedonia"
- **Abstract says:** "The DS group scored higher on the RPAS, while social and **temporal** anhedonia did not differ between groups" (TEPS = Temporal Experience of Pleasure Scale, which covers anticipatory *and* consummatory pleasure).
- **Problem:** "Anticipatory" names only half of what the TEPS measures.
- **Suggested fix:** "not social or temporal (anticipatory and consummatory) anhedonia."

**Issue C. Headline implies a change over time** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Depressed people who are sleepy by day **lose** pleasure in physical things"
- **Abstract says:** Cross-sectional design; "MDD patients with DS exhibit more severe physical anhedonia."
- **Problem:** "Lose" describes a trajectory; the study measured a between-group difference at one timepoint. The caveats correctly flag cross-sectionality.
- **Suggested fix:** "Depressed people who are sleepy by day report less pleasure in physical things."

**NS fit:** 4/10 is appropriately low for 22 vs 45 with a cross-sectional mediation.
