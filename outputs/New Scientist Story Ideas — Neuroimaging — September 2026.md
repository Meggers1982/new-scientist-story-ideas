### 1. People who donate a kidney to a stranger show a reversed brain map of social closeness

**Journal:** *Cognition* | **Published:** December 2026 (online 29 August 2026)
**PMID:** 42667790 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cognition.2026.106696
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — a rare, genuinely surprising fMRI result in a hard-to-recruit population, with a matched control group and a clean replication of the standard pattern built in.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers scanned "extraordinary altruists" — people who had donated a kidney or liver lobe to an anonymous stranger — alongside demographically matched controls, while participants thought about named individuals drawn from different layers of their own social networks. In controls, 75 percent of the brain volume sensitive to social distance responded most strongly to the innermost circle, replicating earlier work. In altruists the pattern flipped: only 30 percent favored the closest circle, and 70 percent responded more strongly to distant acquaintances, with the effect concentrated in the temporoparietal junction.

**Why it matters:** The brain's apparent bias toward close kin and friends has been treated as a near-universal feature of human social cognition; this suggests it is at least partly a dimension on which people differ, and that unusually expansive care has a measurable neural signature.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a donor lying in a scanner thinking about the stranger who received their kidney — and the finding that their brain treats that stranger roughly as most of us treat a sibling. The hook is the reversal of a pattern that has been reported as if it were fixed human nature. Call Shahar Arzy's Computational Neuropsychiatry Lab at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, and it would be worth pairing with Abigail Marsh's long-running work on altruistic donors for an outside view on whether the brain difference is cause, consequence or both.
- **Wider angle:** A longer feature on the science of moral circle expansion — how psychologists and effective-altruism researchers have tried to measure who counts as "one of us", and what happens when the boundary dissolves. Fits a general-interest magazine such as *The Atlantic* or *Aeon*, framed around the ethics of screening donors rather than the imaging.

**Caveats:** Sample size not reported in the abstract and is almost certainly small; cross-sectional design cannot say whether the neural pattern preceded the donation or followed it; fMRI group differences in percentage-of-voxels metrics are sensitive to thresholding choices.

### 2. A brain marker for loneliness shows up across depression, schizophrenia and Alzheimer's

**Journal:** *European Neuropsychopharmacology* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 26 April 2026)
**PMID:** 42044556 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.euroneuro.2026.112847
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a deliberate replication attempt that succeeded on one measure and failed on another, which is unusually informative; limited by modest per-group numbers.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Using resting-state and task fMRI in an overlapping cohort (N=167 and N=152; schizophrenia, major depression, Alzheimer's disease and healthy controls), plus mega-analyses combining these with an earlier independent sample (N=317 and N=291), the team tested whether social dysfunction tracks default mode network integrity. Reduced connectivity within the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex was associated with higher social dysfunction and loneliness scores across all groups, and the association extended to depression for the first time. The previously reported link between social dysfunction and default mode responses to emotional faces did not replicate.

**Why it matters:** It supports the idea that loneliness and social withdrawal have a shared neural correlate that cuts across diagnostic labels — and simultaneously retires a candidate biomarker that looked promising.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the double result: one brain signature of social withdrawal survives replication, another doesn't. The scenario is a clinic where "withdrawn" means the same thing whether the patient has schizophrenia at 25 or Alzheimer's at 75. Call Simone Braak or Martien Kas (University of Groningen), who led the PRISM consortium work. Avoid implying this is anywhere near a diagnostic test.
- **Wider angle:** A piece on the replication crisis inside psychiatric neuroimaging specifically — why the field is now designing studies whose primary job is to check earlier ones, and what fraction survive. Good for a specialist outlet such as *Nature Medicine*'s news pages or *Undark*.

**Caveats:** Observational; per-diagnosis subgroups are small (26–44); social dysfunction is self-reported; several authors have pharmaceutical industry ties (P1vital Ltd, Boehringer Ingelheim) and one industry-affiliated co-author is an employee of P1vital Products Ltd.

### 3. People with epilepsy in rural China are now dying of strokes and heart disease, not accidents

**Journal:** *Epilepsia* | **Published:** 7 September 2026
**PMID:** 42704311 | **DOI:** 10.1002/epi.70480
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — very large cohort and a clear reversal of the accepted picture, though the setting limits how far readers will see themselves in it.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** A nationwide cohort followed 17,515 people with epilepsy in rural China for 25,922 person-years (2018–2020), recording 381 deaths. All-cause mortality was 3.5 times the general population rate. Nearly half of deaths were from circulatory disease — cerebrovascular disease 28.9 percent, heart disease 18.9 percent — rather than the drownings, burns and injuries that dominated earlier studies in low-resource settings. The largest relative excess was for brain malignancies (SMR 20.3), pointing to undiagnosed structural lesions.

**Why it matters:** It shifts the prevention target for millions of people with epilepsy in low- and middle-income settings from seizure safety toward vascular risk and access to brain scans.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is that a historically "accidental" cause-of-death profile has quietly turned into a cardiovascular one as rural China's health profile changed — and that a twentyfold excess of brain tumor deaths implies scans that were never done. Open on the diagnostic pathway: a first seizure in a village clinic with no MRI within reach. Call Ley Liu or Dong Zhou at West China Hospital, or Josemir Sander at UCL's Queen Square Institute of Neurology for international context.
- **Wider angle:** A global health feature on the "imaging gap" — how many neurological diagnoses worldwide depend on a machine most of the planet cannot access, and what portable low-field MRI might change. Fits *The Lancet*'s news pages, *BMJ*, or a development-focused outlet.

**Caveats:** Observational; causes of death partly derived from registry and program records rather than autopsy; three-year follow-up; findings specific to rural China and may not generalize.

### 4. Sleep apnea leaves a fingerprint in the fine wiring of grey matter

**Journal:** *Journal of Sleep Research* | **Published:** 2 September 2026
**PMID:** 42687385 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jsr.70444
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a genuinely new imaging window onto a very common condition, held back by small groups and a purely correlational design.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers applied neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI) — a multi-shell diffusion MRI technique that estimates the density and directional spread of neuronal processes — to people with mild (n=24), moderate (n=26) and severe (n=23) obstructive sleep apnea and 24 healthy controls. Widespread changes appeared across grey matter, with orientation dispersion altered more extensively than neurite density, and both increases and decreases depending on region and severity.

**Why it matters:** Conventional MRI shows that sleep apnea shrinks brain tissue; this suggests the underlying cytoarchitecture is being reorganized rather than simply lost, which may matter for whether the changes are reversible with treatment.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Around a billion people have obstructive sleep apnea and most are undiagnosed — the hook is that a technique designed to count neurites can see something in their grey matter that standard scans miss, and that the changes go in both directions rather than simply downward. Open on someone whose partner noticed the snoring. Contact Yun-Zhi Lin at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University. Do not imply the brain changes cause the daytime cognitive problems — this study cannot show that.
- **Wider angle:** A technical explainer for a neuroscience-literate audience on what NODDI and related diffusion models actually measure and how confidently, given persistent disputes about whether "neurite density index" means what its name implies. Fits a specialist outlet such as *Quanta* or a scientific society magazine.

**Caveats:** Small N in every group (23–26); single-center; cross-sectional and observational; no treatment arm, so nothing can be said about whether CPAP reverses the pattern; multiple-comparison burden is high in whole-brain grey matter statistics.

### 5. Bilingual children's brains predict the next word in the language they don't learn at school

**Journal:** *Brain and Language* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 29 August 2026)
**PMID:** 42667920 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bandl.2026.105833
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a solid, decently sized first demonstration in an understudied group, but the headline finding is a feasibility result and the individual-differences analyses came out null.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Eighty-eight Spanish-English bilingual children aged 7–12 listened to a naturalistic story in Spanish, their heritage language, while fNIRS recorded brain activity. The team used a computational language model to estimate how surprising each word was, then tested whether brain responses tracked that surprisal. They did — indicating the children hold rich predictive representations of Spanish. Attempts to link the strength of that tracking to reading and listening comprehension scores in either language produced only trends the authors say are too weak to interpret.

**Why it matters:** Heritage languages are routinely treated as weak or incomplete; this shows children's brains are doing the same predictive work in Spanish that monolingual brains do in English, which is relevant to how bilingual children are assessed in schools.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the deficit framing being wrong — a child who seems hesitant speaking Spanish to a teacher is, in the scanner, predicting upcoming Spanish words as fluently as any listener. The methodological hook is that a large language model is being used as a measuring instrument for a child's brain. Call Ioulia Kovelman (now University of Texas at Dallas) or Jonathan Brennan (University of Michigan).
- **Wider angle:** A trend piece on language models as a tool rather than a subject — how surprisal estimates from GPT-style systems have quietly become the standard yardstick in neurolinguistics, and what it means that our theory of human prediction now depends on a commercial artifact. Fits a technology or ideas outlet.

**Caveats:** fNIRS measures only cortical surface hemodynamics, with limited spatial coverage; the individual-differences results were null and the authors say so; no monolingual comparison group; two authors receive royalties from Brookes on Spanish-English assessment instruments (BESA/BESAME).

### 6. Brain scans of "digital addiction" converge on the same three regions — and on serotonin

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychiatric Research* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 20 May 2026)
**PMID:** 42190596 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2026.05.027
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — imposes order on a famously messy literature and links it to chemistry and gene expression, but it is a meta-analysis of heterogeneous, mostly small studies.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** A voxel-based morphometry meta-analysis pooled structural MRI studies comparing people with problematic social media, gaming or smartphone use against controls. Consistent grey matter reductions emerged in the bilateral anterior cingulate cortex, left middle frontal gyrus and left medial orbitofrontal cortex. Cross-referencing those maps against normative neurotransmitter atlases implicated serotonergic and GABAergic systems rather than the dopamine story usually told about screens, and transcriptomic decoding identified 534 associated genes enriched for synaptic and ion-channel function.

**Why it matters:** If it holds, the neurochemistry of compulsive screen use looks less like the stimulant-reward caricature and more like a problem of inhibitory control — which points at different treatments.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The counterintuitive line is "it's probably not dopamine." Every popular account of phone addiction invokes dopamine hits; the converging imaging evidence points instead at serotonin and GABA systems in regions governing self-control. Open on the gap between the neuroscience and the bestseller shelf. Call Haibo Yang at Tianjin Normal University. Be explicit that grey matter differences in cross-sectional studies cannot say whether heavy use shrank these regions or smaller regions predisposed to heavy use.
- **Wider angle:** An investigation into whether "digital addiction" should be a diagnosis at all — the contested status of internet gaming disorder in DSM-5 and ICD-11, and what happens to a construct when neuroimaging arrives before the definition settles. Fits a psychiatry-focused or long-form outlet.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of small primary studies with inconsistent inclusion criteria; cross-sectional throughout, so direction of causation is unknown; "digital addiction" pools several behaviors that may not belong together; neurotransmitter and gene-expression maps come from separate reference cohorts, not the participants themselves.

### 7. In frontotemporal dementia, poor memory scores don't mean a damaged memory system

**Journal:** *Assessment* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 25 September 2025)
**PMID:** 40995692 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10731911251361038
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a clinically useful dissociation with a decent sample, but the framing is technical and the design is cross-sectional.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Three hundred participants — people with behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia, Alzheimer's disease, mild cognitive impairment, and controls — completed memory tests and had medial temporal lobe volumes measured. Memory scores correlated strongly with medial temporal volume in MCI, moderately in Alzheimer's and controls, and not at all in bvFTD. Propensity score matching showed that once frontal function was accounted for, many apparent memory differences between groups vanished.

**Why it matters:** People with bvFTD can fail memory tests while their memory architecture is relatively intact — the failure is in the frontal systems that organize retrieval, which has direct implications for how dementias are told apart in clinic.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is a familiar anxiety inverted: failing a memory test doesn't necessarily mean your memory is broken. Open on a patient in their fifties misdiagnosed with early Alzheimer's on the strength of a word-list score. Call Isabel Santana or Ricardo Félix Morais at the University of Coimbra. Useful for readers who have taken or administered these exact tests.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on the limits of standardized cognitive testing — how tests designed to isolate one faculty end up measuring several, and the push toward test batteries that report *why* someone failed rather than that they did. Fits a psychology or clinical-practice outlet.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational; groups drawn from specialist memory clinics and may not generalize; bvFTD subgroup size not stated separately in the abstract; propensity score matching reduces effective sample size and cannot fully remove confounding.

### 8. Your brain treats "will they cheat me?" completely differently from "could I lose?"

**Journal:** *Cortex* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 13 June 2026)
**PMID:** 42341477 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cortex.2026.06.004
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — an elegant dissociation, including a brain signal with no behavioral counterpart, but N=31 is thin for parametric fMRI.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Thirty-one participants played a parametric trust game in an fMRI scanner while three factors varied independently: how much they personally stood to lose, how much their partner stood to gain by betraying them, and whether any betrayal would be deliberate. Behaviorally, people responded almost entirely to their own risk and largely ignored the partner's incentive or agency. Neurally, all three were encoded separately — including partner agency in dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, despite having no effect on choices at all.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the brain tracks information about a partner's motives that people then fail to use, which is a specific account of why trust decisions are so self-centered.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the mismatch — your brain registers whether someone has a reason to betray you, and then you decide as if it hadn't. Open on a concrete case: handing a deposit to a contractor, or lending money to a colleague. Call Frank Krueger (George Mason University/University of Mannheim), who has built much of this literature. Frame it as a puzzle about information that reaches the brain but not the decision.
- **Wider angle:** A behavioral-economics piece on why trust research keeps finding that people use crude self-focused heuristics, and what that predicts for online marketplaces, escrow design and fraud. Fits a business or economics outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (31); single-center; lab-based economic game with hypothetical or modest stakes may not reflect real trust decisions; a null behavioral effect paired with a significant neural effect needs independent replication before much weight is put on it.

### 9. A model using genes, autism and EEG predicts which delayed children will have intellectual disability

**Journal:** *Journal of Intellectual Disability Research* | **Published:** 24 August 2026
**PMID:** 42634999 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jir.70164
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — very large cohort and honest reporting of moderate performance, but the referral-skewed sample and retrospective design cap it.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Researchers retrospectively analyzed 2,453 children diagnosed with global developmental delay at a Chinese tertiary center between 2014 and 2023, all followed to at least age five, testing 28 candidate predictors spanning perinatal, developmental, neuroimaging, EEG, genetic and comorbidity data across five machine learning algorithms. A calibrated logistic regression achieved an AUC of 0.783 — and, notably, beat nothing: all five algorithms, including gradient boosting and random forests, landed within a 0.015 AUC band of each other. The strongest predictors were pathogenic genetic variants, comorbid autism and epileptiform EEG discharges; a higher adaptive developmental quotient was protective.

**Why it matters:** For parents told their toddler is "delayed", the question is always what happens next; this is the first multivariable, calibrated attempt to answer it — and a reminder that fancy algorithms often add nothing over plain regression.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Two hooks in one: a genuinely useful prognostic tool for a conversation clinicians currently have to hedge, and the quiet embarrassment that four machine learning methods couldn't beat logistic regression. Open in the consulting room at the moment of the "we'll have to wait and see" answer. Contact the corresponding group at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. Stress that this is a triage tool for a high-risk referral population, not a community screening test — the authors say so themselves.
- **Wider angle:** A piece on the AI-in-medicine reality check: how often deep and ensemble models are outperformed or matched by simple regressions on tabular clinical data, and why the field keeps rediscovering this. Fits a health-tech or data-science outlet.

**Caveats:** Retrospective and single-center; 83 percent of the cohort progressed to intellectual disability, reflecting extreme referral bias, so the model would need refitting for community use; no external validation; negative predictive value is only 31.7 percent at the chosen threshold.

### 10. Learning a language in later life helps — but only a narrow slice of thinking

**Journal:** *Journal of Psycholinguistic Research* | **Published:** 2 September 2026
**PMID:** 42684539 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10936-026-10295-0
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — deflates a popular claim with a quantitative synthesis, but it is a review with a framework attached rather than new primary data.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** A review with an embedded quantitative synthesis of behavioral studies in which older adults took up a new language, alongside neuroimaging evidence on which systems late learning recruits. The pooled behavioral effect was small but reliable, and confined largely to higher-order executive processes — attentional regulation, executive coordination, working memory — with no broad enhancement of general cognitive function. Imaging shows engagement of frontal-parietal and memory networks, modulated by task demands and learning stage.

**Why it matters:** "Learn a language to protect your brain" is one of the most repeated pieces of cognitive-aging advice; the honest version is narrower and smaller than the slogan.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the deflation — yes, it does something, and here is exactly how little and where. Open on a retiree six months into Duolingo streaks wanting to know what they are actually buying. Call Yan Jing Wu at Jiangsu Normal University or the Centre for Cognitive and Brain Sciences at the University of Macau. Be careful not to overstate: this synthesizes existing, mostly small training studies.
- **Wider angle:** An examination of the "cognitive reserve" industry — language apps, brain-training games and puzzle books that borrow scientific credibility from effects this modest, and what the regulatory position on such claims actually is. Fits a consumer-affairs or business outlet.

**Caveats:** Review and secondary synthesis rather than new data; primary training studies are typically small, short and unblinded; publication bias is likely in this literature; no long-term dementia outcomes.

### 11. Schizophrenia's motivation symptoms track how rigidly the cortex is linked together

**Journal:** *Schizophrenia Research* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 23 May 2026)
**PMID:** 42176687 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.schres.2026.05.013
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a reasonable multi-site sample and a specific dissociation, but the effect is small and the concept is hard to translate for general readers.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Using cortical thickness data from 203 people with schizophrenia across three independent sites, normalized against ENIGMA Lifespan Working Group normative models, the team built structural covariance networks for regions associated with two clusters of negative symptoms: diminished motivation and pleasure (MAP), and diminished expressivity (EXP). Severity of motivation and pleasure deficits predicted how tightly embedded regions were within the MAP network; expressivity deficits showed no such relationship.

**Why it matters:** Negative symptoms respond poorly to existing drugs and have long been treated as one thing; this suggests their two components have different neural bases, with apathy tied to excessive structural coupling rather than to loss of tissue in any one region.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is that apathy in schizophrenia looks less like a damaged region and more like a network that has become too tightly wired — rigidity rather than deficit. Open on the symptom clinicians find hardest to treat and families find hardest to understand. Call Derin Cobia at Brigham Young University. Frame carefully: this is a correlational imaging association, not a treatment target yet.
- **Wider angle:** A piece on normative modeling — the shift in psychiatric imaging from "patients versus controls" to growth-chart-style deviation scores, and what it lets researchers ask that group averages never could. Fits a specialist neuroscience or psychiatry outlet.

**Caveats:** Observational; the significant effect is small (β=0.085, p=0.03) and would not survive stringent correction across many tests; data pooled from three sites with different scanners; medication effects on cortical thickness not addressed in the abstract; NIH, DOE and NSF funding declared.

### 12. The cerebellum turns up as an unexpected marker of problem gaming

**Journal:** *Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology & Biological Psychiatry* | **Published:** 11 September 2026
**PMID:** 42727812 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pnpbp.2026.111932
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — large sample and an unexpected region, but classification accuracy of 62.6 percent is barely above chance and undercuts the biomarker framing.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** A graph convolutional network was trained on whole-brain functional connectivity graphs from 784 young adults aged 17–32 (453 with internet gaming disorder, 331 controls), with explainability methods used to identify which regions drove the classification. The network beat a linear support vector machine (balanced accuracy 62.6 percent versus 57.7 percent), and both approaches converged on the cerebellum rather than the reward regions usually invoked. Nodal efficiency in cerebellar lobule X, vermal lobule IX and left thalamus correlated negatively with DSM-5 symptom severity — but no correlations with Internet Addiction Test scores survived correction.

**Why it matters:** It nudges attention toward the cerebellum's role in habit and compulsion, a region largely absent from the reward-circuit story of behavioral addiction. As a diagnostic tool it is nowhere near useful.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The cerebellum has spent a century labelled the movement coordinator; here it comes out as the most discriminative region in a large gaming-disorder classifier. Open on the historical dismissal of the "little brain" and the steady accumulation of evidence that it is doing cognitive work. Call Guang-Heng Dong at Yunnan Normal University. Be blunt about the 62 percent accuracy — this is a clue, not a test.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on explainable AI in brain imaging: the field is racing to build classifiers that can say *why*, but saliency maps have known instability problems. Ask whether "the algorithm says cerebellum" is evidence at all. Fits a machine-learning or research-methods outlet.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional; barely-above-chance discrimination; one of two symptom measures produced no surviving correlations, suggesting fragility; attribution methods like CAM and Grad-CAM are not validated as neuroscientific evidence; recruitment restricted to young Chinese adults.

### 13. Mitochondria may shape how fast time feels — but the evidence is thin

**Journal:** *Psychophysiology* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42669931 | **DOI:** 10.1111/psyp.70383
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — an irresistible question and a genuinely unusual patient group, but the authors themselves describe the evidence as weak and the headline group comparison was null.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Sixty-five healthy adults (n=59) and 36 people carrying rare mitochondrial DNA mutations completed time estimation and time production tasks while researchers measured immune-cell mitochondrial bioenergetics, blood and urine catecholamines, resting energy expenditure, working memory, mood and structural and functional MRI. People with mitochondrial disorders did not differ from healthy controls in time perception. However, the *pattern* of associations between time perception and physiological and neural variables differed between the groups, suggesting mitochondrial function moderates which processes shape subjective time.

**Why it matters:** Not much yet — but it is the first attempt to connect cellular energy metabolism to the subjective speed of time in humans, and it opens a line of enquiry that has previously been almost entirely theoretical.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the question, honestly reported: does the internal clock run on cellular energy? Open on the near-universal experience of time speeding up with age and the fact that nobody can say why. Call Martin Picard at Columbia, whose lab has built its reputation on linking mitochondrial biology to psychological states, and lead with the null — the mutation carriers' time sense was normal. The story must be framed as an exploratory first pass, not a finding.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on mitochondria's expanding portfolio — from energy plants to signaling hubs implicated in stress, mood and aging — and how much of that expansion is supported by evidence. Fits a biology-focused magazine or a critical long-read.

**Caveats:** Exploratory with many measures and weak associations, acknowledged by the authors; small N per group; primary group comparison was null; cross-sectional; findings are hypothesis-generating only.

### 14. A genetic epilepsy comes with a second kind of seizure that scans can't see

**Journal:** *Epileptic Disorders* | **Published:** 7 September 2026
**PMID:** 42704296 | **DOI:** 10.1002/epd2.70406
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — the first systematic description of a clinically consequential overlap, but tiny N and retrospective chart review.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Across six European epilepsy centers, researchers reviewed 27 people with genetically confirmed PCDH19-clustering epilepsy and found that eight (29.6 percent) also had functional, or dissociative, seizures — episodes that look like seizures but produce no epileptic activity on EEG. Median onset was age 16. Six of the eight were initially misdiagnosed as having epileptic seizures, leading to escalating antiseizure medication and hospital admissions. After correct diagnosis and multidisciplinary care, all treated individuals improved.

**Why it matters:** It documents a specific and preventable harm — people with a confirmed genetic epilepsy being given more drugs for seizures that are not epileptic — and suggests the two conditions are far from mutually exclusive.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The counterintuitive premise: having a proven genetic epilepsy makes clinicians *less* likely to consider that a given seizure is functional, and that assumption leads to years of unnecessary medication. Open on an adolescent whose drug doses kept climbing. Call Ángel Aledo-Serrano (Neuralma Institute, Madrid) or Rhys Thomas (Newcastle University). The wider point for readers is that "functional" does not mean fake — and that these seizures responded to treatment.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on functional neurological disorder's rehabilitation as a diagnosis: how a condition once dismissed as hysteria has acquired its own imaging literature, positive diagnostic criteria and effective treatments. Fits a health or medical-history outlet.

**Caveats:** Very small N (27 total, 8 with functional seizures); retrospective chart review across six centers with no standardized protocol; tertiary referral centers select for complex cases; no control group; improvement after treatment reported without systematic outcome measures.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- *Replication is now a design goal, not an afterthought.* The loneliness/default-mode study (PMID 42044556) is explicitly a confirmatory test: dorsomedial prefrontal connectivity survives across schizophrenia, depression and Alzheimer's, while the emotional-faces default-mode effect does not. That is the same self-auditing impulse seen last run in the failed grey-matter neuroprotection replication of Cognitive Enhancement Therapy (PMID 42623965) and the ABCD subtype clustering that largely came up empty (PMID 41989408).
- *Deflating popular neuro-narratives with numbers.* "Learn a language to protect your aging brain" turns out small and confined to executive processes (PMID 42684539), and the screen-addiction meta-analysis points at serotonergic and GABAergic systems rather than dopamine (PMID 42190596). Both echo last run's 1 percent mediation figure for childhood trauma and bipolar brain morphology (PMID 42234441).
- *Transdiagnostic and normative framing keeps gaining ground.* Social withdrawal tracking one network marker across three diagnoses (PMID 42044556) and negative-symptom severity indexed against ENIGMA Lifespan normative models (PMID 42176687) continue the reclassification pressure of the COGDIS-versus-UHR psychosis-risk study (PMID 41823413) and the RDoC-adjacent subtype failure (PMID 41989408).
- *Imaging is escaping the undergraduate participant pool.* Anonymous organ donors (PMID 42667790), Spanish-English heritage-language children (PMID 42667920) and rural Chinese epilepsy cohorts (PMID 42704311) extend last run's coal-miner fNIRS comparison (PMID 42518290).

**Advances**
- The GENFI work on resilience as an active network property (PMID 41277710) used group modeling; the schizophrenia structural covariance study (PMID 42176687) pushes the same normative-deviation approach into symptom dimensions, separating diminished motivation from diminished expressivity.
- Last run flagged mitochondrial and metabolic signatures as spatial overlays in night-shift nurses (PMID 42624217); this run has direct cellular bioenergetics measured in participants alongside MRI and time perception (PMID 42669931) — a real methodological step up, even though the headline group comparison was null.

**Counterpoints**
- Last run's connectome fingerprinting hit 93–99 percent accuracy at identifying individuals (PMID 42527301). This run's graph convolutional network reaches 62.6 percent balanced accuracy at identifying internet gaming disorder (PMID 42727812). Together they make a sharp point: fMRI is excellent at telling people apart and poor at telling disorders apart.
- The previous digest's implicit hope was that functional and connectivity measures would outperform anatomy. The loneliness replication (PMID 42044556) splits that hope down the middle — one functional marker holds, another dies.

**New themes**
- *Access, not analysis.* The rural China epilepsy cohort (PMID 42704311), with a twentyfold excess of brain malignancy deaths implying scans never done, introduces a global imaging-availability angle absent from the previous digest.
- *Misattributed clinical failure.* Poor memory scores in behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia with intact medial temporal structure (PMID 40995692) and functional seizures misread as epileptic in genetically confirmed PCDH19 epilepsy (PMID 42704296) are a new strand.
- *Algorithms audited rather than celebrated.* Logistic regression matching four machine learning methods on developmental delay prognosis (PMID 42634999), and saliency-map attribution being asked whether it counts as evidence at all (PMID 42727812).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch converge on the same uncomfortable point — the standard tests that decide a diagnosis frequently fail for reasons other than the one printed on the test's name. People with bvFTD flunk memory tests while their memory architecture is intact, the failure sitting in frontal retrieval systems (PMID 40995692); people with a confirmed genetic epilepsy have their functional seizures read as epileptic and their drug doses escalated (PMID 42704296); bilingual children who present as weak in their heritage language turn out to be predicting upcoming Spanish words fluently in the scanner (PMID 42667920); and a calibrated prognostic model for developmental delay reports a negative predictive value of just 31.7 percent, meaning most "reassuring" results aren't (PMID 42634999). The loneliness study adds the mirror image: a single neural marker of social withdrawal that ignores diagnostic boundaries altogether (PMID 42044556).

**Why pitch this now:** Cognitive testing is expanding fast — memory clinics, school language assessments, AI-assisted prognostic scoring — and these papers show, with imaging as the independent arbiter, that the score alone routinely misidentifies what is broken, with consequences that include misdiagnosed dementia, escalating antiseizure medication and children misplaced in school support systems.

**Angle:** A reported feature following one test score through several settings — clinic, school, developmental assessment — asking what it was designed to measure, what it actually measures, and what it would take to report *why* someone failed rather than just that they did. For a science-literate general readership with an appetite for how measurement goes wrong in ordinary institutional settings, told through named patients, clinicians and the researchers auditing their own instruments.

**Potential headlines:**
- Brain scans are revealing that clinical tests often fail for the wrong reason
- Failing a memory test doesn't mean your memory is broken
- The score says impaired, the brain says otherwise — and the treatment follows the score

**Potential outlets:**
- **New Scientist Features** — the batch's home section, and the bvFTD dissociation plus the heritage-language result give the feature two vivid, reader-implicating scenes rather than one abstract argument.
- **STAT** — owns the misdiagnosis-and-overtreatment beat; the PCDH19 medication-escalation finding and the 31.7 percent negative predictive value are exactly its kind of clinical-consequence detail.
