### 1. Cannabis anxiety does not rise with the dose — and eaten THC barely registers at all

**Journal:** *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42242480 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2026.106795
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — a large, well-powered dose-response synthesis of blinded placebo-controlled trials that contradicts the "stronger weed, more panic" assumption; limited slightly by reliance on infrequent users only.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Researchers pooled 41 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled experiments on the acute subjective effects of Δ9-THC in 1,020 healthy adults who used cannabis infrequently (under twice weekly, under 100 lifetime occasions). Compared with placebo, THC reliably increased feeling high, anxiety and tiredness while lowering alertness and contentedness. But dose-response meta-regression showed that higher inhaled doses increased feeling high and *decreased* calmness without increasing anxiety at all (b = −0.03, p = 0.845), and oral dose was not associated with any subjective effect measured.

**Why it matters:** The public health message that higher-potency cannabis produces proportionally more anxiety is not supported by controlled data in inexperienced users — the anxiety appears to be roughly all-or-nothing once you are intoxicated, which changes how harm-reduction advice about "low-dose" products should be framed.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on someone handed a 5 mg edible and told it is "the beginner dose" — then show what the pooled trial evidence actually predicts: on a 100-point scale, inhaled 5 mg THC raised anxiety by about 14 points and *cut* contentedness by 71, and doubling the dose did not double the anxiety. Call Amir Englund at King's College London, who leads this group's THC dosing work, and Tom Freeman at Bath on potency policy.
- **Wider angle:** A policy feature for a health or drug-policy outlet on why cannabis regulation has anchored itself to THC percentage as the proxy for harm, when the experimental dose-response curves for the effects consumers actually notice are flat or inconsistent — and what an evidence-based labeling standard would look like instead.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of heterogeneous lab studies; restricted to healthy infrequent users, so it does not generalize to daily users or people with psychiatric conditions; oral null results may partly reflect poor timing of peak-effect measurement; several authors declare pharmaceutical speaker fees (Jazz/GW, Otsuka, Lundbeck) unrelated to the work; an erratum has been published.

### 2. Anxious attachment at 56 predicts frailty and cognitive impairment at 68

**Journal:** *Health Psychology* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 41885885 | **DOI:** 10.1037/hea0001600
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a 12-year prospective design with a clean mediation story, held back by an all-male veteran sample.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** In 1,608 men from the Vietnam Era Twin Study of Aging, adult attachment style toward romantic partners was measured at mean age 56, candidate health mediators at 62, and cumulative deficit frailty plus mild cognitive impairment at 68. Attachment anxiety predicted later frailty entirely indirectly, via perceived stress, doctor visits and smoking (total indirect β = 0.147); its effect on cognitive impairment ran through perceived stress alone. Attachment avoidance did not predict frailty but directly predicted mild cognitive impairment (β = 0.134).

**Why it matters:** It suggests that how anxiously you attach to a partner in midlife tracks physical and cognitive aging two decades later, and points at stress and health behaviors as the route rather than something mysterious about relationships per se.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the surprise that two different insecure attachment styles land on two different endpoints by different routes — anxious attachment burns through the body via chronic stress and smoking, avoidant attachment shows up in cognition directly. Open on the midlife couple who assume relationship style is a matter of temperament, not health. Call Carol Franz or William Kremen at UC San Diego.
- **Wider angle:** A longevity feature for a general-interest magazine on the growing list of psychosocial variables now sitting alongside blood pressure in dementia risk models — loneliness, marital quality, attachment — and the awkward question of whether any of them are actually modifiable in your fifties.

**Caveats:** Observational, cannot establish causation; all-male, predominantly white US veteran cohort that will not generalize to women or other populations; mediators self-reported; twins are non-independent observations.

### 3. Body dissatisfaction feeds social anxiety through self-disgust — but only in girls

**Journal:** *Body Image* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42497472 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bodyim.2026.102155
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — three waves, over 1,000 adolescents and a clean gender dissociation; limited by self-report and a single-country sample.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** A total of 1,109 Chinese junior high school students (mean age 12.67, 51.9% girls) completed measures of body dissatisfaction, self-disgust and social anxiety at three time points. Controlling for autoregressive effects, body dissatisfaction predicted later social anxiety via self-disgust, and also predicted later self-disgust via social anxiety. Multi-group analysis found the self-disgust route was significant only for girls, while for boys the route ran the other way, through social anxiety.

**Why it matters:** Self-disgust — a visceral, moralized reaction to one's own body — emerges as a specific bridge from appearance worry to social fear in adolescent girls, which is a more targetable construct than "low body image."

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is that boys and girls arrive at the same place by opposite paths: for girls it is disgust at the body that breeds fear of other people; for boys it is fear of other people that breeds disgust at the body. Open on the 12-year-old avoiding the school changing room. Call Xuliang Shi at Hebei University; pair with a UK body-image researcher for context on whether the pathway replicates outside China.
- **Wider angle:** A features piece for an education or parenting outlet on self-disgust as an under-recognized emotion in adolescence — distinct from shame and from low self-esteem — and what school-based body-image programs currently miss by not measuring it.

**Caveats:** Observational and self-reported throughout; single-country early-adolescent sample; effect sizes for indirect paths not reported in the abstract; cannot establish causation despite longitudinal design.

### 4. Trait anxiety shows up in how brain states switch, not in alpha power

**Journal:** *International Journal of Psychophysiology* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42379500 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2026.113436
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — prespecified metrics, cross-validation and bootstrapping in 333 people make this unusually disciplined EEG work; it is still a correlational student sample.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Researchers recorded roughly six minutes of eyes-closed resting EEG from 333 undergraduates alongside questionnaires on trait anxiety, intolerance of uncertainty, metacognitive beliefs and depression. Three prespecified microstate transition metrics — the moment-to-moment switching between canonical whole-brain states — tracked trait anxiety and survived adjustment for depression, intolerance of uncertainty and metacognition, whereas the conventional markers, frontal alpha and midline theta power, did not. Model comparison with repeated five-fold cross-validation favored the microstate model over the spectral one.

**Why it matters:** Two decades of resting-EEG anxiety research has leaned on band power; this suggests the informative signal is in the temporal choreography of brain states rather than in how much alpha you have, and that much of the spectral signal was really depression riding along.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Frame it as the anxious brain being defined by its editing rhythm, not its volume — the sequence in which resting states hand off to one another. Open on the familiar "alpha waves = calm" folklore that powers consumer neurofeedback headsets, then show what a preregistered comparison found. Call Meng Zhang at Xinxiang Medical University; get an independent microstate methodologist to referee.
- **Wider angle:** A skeptical consumer-tech piece for a technology outlet on the neurofeedback and "brain-training" wearable industry, which is largely built on alpha-band metrics that this and similar work suggest may not index anxiety once depression is controlled for.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and correlational; undergraduate sample with subclinical trait anxiety, so it may not generalize to diagnosed anxiety disorders; single site; no clinical outcome measured; effect sizes are modest even where robust.

### 5. How unpleasant a threat cue still feels after therapy-style extinction predicts whether fear comes back

**Journal:** *Behaviour Research and Therapy* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42546511 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.brat.2026.105126
**Novelty:** Failed replication
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a genuine conceptual replication with a partly negative result, which is exactly the kind of nuance the desk likes; small student sample limits it.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Seventy-two first-year psychology students (65 female) went through a standard fear conditioning, extinction and context-renewal paradigm. Residual unpleasantness ratings of the conditioned stimulus at the end of extinction predicted self-reported fear when the cue was re-encountered in the original context. The relationship did not hold for electrodermal responding, which the authors attribute to non-fear drivers such as orienting.

**Why it matters:** It supports a cheap, one-question marker — "how unpleasant does this still feel?" — for who might relapse after exposure therapy, while showing that the physiological measure clinicians and researchers often treat as objective does not carry the same signal.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is that the sweaty-palms measure fails where a simple subjective rating succeeds. Open on the person leaving their final exposure session for spider phobia, no longer frightened but still finding the image faintly repellent — and what that residue predicts. Call Ottmar Lipp's group at Queensland University of Technology.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-and-culture feature for a psychology or science-studies outlet on the long-running assumption that physiological measures are more trustworthy than self-report in fear research, and the accumulating evidence that in extinction paradigms the reverse can be true.

**Caveats:** N = 72, heavily female first-year psychology students; laboratory analog of clinical relapse, not clinical relapse itself; funded by the Australian Research Council (declared as a competing interest by the authors); partial replication — one of two outcome channels did not replicate.

### 6. The pandemic did not uniformly worsen existing mental illness — and bipolar disorder consistently improved

**Journal:** *Current Opinion in Psychiatry* | **Published:** 1 September 2026
**PMID:** 42474420 | **DOI:** 10.1097/YCO.0000000000001103
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — genuinely corrective of a dominant narrative because it restricts itself to studies with pre-pandemic baselines; it is a review, not new data.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)

**The study:** This review synthesizes longitudinal studies of people with pre-existing mental disorders that had actual pre-pandemic symptom measurements, rather than the retrospective or cross-sectional designs that dominate the COVID mental health literature. Symptoms worsened in bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, substance use disorders and contamination-related obsessive-compulsive symptoms; symptoms consistently *improved* in bipolar disorders; and depressive and anxiety disorders, anorexia nervosa and non-contamination OCD stayed largely stable. People with chronic or more severe pre-pandemic illness more often showed stable or improving trajectories than those with milder or recent-onset conditions.

**Why it matters:** The widely repeated claim that lockdowns devastated the mental health of the already-ill does not survive contact with studies that measured people beforehand — and the pattern where the most severely ill fared relatively best is the opposite of what was predicted.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the inversion: the people everyone worried about most were, on average, not the ones who deteriorated. Open on a patient with long-standing anxiety disorder whose symptoms flatlined through 2020 while the newly worried filled the surveys. Call Hans Hoek at Groningen/Parnassia or first author Diana Czepiel at VU Amsterdam. Be careful not to imply lockdowns were harmless overall.
- **Wider angle:** A media-criticism feature for a general-interest or journalism outlet on how the "mental health tsunami" story was built almost entirely from cross-sectional surveys with no baseline, and what pre-pandemic cohorts revealed once they reported years later — a case study in the epistemics of crisis science.

**Caveats:** Narrative review of a small and methodologically diverse literature; the authors explicitly say definitive statements are premature; selection into cohorts with pre-pandemic data may bias toward more engaged, better-treated patients; no meta-analytic pooling.

### 7. Trans patients use more mental health care and still fare worse — inside a trans-protective state

**Journal:** *American Psychologist* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42658622 | **DOI:** 10.1037/amp0001706
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — 32,000 patients of real electronic health record data with a formal disparity-decomposition method; the design cannot say why access fails to help.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Using electronic health records from 2015–2023 at a large safety-net system in the Bronx, researchers compared 2,898 transgender, nonbinary and gender-expansive adults with 29,818 cisgender adults, applying the Institute of Medicine rank-and-replace method to isolate disparities not explained by clinical need. Trans patients had roughly three times the rate of anxiety disorders (9.7% vs 3.1%) and four times the rate of mood disorders (10.9% vs 2.6%), and higher rates of primary care use, minimally adequate mental health treatment, emergency mental health visits, suicide-related visits and inpatient stays. Disparities compounded for Black and Hispanic patients.

**Why it matters:** It undercuts the assumption that access is the binding constraint — in a legally protective jurisdiction, with more contact with services, outcomes remained worse, which points at what happens inside the appointment rather than whether it happens.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Sell the "care paradox": more visits, more treatment, worse anxiety and mood outcomes, in one of the most protective legal environments in the US. Open on the trans patient who has done everything the system asked. Call Aaron Breslow at Albert Einstein College of Medicine. Frame carefully around what the data can and cannot attribute to clinical quality.
- **Wider angle:** A health-services piece for a specialist outlet on rank-and-replace and other disparity-decomposition methods — how health economists separate "sicker" from "worse served," and why the distinction keeps producing uncomfortable findings across other minoritized groups.

**Caveats:** Retrospective observational cohort, cannot establish causation; single urban safety-net system, so it may not generalize; diagnoses derived from billing and clinical coding, which can itself be biased; gender identity capture in EHRs is incomplete.

### 8. Four days of intensive treatment outperforms three weeks of inpatient care for OCD

**Journal:** *Australian & New Zealand Journal of Psychiatry* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42500867 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00048674261462748
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a striking effect size and zero dropouts, but N = 25 and non-randomized allocation cap it.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Twenty-five inpatients with moderate-to-severe obsessive-compulsive disorder were non-randomly assigned to either the Bergen 4-Day Treatment (n = 12) or a standard three-week specialist program (n = 13) in Australia, with independent assessments at 10 days and three months. Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale scores were substantially lower after the four-day program at both time points (13.46 and 11.84) than after standard care (19.04 and 19.15), with 91.9% response and 45.8% remission, no dropouts, and parallel reductions in depression and anxiety.

**Why it matters:** If it holds up in randomized trials, a compressed four-day format could shorten waiting lists and inpatient stays for a condition where specialist treatment is scarce — the result is that less time, delivered differently, did more.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead on the arithmetic: four days beat 21, and nobody dropped out. Open on the person who has waited two years for a specialist OCD bed. Call Nick Dowling at the University of Melbourne, and Bjarne Hansen in Bergen, who co-designed the protocol — and note his declared conflict.
- **Wider angle:** A health-systems feature for a broadsheet or specialist outlet on the "concentrated treatment" movement spreading out of Norway into other conditions, and the awkward question of whether long inpatient stays for anxiety-spectrum disorders ever had an evidence base in the first place.

**Caveats:** N = 25, single center, non-randomized allocation with obvious selection risk; open-label with self-rated secondary outcomes; three-month follow-up only; the developer of the intervention is an author and declares a conflict of interest.

### 9. Nearly half of women with polycystic ovary syndrome have clinically significant anxiety

**Journal:** *European Neuropsychopharmacology* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42217371 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.euroneuro.2026.112880
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — an enormous, credibility-graded mapping exercise; the individual headline numbers come from underlying meta-analyses of varying quality.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** An umbrella review of 81 meta-analyses of observational studies (1,780 primary studies, 254,154,533 records) mapped how psychiatric disorders co-occur with endocrine, nutritional and metabolic conditions, regrading each estimate for credibility. Only 10 of 64 prevalence estimates reached strong credibility — among them anxiety in polycystic ovary syndrome at 48%, anxiety in type 1 diabetes at 21.3%, depression in diabetes at 61.8%, and vitamin D deficiency in schizophrenia at 65.3%. Comorbid depression in people with diabetes carried a 48% higher all-cause mortality risk and more than double the dementia risk.

**Why it matters:** It provides the first credibility-graded atlas of psychiatric-metabolic multimorbidity, and it makes clear that only a small minority of widely cited comorbidity figures survive quantitative grading.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Focus the piece on PCOS — a condition affecting roughly one in ten women of reproductive age, where the strongest-credibility estimate says nearly half have anxiety, and where endocrinology appointments rarely screen for it. Open on a woman managing PCOS for a decade with no one ever asking about panic. Call Marco Fornaro at Federico II Naples or Marco Solmi at Ottawa.
- **Wider angle:** A meta-science feature for a specialist outlet on what happens when you grade the evidence base rather than just count it: only 15 of 119 estimates in this atlas reached strong credibility, a hit rate that says something uncomfortable about how comorbidity statistics circulate in clinical guidelines.

**Caveats:** Umbrella review of observational meta-analyses — every estimate is correlational; heterogeneity across underlying studies is substantial; AMSTAR-2 quality of included reviews varied; two authors declare pharmaceutical consultancy and equity holdings (Solmi: AbbVie, Angelini, Lundbeck, Otsuka and others, shares in MESAS and S2M; Siskind: Viatris, Servier, Lundbeck).

### 10. Learning to step back from your thoughts blunts the link between symptoms and daily impairment

**Journal:** *Behavior Therapy* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42660582 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.beth.2026.04.002
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — 12 monthly waves in 386 people gives real within-person resolution, but everything is self-reported.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** A community sample of 386 adults completed monthly surveys for a year covering depression, generalized anxiety, social anxiety, panic, functional impairment, well-being and decentering — the capacity to observe your own thoughts as passing mental events rather than facts. Within any given month, higher symptoms predicted lower well-being via impairment, and prospective analyses showed the loop ran in both directions. Decentering weakened the symptom-to-impairment link, both month by month and cumulatively, but did not touch the impairment-to-well-being link.

**Why it matters:** It locates a specific, trainable psychological skill at one particular joint in the vicious circle — it stops anxiety from wrecking your functioning, but does not stop wrecked functioning from flattening your mood.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The interesting bit is where decentering *fails*. Open on someone who has learned in therapy to watch their anxious thoughts drift past, functions fine at work, and still feels their life has narrowed — then explain the two-link chain and which link the skill protects. Call Kate Naragon-Gainey at the University of Western Australia.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a psychology or wellness-skeptical outlet on the mindfulness industry's mechanism problem: decentering is the ingredient most mindfulness programs actually deliver, and dismantling studies like this one show it works on some outcomes and not others — useful ammunition against blanket claims.

**Caveats:** Observational, all self-report, cannot establish causation; monthly resolution may miss faster dynamics; community sample recruited online, generalizability uncertain; no intervention component, so "malleable" is an assumption imported from other literatures.

### 11. Your partner's ADHD symptoms track your postpartum anxiety

**Journal:** *Journal of Attention Disorders* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 41982066 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10870547261438170
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — clinician-administered ADHD assessment in both parents of each dyad is a genuine strength; N = 117 dyads and cross-sectional timing limit it.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Researchers recruited 117 mother-father dyads (half with parental ADHD) when their infants were 6–10 months old, combining questionnaires with clinician-administered semi-structured interviews for ADHD and psychiatric history. Individuals with ADHD had 4.58 times the odds of moderate-to-severe anxiety symptoms and 2.70 times the odds of depressive symptoms. Crucially, the coparent's ADHD symptoms were also associated with an individual's postpartum mood symptoms in self-report models, and the pattern held regardless of parent sex.

**Why it matters:** Postpartum mental health screening focuses almost exclusively on the birthing mother's mood; this suggests ADHD in either parent is a candidate risk marker for the other's distress, and that fathers belong in the frame.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the coparent effect — the possibility that living with an undiagnosed adult ADHD partner in the first year of a baby's life tracks your own anxiety. Open on a new father whose forgetfulness has never been named. Call Heather Joseph or Brooke Molina at the University of Pittsburgh. Do not imply that treating ADHD would reduce a partner's anxiety — the design cannot show that.
- **Wider angle:** A family-health feature for a parenting or general-interest outlet on the surge in adult ADHD diagnoses hitting couples in early parenthood, and how perinatal services — built around one patient — are structurally unable to see a two-person problem.

**Caveats:** N = 117 dyads, single site; cross-sectional at 6–10 months postpartum, so the direction of the coparent association is unknown; the coparent effect appeared only in self-report and not clinician-report models; ADHD and non-ADHD dyads differed on prior mood disorder history (47% vs 24%).

### 12. Guided touch beats virtual reality for bonding in unplanned pregnancy — and both cut anxiety

**Journal:** *Journal of Reproductive and Infant Psychology* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42610239 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02646838.2025.2539989
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a three-arm randomized trial with 217 participants and a head-to-head comparison of high-tech against no-tech; single-site and unblinded.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Two hundred and seventeen women with unplanned pregnancies at a public hospital in eastern Turkey were randomized to haptonomy (a touch-based bonding technique), virtual reality fetal visualization, or control, receiving four sessions between 24 and 32 weeks' gestation. Both interventions lowered pregnancy-related anxiety and raised acceptance of pregnancy and prenatal attachment relative to control. Prenatal attachment was significantly higher in the haptonomy group than the virtual reality group.

**Why it matters:** In a direct contest between an immersive digital rendering of the fetus and hands on the belly, the hands won on attachment — a useful data point for a maternity technology sector betting heavily on immersive imaging.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Frame it as low tech versus high tech in the same trial, and the low-tech arm winning where it counts. Open on the moment a midwife guides a woman's hands to feel the fetus respond, versus the same woman in a headset. Call Ayşe Nur Yılmaz at Fırat University. Note this is one trial in one setting.
- **Wider angle:** A technology-and-medicine feature for a specialist health or tech outlet on VR's expanding role in obstetrics — from pain management in labor to fetal visualization — and the emerging evidence that immersion is not automatically more powerful than embodied contact.

**Caveats:** Single-site trial in one region of Turkey, unlikely to generalize culturally; participants and providers could not be blinded to allocation; all outcomes self-reported on questionnaires; data collected 2020–2021 during the pandemic; no long-term or postnatal follow-up reported.

### 13. A gamified app cuts anxiety by breaking up rumination, and the mediation checks out

**Journal:** *Behavior Therapy* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42660574 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.beth.2026.06.003
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — randomized with weekly mechanism measurement, which is rare in app trials, but the comparator is a waitlist.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** One hundred and one adults with mild to moderate depression were randomized to an eight-week gamified mobile app built on the Facilitating Thought Progression framework — designed to loosen stuck, narrow thinking — or to a waitlist. Weekly assessments of depression, anxiety and rumination fed a multilevel structural equation model, which showed that reduction in rumination significantly mediated decreases in both depression and anxiety in the intervention arm but not in the waitlist arm.

**Why it matters:** Most digital mental health trials report only whether symptoms fall; this one tests and supports a specific cognitive mechanism, which is what would let developers know what they are actually building.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The angle is mechanism, not efficacy: an app that works by making your thoughts move rather than by telling you to challenge them. Open on someone lying awake with the same sentence cycling. Call Paola Pedrelli at Massachusetts General Hospital and Moshe Bar, whose thought-progression theory underlies the design.
- **Wider angle:** A digital-health feature for a technology or business outlet on the mental health app market's replication and mechanism gap — thousands of products, a handful with randomized data, almost none with a tested theory of change.

**Caveats:** N = 101 with a waitlist rather than active control, so expectancy effects cannot be separated from mechanism; all outcomes self-reported; participants had mild to moderate depression, not diagnosed anxiety disorders; eight weeks with no reported follow-up; mediation from a randomized-but-unblinded design is suggestive rather than conclusive.

### 14. Highly anxious people over-generalize fear — but extinguish it just as well as anyone else

**Journal:** *Behaviour Research and Therapy* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42546510 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.brat.2026.105120
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a well-designed extreme-groups experiment whose value is mostly in what it failed to find.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Eighty-eight pain-free adults preselected for high versus low trait anxiety completed a conditioning task in which some visual cues predicted pain, then learned to avoid the pain with button presses, then faced generalization, response prevention with extinction in a new context, and a renewal test. Avoidance renewal replicated. High-anxious participants showed more fear generalization to similar cues and more avoidance of the threat cue at renewal — but no deficit in differential learning, no broader avoidance generalization, no reduced extinction and no greater context-driven renewal.

**Why it matters:** The popular picture of the anxious brain as globally bad at learning safety is not what this experiment found; the vulnerability looks narrower and more specific — over-spreading fear to resembling cues — which changes what exposure therapy would need to correct.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the negative results, which are the story: four of five predicted anxiety deficits did not appear. Open on the chronic pain patient who avoids a whole category of movements after one bad episode. Call Ann Meulders at Maastricht/KU Leuven, a leading figure in pain-related avoidance conditioning.
- **Wider angle:** A chronic-pain feature for a health or specialist outlet on why exposure-based rehabilitation so often unravels once the patient leaves the clinic — the renewal effect — and what physiotherapists are doing about context.

**Caveats:** N = 88 pain-free volunteers in a laboratory analog, not patients with anxiety disorders or chronic pain; extreme-groups design inflates apparent contrasts; several null findings may reflect limited power rather than true absence of effect; single site.

### 15. A stronger stress response protects lonely young adults from anxiety

**Journal:** *Biological Psychology* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42302916 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.biopsycho.2026.109328
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — measures both branches of the autonomic nervous system, which is unusual, but it is a moderation analysis in 188 undergraduates with self-reported outcomes.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** In 188 undergraduates, researchers measured parasympathetic activity (high-frequency heart rate variability) and sympathetic activity (pre-ejection period) both at rest and in response to a laboratory challenge, then tested whether these patterns moderated the link between loneliness and self-reported adjustment. High resting HF-HRV combined with a large drop during challenge protected lonely young adults from depressive symptoms and sleep disturbance. High resting sympathetic activity combined with a large sympathetic response protected them from anxiety and depression.

**Why it matters:** It complicates the intuitive idea that a big physiological stress response is bad for you — here, reactivity in either autonomic branch buffered the psychological cost of loneliness, suggesting the relevant variable is flexibility rather than calm.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is that the students whose bodies reacted most strongly to a lab stressor were the ones whose loneliness cost them least in anxiety. Open on the lonely first-year whose heart pounds in a seminar — and what that might indicate. Call Lauren Philbrook at Colgate University. Note this is correlational moderation, not a physiological training recommendation.
- **Wider angle:** A physiology feature for a specialist or general-science outlet on the rise and fall of heart rate variability as a wellness metric — millions of people now track HRV daily on wearables, and studies like this show resting HRV alone tells you very little without a challenge condition.

**Caveats:** N = 188 undergraduates at a single US college, unlikely to generalize; cross-sectional and observational; adjustment outcomes entirely self-reported; multiple moderation tests raise the risk of chance findings; no replication sample.

### 16. An expressive writing trial for trans adults works — and so does the control condition

**Journal:** *American Psychologist* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42658633 | **DOI:** 10.1037/amp0001639
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a properly randomized three-arm trial in a hard-to-reach population, with an honest null between groups that the abstract somewhat softens.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** One hundred and fifteen transgender and gender expansive adults in the US were randomized to one of two versions of RECLAIM, a free, self-administered online expressive writing intervention targeting internalized anti-trans stigma, or to an expressive writing active control. Between-group analysis found all three conditions improved depressive and anxiety symptoms to a similar degree; differences emerged only in within-group trajectories over time.

**Why it matters:** For a population facing acute barriers to affirming care, the finding that any structured expressive writing helped as much as the stigma-targeted version is practically useful — and a reminder that active controls in psychological trials are rarely inert.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the honest result: the specially designed intervention did not beat plain expressive writing, and both helped. Open on the trans adult in a state with no accessible affirming provider, opening a laptop. Call Elliot Tebbe at UW-Madison. Frame this as evidence about what free self-help can and cannot do, without overclaiming.

### 17. Rewriting childhood memories helps hoarders whom standard CBT failed

**Journal:** *Behaviour Research and Therapy* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42462484 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.brat.2026.105115
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a randomized multiple-baseline design is a credible way to test a novel intervention, but N = 10.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Ten people with hoarding disorder (mean age 65.8, 80% female) who had not benefited sufficiently from cognitive behavioral therapy received 12 fortnightly sessions of imagery rescripting targeting early aversive memories, following a randomized baseline monitoring period. Nine completed treatment with high satisfaction. Hoarding symptoms, hoarding beliefs, depression and avoidant attachment all fell with large effect sizes; anxiety and obsessive-compulsive symptoms fell by follow-up; half the sample showed clinically significant improvement and three were classifiable as recovered.

**Why it matters:** It tests a specific theory — that intense attachment to objects compensates for early thwarted relatedness — by intervening on the memories rather than the clutter, in exactly the patients for whom decluttering-focused CBT has already failed.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a 65-year-old who has spent years in therapy sorting boxes, then spent 12 sessions revisiting a childhood scene instead — and started letting things go. The hook is that the treatment never addresses the possessions. Call Jessica Grisham at UNSW Sydney, whose lab drives the attachment account of hoarding.
- **Wider angle:** A long-read for a general-interest magazine on how hoarding disorder got reframed in the last decade — from a variant of OCD to a condition of relationships and loss — and what that reframing has cost and gained the people living in it.

**Caveats:** N = 10, single center, no control group beyond the within-person baseline; all participants were prior CBT non-responders, a highly selected group; predominantly older and female; one-month follow-up only; outcomes largely self-reported; funded by an Australian Government Research Training Program Scholarship.

### 18. Boredom may be a transdiagnostic symptom rather than a mood, and it has a brain signature

**Journal:** *Brain and Behavior* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42675870 | **DOI:** 10.1002/brb3.71718
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — an appealing and highly relatable framing, but it is a narrative review with no new data and no formal synthesis.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)

**The study:** This narrative review pulls together conceptual, EEG, neuroimaging, autonomic and clinical work on boredom, arguing it should be treated as a dimensional construct cutting across ADHD, mood and anxiety disorders, psychosis, addiction, eating disorders, personality disorders and suicidality. Reported neural correlates include increased alpha activity, altered theta dynamics, greater default mode network involvement and reduced fronto-parietal control engagement. Autonomic data suggest boredom is not simply a low-arousal state but a fluctuating pattern of disengagement and re-engagement.

**Why it matters:** Not much clinically yet, but it opens a line of enquiry: if boredom is a measurable dimension that predicts poor treatment adherence and maladaptive coping across diagnoses, it belongs in assessment batteries where it currently does not appear.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the counterintuitive physiology — boredom is not the brain idling but a restless oscillation between disengagement and search. Open on the familiar phone-scrolling loop. Call Donatella Marazziti at the University of Pisa, and pair with John Eastwood or James Danckert, who lead the experimental boredom field, for an independent view.
- **Wider angle:** A cultural feature for a general-interest outlet on boredom as a modern clinical variable — the argument that our attention environment has made sustained meaningful engagement harder, and what that does to people already vulnerable to addiction or anxiety.

**Caveats:** Narrative review with no systematic search strategy or quality appraisal; the underlying EEG and imaging literature is small and heterogeneous; no new data; the transdiagnostic claim is currently a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated finding.

### 19. Guided deep relaxation for autistic children eases anxiety while heart rate variability moves the wrong way

**Journal:** *Research in Developmental Disabilities* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42632169 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ridd.2026.105367
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — an intriguing dissociation between subjective and physiological outcomes, but N = 13 with no control group means it can only generate hypotheses.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Thirteen autistic children aged 8–14, including one minimally speaking child, completed a co-designed six-week online yoga nidra program — guided meditation built on concrete visualizations and breath awareness, chosen because standard CBT and abstract mindfulness make heavy cognitive demands. No statistically significant anxiety change emerged, but medium effect sizes appeared in self- and parent-reported anxiety, three children moved out of the clinically significant range, and greater attendance correlated strongly with self-reported anxiety reduction. Unexpectedly, heart rate variability indicated reduced autonomic functioning after the program.

**Why it matters:** The mismatch between children reporting less anxiety and their physiology moving in the opposite direction is a genuine puzzle, and it matters because HRV is increasingly used as an "objective" outcome in autism intervention research.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on an autistic child who says the guided practice helped, whose parent agrees, and whose heart rhythms say otherwise — then ask which measure we should believe in a population with well-documented atypical interoception. Call Tundi Loftus and Danielle Mathersul at Murdoch University.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a specialist health or disability outlet on co-design in autism research — this is the first yoga nidra program built with autistic input, and the qualitative themes ("Thinking About the Body is Hard", "Homework Sucks") say more about intervention design than the effect sizes do.

**Caveats:** N = 13 feasibility study with no control group and no significant primary outcome; self- and parent-reported anxiety measures; qualitative interviews with only three families; online delivery with adherence problems; single site.

### 20. Telling teenagers with epilepsy that they could die suddenly does not make them more anxious

**Journal:** *Epilepsy & Behavior* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42208388 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.yebeh.2026.111134
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — addresses a real clinical taboo with a clear answer, but 30 participants, no control group and one month of follow-up.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Thirty adolescents aged 13–18 with epilepsy in New Delhi (median age 14.5) received structured counseling that explicitly incorporated communication about sudden unexpected death in epilepsy, delivered via written material, a video and a face-to-face discussion. One month later, medication adherence, sleep practices, seizure action planning, rescue medication availability and avoidance of unsafe activities had all improved, with 93.3% changing at least one safety behavior. Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 scores did not change, and most participants endorsed including the SUDEP discussion.

**Why it matters:** Clinicians frequently withhold SUDEP information from adolescents on the grounds it will frighten them; this pilot suggests the fear is misplaced and the behavioral payoff is real, though it needs a controlled trial.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the taboo: neurologists routinely decide not to tell teenagers about the leading cause of epilepsy-related death. Open on a 15-year-old who has never heard the term SUDEP. Call Suvasini Sharma at Lady Hardinge Medical College or Robyn Whitney at McMaster. Emphasize this is a pilot and that a null on a distress scale is not proof of no distress.
- **Wider angle:** A medical-ethics feature for a health or general-interest outlet on therapeutic non-disclosure in adolescent medicine — cystic fibrosis, congenital heart disease, epilepsy — and the growing evidence that young patients handle prognostic information better than their doctors expect.

**Caveats:** N = 30, single center in India, no control group; pre-post design cannot rule out attention or expectancy effects; behavioral outcomes self-reported; one-month follow-up only; a null result on DASS-21 in a small sample is weak evidence of no emotional harm.

### 21. Adolescents with cannabis use disorder hold distinctive beliefs about their own thinking

**Journal:** *Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy* | **Published:** September–October 2026
**PMID:** 42656180 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cpp.70326
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a decent matched case-control sample in an under-studied population, but cross-sectional and cannot separate cause from consequence.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Sixty-nine adolescents aged 12–18 diagnosed with cannabis use disorder were compared with 83 age- and sex-matched healthy controls in Diyarbakır, Turkey, using measures of metacognition, perceived cognitive flexibility and depression-anxiety-stress. The cannabis group scored higher on total metacognition, markedly higher on superstition-punishment-responsibility beliefs, higher on depression, anxiety and stress, and lower on perceived cognitive flexibility. Within the cannabis group, negative metacognitive beliefs and total emotional symptoms together explained 36.5% of variance in addiction severity.

**Why it matters:** It points toward metacognitive beliefs — convictions about the uncontrollability and danger of your own thoughts — as a candidate treatment target in adolescent cannabis use, sitting alongside anxiety rather than downstream of it.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a teenager who believes their own thoughts are dangerous and uncontrollable, and uses cannabis to shut them down — the profile these data describe. The hook is that treatment for adolescent cannabis use rarely touches beliefs about thinking. Call Nurullah Kızıl or Masum Öztürk at Dicle University; ask Adrian Wells' metacognitive therapy group for an independent view.
- **Wider angle:** A piece for a specialist mental health or education outlet on metacognitive therapy's expansion out of generalized anxiety disorder into addiction, eating disorders and adolescence — a genuinely different treatment logic from CBT that most clinicians outside the UK and Scandinavia have not encountered.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional case-control, so causal direction is unknown — cannabis use could produce the metacognitive profile rather than the reverse; N = 152 total across two groups; single center in one Turkish city; all psychological measures self-reported; controls described as healthy but selection process unclear.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Four studies in this batch independently find that the physiological measures anxiety research treats as objective ground truth fail to track anxiety — while cheap self-report does. Residual unpleasantness ratings predicted the return of fear after extinction but electrodermal responding did not (PMID 42546511); autistic children and their parents reported less anxiety after guided relaxation while heart rate variability moved the wrong way (PMID 42632169); prespecified EEG microstate transitions tracked trait anxiety whereas frontal alpha and midline theta — the workhorses of two decades of resting-EEG work — did not survive adjustment for depression (PMID 42379500); and resting heart rate variability was uninformative about loneliness-related anxiety except in combination with a challenge response, where larger reactivity in either autonomic branch was protective (PMID 42302916).

**Why pitch this now:** Consumer wearables now push daily HRV "readiness" and stress scores to tens of millions of people, and the neurofeedback headset market sells alpha-band calm — both resting on exactly the metrics these four papers find weakest, at the same moment regulators and clinicians are being asked to accept digital biomarkers as trial endpoints.

**Angle:** A reported piece on the quiet reversal inside anxiety science — that the sweaty palms, the alpha waves and the resting heart rhythm are turning out to be worse guides to who is anxious, and who will relapse, than simply asking; written for a general science readership that has been sold the opposite premise by its own wrist.

**Potential headlines:**
- Asking people how they feel beats measuring their heart, brain and skin
