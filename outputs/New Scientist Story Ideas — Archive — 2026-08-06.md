### 1. Your brainwaves before you drink ayahuasca predict how mystical the experience will feel

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychopharmacology* | **Published:** 2026 Aug
**PMID:** 41342247 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02698811251389563
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Counterintuitive finding (pre-drug brainwaves predict subjective psychedelic experience), RCT design, clear hook for NS Mind's interest in psychedelics and consciousness research; small N and single-centre status limit score.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** In a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 50 healthy volunteers in Brazil received either ayahuasca (1 mL/kg) or placebo, with EEG recorded before and two and four hours after administration. Ayahuasca decreased global alpha waves and increased frontomedial delta and right posterior theta and beta activity, while also intensifying perception, emotion, mystical experiences, and a form of chaotic, content-rich mind-wandering. Crucially, individual differences in baseline EEG — measured before any drug was given — predicted the intensity of subsequent subjective experiences: lower pre-dose theta was linked to stronger interoceptive sensations, and lower pre-dose beta to more positive emotion.

**Why it matters:** The idea that pre-existing brain activity patterns predict psychedelic experiences suggests it may one day be possible to identify who will respond most strongly to psychedelic-assisted therapy before treatment begins.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Your brainwaves before taking ayahuasca predict how mystical the trip will feel" Before a single drop of ayahuasca was consumed, the participants' own brain oscillations were already foretelling how intense and mystical their experience would become. In the first placebo-controlled EEG study of its kind, Brazilian researchers found that ayahuasca triggers sweeping changes in brain oscillations — suppressing the alpha waves associated with relaxed wakefulness while amplifying delta and theta activity. More surprisingly, EEG recorded before the drug was administered predicted how strongly each person would feel bodily sensations and positive emotions during the experience. If replicated, this hints that brain scanning could eventually help clinicians select — or prepare — patients before psychedelic therapy sessions. Why it fits: With psychedelic-assisted therapy attracting enormous public and scientific interest, NS Mind readers will be fascinated by the idea that baseline neuroscience could predict individual responses.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** small sample (N<100), single-center. Small sample (N=50), single centre in Brazil, healthy volunteers only so findings may not translate to clinical populations; correlational relationships between EEG and subjective experience do not establish causation *Editor's fact-check note: Abstract confirms N=50, randomised double-blind placebo-controlled parallel-arm design, single centre (Federal University of Rio Grande do Norte, Brazil). EEG direction of effects verified: alpha decreased, frontomedial delta and right posterior theta/beta increased. Baseline EEG predictions confirmed as stated. No corrections needed.*

### 2. Playing balance video games at higher difficulty cuts a key Alzheimer's protein in people with mild cognitive impairment

**Journal:** *Games for Health Journal* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 30
**PMID:** 42533557 | **DOI:** 10.1177/2161783X261461636
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — Strong topic fit (cognition, brain health, novel intervention) and RCT design with biomarkers boost the score, but small N, single-center, and short follow-up cap it at 7.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers randomly assigned 97 adults with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to one of four exergame balance-training groups — mild, moderate, high difficulty, or a Wii Fit control — for 40-minute sessions three times a week over eight weeks. Blood markers of neurodegeneration and cognitive function were assessed at baseline, week 4, and week 8. Higher-difficulty exergame training was associated with increased serum BDNF (a brain growth factor) and reduced p-tau181 (a marker linked to Alzheimer's pathology), alongside improvements in attention, abstraction, and visuospatial skills. The Alzheimer's-related Aβ1-42 marker did not change significantly.

**Why it matters:** The findings suggest that the cognitive challenge level of exercise-based video games may matter as much as the exercise itself for brain health, pointing toward a potential low-cost, scalable intervention for people in the early stages of cognitive decline.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Harder balance video games shrink an Alzheimer's protein — easy ones don't" Cranking up the difficulty on a balance exergame appears to reduce a key Alzheimer's blood marker in people already showing early cognitive decline — something the easy version failed to do. A randomised trial in 97 adults with mild cognitive impairment found that cognitively demanding balance video games — but not easy ones — lowered p-tau181, a blood marker associated with Alzheimer's disease, while also boosting a brain growth factor and improving attention and spatial skills. The finding upends the assumption that any exercise-based game is equally beneficial and raises the intriguing possibility that mental challenge embedded in physical activity is the active ingredient. With dementia affecting tens of millions worldwide and no widely accessible pharmacological prevention, a difficulty dial on a video game could be a surprisingly accessible tool. Why it fits: NS Mind readers care deeply about practical, non-pharmaceutical ways to protect brain health as they age, and the 'difficulty matters' angle is genuinely surprising.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** small sample (N<100), single-center, short follow-up, observational biomarker outcomes in RCT context. Small sample (N=97), short eight-week follow-up, single-centre trial in Pakistan — long-term effects and generalisability to Western populations are unknown. Blood biomarker changes do not confirm clinical benefit *Editor's fact-check note: N=97 is just below the N≥100 threshold; noted as small sample. Study is conducted in Pakistan, which is a context-specific population but the biological mechanism (BDNF, tau) is universal enough not to warrant a regional-specificity deduction.*

### 3. People with schizophrenia can feel pleasure in the moment — the real problem is anticipating and holding onto it

**Journal:** *Schizophrenia Research* | **Published:** 2026 Aug
**PMID:** 41996962 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.schres.2026.04.003
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — Counterintuitive finding that challenges a widely-held model of schizophrenia (+2), strong conceptual hook for general readers interested in emotion and mental illness (+1), but no primary human participants data and a somewhat technical argument reduce broad accessibility.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** In this critical review paper, researchers reexamined the 'emotion paradox' in schizophrenia — the puzzling observation that people with the condition report reduced pleasure yet show relatively normal positive affect when directly exposed to pleasant stimuli. The authors argue the paradox is partly a methodological artefact: measures differ in how much they rely on memory and mental representation versus immediate sensation. Their reanalysis suggests the core problem in anhedonia is not an inability to feel pleasure in the moment, but impaired processes of anticipating, maintaining, and translating reward into motivated behaviour.

**Why it matters:** If the deficit in schizophrenia is in anticipating and sustaining reward — not experiencing it — that reframes treatment targets and could help clinicians design more effective therapies focused on motivation and future-thinking rather than pleasure itself.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "People with schizophrenia can feel pleasure — they just struggle to look forward to it" The longstanding idea that schizophrenia strips away the capacity for pleasure turns out to be more complicated — and more hopeful — than scientists assumed. For decades, clinicians believed people with schizophrenia simply couldn't feel pleasure — a symptom called anhedonia. But a new critical review argues the 'emotion paradox' at the heart of this belief is partly a measurement illusion: when you put people with schizophrenia in front of something genuinely enjoyable, they respond much like anyone else. The real deficit, researchers say, is in the mental machinery that lets us anticipate future pleasures, hold them in mind, and convert them into motivation. This reframing matters enormously: it suggests that therapies targeting expectation and planning — rather than pleasure itself — might be more effective. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are fascinated by counterintuitive findings about emotion and mental illness, especially when they challenge stigmatising narratives about conditions like schizophrenia.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** None identified — this is a theoretical/review paper; no primary data, no participants enrolled. This is a theoretical review, not a new empirical study; no new patient data were collected. The authors' argument is interpretive and contested in the field — journalists should seek independent comment from schizophrenia researchers not involved in the paper *Editor's fact-check note: This is a perspective/critical review article, not an empirical study with a participant N. No sample size to verify. Caveats updated accordingly.*

### 4. Skipping meals before drinking to get drunk faster is common across six countries — but not equally so

**Journal:** *Psychology of Addictive Behaviors* | **Published:** 2026 Aug
**PMID:** 41989429 | **DOI:** 10.1037/adb0001153
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — Large multinational sample (N=2,886), clear societal hook around disordered eating and drinking culture among young adults, and counterintuitive country-level variation lift the score; psychometric caveats and scale-validation framing slightly limit impact.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers examined food-and-alcohol disturbance behaviours — such as skipping meals or exercising before drinking to enhance alcohol's effects or offset its calories — in 2,886 college students across six countries (US, Argentina, Spain, South Africa, England, Canada). Exercising to compensate for alcohol calories and restricting food to intensify intoxication were the most commonly endorsed behaviours in all countries. Endorsement rates were highest among participants from England and lowest in Argentina and Spain, and all behaviours were positively associated with alcohol-related harms across every country.

**Why it matters:** The study highlights that disordered eating linked to alcohol use is a cross-national phenomenon among young adults, pointing to a need for integrated screening that looks at both eating behaviour and drinking rather than treating them separately.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Skipping meals to get drunk faster is common worldwide — but English students do it most" Across six countries, one of the most common drinking strategies among college students is simply not eating beforehand — and researchers now have the data to prove it. A study of nearly 3,000 college students in six countries found that food-and-alcohol disturbance behaviours — restricting food to intensify a buzz, exercising to 'burn off' alcohol calories — are widespread everywhere, but especially in England. The finding challenges the idea that these are niche or culture-specific behaviours; they appear to be a global feature of student drinking culture. It also raises questions about whether clinicians should be screening for disordered eating when they ask about alcohol use. Why it fits: The intersection of disordered eating and alcohol culture is a topic NS Mind readers actively follow, and the cross-national data gives it real breadth.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** observational design, self-reported outcomes, psychometric limitations of the scale noted by authors. Self-reported survey data; the psychometric tool (CEBRACS) showed structural weaknesses in cross-national use, so country-level comparisons should be interpreted with caution; college students are not representative of the broader population

### 5. Using cannabis and tobacco one after the other drives up consumption of both — but smoking them together cuts cigarettes

**Journal:** *Addictive Behaviors* | **Published:** 2026 Aug
**PMID:** 41904833 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.addbeh.2026.108678
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — Counterintuitive finding (co-administering both drugs together is associated with fewer cigarettes, not more), clear real-world hook for quit attempts, and innovative real-time EMA methodology, but small EMA subsample and single-center design are notable limitations.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Using daily diaries and ecological momentary assessment (EMA), researchers tracked tobacco and cannabis use in real time among 110 daily cigarette smokers who also used cannabis, in the week before they attempted to quit tobacco. Co-use occurred on 76% of days and in 21% of sessions. Sequential use — consuming one substance then the other — was associated with smoking more cigarettes and more cannabis that day, whereas co-administration (using both simultaneously) was linked to greater cannabis use but actually fewer cigarettes.

**Why it matters:** For the growing number of people trying to quit tobacco while also using cannabis, the pattern of how they combine the two substances — simultaneously versus one after the other — may meaningfully affect how much they consume, with implications for how quit programmes are designed.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Smoking weed and tobacco together is linked to fewer cigarettes — doing them one after the other makes things worse" How you combine cannabis and tobacco turns out to matter enormously: lighting up both at once is associated with smoking fewer cigarettes, while doing them sequentially drives consumption of both up. A US study using real-time smartphone tracking found that daily tobacco smokers who also use cannabis co-used on three in every four days. The counterintuitive headline: when they used both substances simultaneously, they smoked fewer cigarettes than usual — yet using them one after the other sent cigarette and cannabis consumption higher. The finding could reshape how addiction specialists approach smoking cessation in people who also use cannabis, a group that is typically harder to treat. Why it fits: With cannabis legalisation spreading and tobacco quit rates stalling, the interaction between these two substances is directly relevant to millions of readers trying to understand — or change — their own behaviour.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** small sample (N=110 diaries; n=32 for EMA subsample), observational design, single-center, short follow-up. The real-time EMA subsample was only 32 people, making moment-to-moment findings preliminary; single-centre US study; observational design means the pattern of use could reflect pre-existing differences between people rather than the effect of the co-use style itself *Editor's fact-check note: The EMA subsample is n=32, not 110 — the summary correctly distinguishes between the diary sample (N=110) and the EMA subsample (n=32). Caveats updated to reflect the particularly small EMA subsample.*

The study suggests that culturally tailored harm-reduction tools may work better than one-size-fits-all approaches.

Both cultures share a sense of self-efficacy, but the routes to it are culturally distinct. The finding matters because most cannabis harm-reduction programmes are designed in and for the US — and may not translate. **Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers compared 60 Taiwanese adults diagnosed with gaming disorder (ICD-11 criteria) with 120 matched community controls, examining frustration intolerance, sensitivity to non-reward, and procrastination. All three traits were significantly elevated in the gaming disorder group and independently predicted a gaming disorder diagnosis. Within the gaming disorder group, core addictive symptoms (such as loss of control) were most strongly linked to emotional intolerance and poor time management, while related problems (such as impaired functioning) were most strongly tied to non-reward sensitivity and emotional intolerance.

**Why it matters:** The findings suggest that gaming disorder may be partly driven by difficulty tolerating frustration and disappointment in everyday life — not just within games — pointing toward emotion-regulation therapies as a potential treatment avenue.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Gaming disorder may be less about games and more about not being able to cope with frustration" People with gaming disorder are distinguished not by how much they love playing, but by an unusually low tolerance for frustration, disappointment, and the feeling that effort goes unrewarded. A Taiwanese study comparing 60 adults diagnosed with gaming disorder to 120 controls found that frustration intolerance, sensitivity to not being rewarded, and procrastination each independently predicted a gaming disorder diagnosis — and were all elevated in the clinical group. The implication is striking: the problem may not be gaming per se, but a broader difficulty tolerating life's frustrations, with games offering an escape. This reframes gaming disorder as a possible emotion-regulation problem rather than purely a behavioural addiction. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are fascinated by the psychology of compulsive behaviour and the ongoing debate about whether gaming disorder is a genuine addiction — this study adds a nuanced psychological dimension.

### 9. Rural young men who stay put are the group most likely to spend decades weakly connected to the labour market

**Journal:** *Longitudinal and Life Course Studies* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 30
**PMID:** 42538101 | **DOI:** 10.1332/17579597Y2026D000000086
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — Large longitudinal cohort with a counterintuitive gender finding, but the Sweden-specific welfare and labour market context significantly limits generalisability to a US/global NS Mind readership, and the topic sits at the edge of NS Mind's core areas (social behaviour rather than mental health/cognition).
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Using Swedish national register data for 42,913 people born between 1977 and 1982, researchers tracked labour market participation and geographic mobility across two decades of adulthood using sequence analysis and fuzzy clustering. They identified four distinct life-course trajectories: stayers with strong labour market attachment, stayers with weak attachment, movers to metropolitan areas, and movers to other rural or small-city contexts. A key finding was that men were overrepresented among the 'weakly attached stayers' — those who remained in rural areas but never established stable employment — while women more often pursued metropolitan mobility. Parental education largely erased these gender gaps.

**Why it matters:** The findings challenge the assumption that staying rural is uniformly disadvantageous, showing instead that outcomes depend heavily on gender and class background, with rural men from lower socioeconomic backgrounds facing the most persistent labour market marginalisation.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "It's rural men who stay home — not those who leave — who face the bleakest long-term job prospects" A 20-year tracking study of over 40,000 Swedes found that rural young men who never moved were the most likely to spend decades in precarious, unstable employment — while women who left for cities fared the best. Migration is routinely framed as the escape hatch from rural disadvantage, but new Swedish register data following 42,913 people over 20 years complicates that picture. It isn't simply leaving or staying that determines outcomes — it's the interaction of gender and class. Women from less-educated families were more likely to move to cities and build stable careers; men from similar backgrounds who stayed were the most vulnerable group of all, stuck in a pattern of weak labour market attachment that persisted well into their 30s and 40s. The study also overturns the idea that rural trajectories are set in early adulthood: many people renegotiated their paths much later than expected. Why it fits: The psychology of place identity, gender, and economic marginalisation overlaps with NS Mind's interest in social behaviour and wellbeing.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** observational design, single-country (Sweden) — findings tied to Swedish welfare state and labour market context. Sweden's specific welfare state, labour market structure, and generous parental leave policies mean findings may not transfer directly to the UK, US, or other countries; observational design cannot establish causation; gender effects may reflect structural factors specific to Nordic economies *Editor's fact-check note: Sample size N=42,913 confirmed in abstract. **PMID:** 41389081 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13691058.2025.2600046

**NS fit:** 4/10 — Important public health topic with a clear societal hook, but the finding is closely tied to a specific rural Ethiopian socio-religious and healthcare context unlikely to generalise to a US/global NS Mind readership, qualitative design has no quantitative effect size, and sample size is unspecified.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** A qualitative study in the Gurage and Hadiya zones of rural central Ethiopia used in-depth interviews, key informant interviews, and focus group discussions with adolescent girls (both vaccinated and unvaccinated), parents, public health workers, and community leaders to understand why HPV vaccine uptake remains low despite its inclusion in Ethiopia's national immunisation programme. Thematic analysis identified five key barriers: limited knowledge and awareness, cultural stigma around reproductive health, religious resistance to biomedical interventions, parental and community influence, and fear of side effects — particularly a widely held belief that the vaccine causes infertility. Parental and community influence could both facilitate and discourage vaccination depending on context.

Ethiopia has one of the world's highest cervical cancer death rates, and the HPV vaccine is now part of the national immunisation programme — yet uptake in rural areas remains stubbornly low. New research in the Gurage and Hadiya zones found that the infertility rumour was so entrenched it cut across religious lines, affecting even families who were not religiously opposed to vaccination in principle. The findings suggest that standard awareness campaigns are failing because they don't address the specific fears that are actually circulating — and that recruiting religious leaders and parents as trusted messengers is essential.
