### 1. National pride makes people in weak democracies blind to how bad their democracy actually is

**Journal:** *British Journal of Social Psychology* | **Published:** 2026 Jul
**PMID:** 42057255 | **DOI:** 10.1111/bjso.70084
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Large cross-national dataset, counterintuitive challenge to the positive framing of national pride, and a strong societal hook around democratic erosion; loses one point for observational/self-report design and one for limited direct mental health or brain science angle.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** A researcher at ELTE Eötvös Loránd University in Budapest analysed data from 156,658 participants across 92 countries drawn from the combined World Values Survey and European Values Study, using multilevel structural equation modelling. The study distinguished two facets of national identity — attachment (emotional bond) and pride (positive evaluation of the nation) — and tested how each related to people's perception of their country's democratic quality. National pride, but not national attachment, was more strongly associated with inflated perceptions of democratic quality in countries that objective indicators rated as less democratic or illiberal, suggesting pride functions as a cognitive blind spot that distorts political reality.

**Why it matters:** The finding suggests that national pride — often framed positively in psychology — may inadvertently shield authoritarian or backsliding governments from citizen scrutiny, with implications for how democracies erode from within.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "National pride makes people blind to how bad their democracy actually is" In countries where democracy is weakest, citizens tend to think it is working just fine — and a massive 92-country study suggests national pride is the reason why. Psychology has generally treated national pride as a healthy form of social identity. But a new analysis of over 156,000 people across 92 countries finds that pride — unlike simple emotional attachment to one's country — is associated with rosier assessments of democratic quality precisely where that quality is objectively poor. People in illiberal or backsliding states feel their democracy is doing better than external indicators suggest, and the stronger their national pride, the wider the gap. The finding reframes pride as a potential psychological mechanism that helps authoritarian systems survive citizen scrutiny. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are engaged by the psychology of political belief and motivated reasoning, and this study offers a striking, data-rich illustration of how a positive emotion can systematically distort perception of reality.

This is an observational, cross-sectional analysis using self-reported survey data, so causality cannot be established; cultural differences in how pride and democratic quality are understood may also vary across the 92 countries studied

**The study:** Researchers analysed data from a census-matched sample of 2,806 U.S. adults to examine whether religious identification and moral disapproval of pornography act as risk factors for problematic pornography use (PPU) or as protective resources. It is pornography frequency itself, not religiosity, that tracks with worse mental health outcomes. The finding reframes a culture-war narrative and raises serious questions about how selection bias has distorted a decade of research. But subtle, chronic everyday racism appears to do something more insidious: it is associated with higher internalized racism, a form of unconscious self-directed prejudice, which in turn predicts that parents will share less of their cultural heritage with their children. **Journal:** *Culture, Health & Sexuality* | **Published:** 2026 Jul
**PMID:** 41273241 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13691058.2025.2588786
**Novelty:** Included for relevance
**NS fit:** 4/10 — Theoretically interesting angle on identity fluidity in healthcare, but the very small sample (N=15), single-researcher qualitative design, and heavily theoretical framing make it a weak fit for NS Mind's evidence-focused readership.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** A qualitative study based on in-depth interviews with 15 US-based sexual-minority women aged 31 to 73 years examined how their sense of identity shifts across different healthcare encounters. Drawing on identitarian articulations theory, the researcher found that identifications are not fixed but are dynamically shaped by provider attitudes, physical spaces, medical protocols, and emotional atmospheres within clinical settings. The analysis highlights how non-human elements — such as room layouts and intake forms — can activate or suppress aspects of a patient's identity in ways that affect care quality. The author argues this relational framework offers new possibilities for transforming healthcare environments to better serve sexual-minority patients.

**Why it matters:** Sexual-minority women face documented disparities in healthcare access and outcomes, and this study suggests that the physical and social design of clinical environments plays an underappreciated role in shaping those disparities. Understanding identity as fluid rather than fixed could open new, environment-level approaches to reducing healthcare inequity.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "The shape of a waiting room can change how a queer woman experiences her identity" A healthcare waiting room's layout, an intake form's tick-boxes, even a provider's tone of voice can shift how a sexual-minority woman perceives her own identity — and that shift affects the care she receives. A new qualitative study interviewed 15 sexual-minority women about their healthcare experiences and found that their sense of who they are — lesbian, bisexual, queer — was not a fixed backdrop but something that flickered and shifted depending on where they were and who they were with. The physical layout of a clinic, a provider's body language, and even medical paperwork all appeared to amplify or suppress different aspects of women's identities. The researcher argues that fixing healthcare inequity for LGBTQ+ patients means redesigning environments, not just training individual clinicians. Why it fits: NS Mind readers interested in identity, social behaviour, and healthcare inequity will find the idea that environments reshape identity in real time genuinely surprising.
