### Study 1: Teenagers' hearts slow down when they scroll before bed — so screens may not be keeping them awake the way we think
**PMID:** 41556268 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

All core numbers check out: N=70, ages 11–14.9, 37% Māori, 42% female, four nights, Fitbit Inspire 2, 83 vs 93 BPM, −3 BPM for social media, +3 BPM for communication, sleep latency +9 min per 10 BPM. Journal, issue and epub date are correct.

**Issue A. Invented comparison in the pitch scene** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "and a pulse that is calmer than when she was doing homework"
- **Abstract says:** "Median heart rate was lower during screen use than during non-screen activities (83 BPM, [IQR: 77-91] vs. 93 [IQR: 87-100])."
- **Problem:** The contrast measured was screen use vs. all non-screen activity. Homework is never named, and much homework is itself screen-based, so the scene attributes a specific comparison to the study that it did not make.
- **Suggested fix:** "and a pulse that is calmer than during her non-screen evening activities"

**Issue B. NS fit score sits slightly high for the size of the claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — objective wearable-camera plus heart-rate data directly contradict the 'screens cause physiological arousal' story, though the sample is small."
- **Abstract says:** "differences were small and effects on sleep only modest"; N=70 over four nights, heart rate via consumer wearable.
- **Problem:** A "overturns prior research" claim resting on 70 adolescents, four nights, and Fitbit-derived heart rate is a large claim on modest evidence, and the authors themselves describe the differences as small.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10, with the fit line noting that the authors characterise the differences as small and the sleep effects as modest.

### Study 2: Unusual mental experiences come first, phone dependence follows — via daytime sleepiness
**PMID:** 42636649 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=6,153, 79.7% female, mean age 20.06, three waves at six-month intervals, CLPM vs RI-CLPM contrast, and the PLEs(T1)→EDS(T2)→PSU(T3) pathway are all reported accurately. Corresponding author and institution are correct.

**Issue A. "Overturns prior research" mislabels the novelty** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** "few studies have specifically investigated the bidirectional relationship between problematic smartphone use (PSU) and psychotic-like experiences (PLEs)"
- **Problem:** There was little prior work to overturn. What the study does is apply RI-CLPM and find no within-person support for bidirectionality — a methodological correction to an assumption, not a reversal of an established literature. The digest body ("the usual tangle of associations") implies a settled prior finding the abstract does not describe.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** New method applied to an under-tested question — first RI-CLPM test of the PSU–PLE relationship, which finds no within-person bidirectionality"

*Note: the wider-angle claim that "the switch from CLPM to RI-CLPM has been erasing 'bidirectional' screen findings across the field" is unverifiable from this abstract alone.*

### Study 3: Feeling short of time hollows out life's meaning — and the fix is what you aim for, not what you do
**PMID:** 42541341 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

All five sample sizes (54,568; 126,424; 383; 160; 396) match, as does the mediation via intrinsic goal aspirations, the null for extrinsic goals on meaning, and the Study 5 reflection task. Author affiliation correct.

**Issue A. Study 1's outcome was a proxy, not meaning in life** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "researchers linked the chronic feeling of 'time poverty' to lower meaning in life: archival data (N=54,568)"
- **Abstract says:** "Study 1 provided initial archival evidence linking perceived time poverty to a proxy for meaninglessness-related experience (N = 54,568)"
- **Problem:** The largest dataset in the package did not measure meaning in life; it used a proxy for meaninglessness-related experience. Presenting it as a direct link inflates the strength of the headline number.
- **Suggested fix:** "archival data linking time poverty to a proxy for meaninglessness-related experience (N=54,568)"

**Issue B. "The fix" overstates a single brief lab task** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "and the fix is what you aim for, not what you do"
- **Abstract says:** "Study 5 showed that enhancing intrinsic goal aspirations through a brief reflection task attenuated this negative effect (N = 396)."
- **Problem:** "Attenuated" in a short lab study with no follow-up is not "the fix." The subhead promises a remedy the data only gesture at.
- **Suggested fix:** "and a brief shift in what you aim for may soften it"

*Note: the caveat "samples are largely Chinese" is unverifiable from the abstract only (author affiliations are Chinese, but participant nationality is not stated).*

### Study 4: What you see in your feed pushes your body image around — but your body image barely steers what you look at
**PMID:** 42537570 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=858, 78.3% women, six prompts a day for seven days, and all four directional findings (thinspiration→dissatisfaction; fitspiration→lower appreciation; fatspiration→exercise urges; objectification→less thinspiration viewing) are reported correctly.

**Issue A. "No safer" is a comparison the study did not make** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "and 'fitspiration' looks no safer than thinspiration"
- **Abstract says:** "viewing thinspiration content predicted greater body dissatisfaction; viewing fitspiration content predicted lower body appreciation"
- **Problem:** The two content types predicted different outcomes; no head-to-head test of magnitude is reported, and the abstract gives no effect sizes. The pitch angle compounds this with causal wording ("why fitspiration *erodes* body appreciation") for momentary associational data.
- **Suggested fix:** "and fitspiration carries its own signal — lower body appreciation — rather than looking benign"; in the pitch, "why fitspiration tracks with lower body *appreciation*"

**Issue B. Gender moderation null stated more broadly than tested** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Gender did not moderate any of it."
- **Abstract says:** "Gender did not moderate inter-individual variation in these momentary relationships."
- **Problem:** The null applies specifically to between-person variation in momentary associations; the authors explicitly flag that gender differences may still appear in cumulative exposure or trait-level self-evaluations. The "Why it matters" line ("undercuts the framing of this as a girls' issue") leans on the broader reading.
- **Suggested fix:** "Gender did not moderate between-person variation in these momentary relationships — though the authors note it may still matter for cumulative exposure."

### Study 5: Watching yourself through the camera's eye may make you less able to feel hunger
**PMID:** 42537569 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N figures (T1 762; T2 353), one-month interval, women-identifying college students, half-longitudinal mediation model and the significant indirect effect are all accurate. Author and institution correct.

**Issue A. Headline names the wrong construct** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Watching yourself through the camera's eye may make you less able to feel hunger"
- **Abstract says:** "ASMC also predicted decreases in interoceptive sensibility, specifically a decreased acceptance of somatic sensations"
- **Problem:** The measured variable was self-reported *acceptance* of bodily sensations, not the ability to detect them, and nothing hunger-specific was measured. "Less able to feel hunger" implies an interoceptive accuracy deficit the study did not test. "Make you" also imports causation into a half-longitudinal observational design.
- **Suggested fix:** "Monitoring how you'd look to an online audience tracks with accepting your body's signals less — and eating less"

**Issue B. Null result on restricting omitted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "ASMC predicted rising body dissatisfaction but not binge eating or restraint."
- **Abstract says:** "ASMC significantly predicted increases in body dissatisfaction but did not predict changes in binge eating, cognitive restraint, or restricting."
- **Problem:** ASMC also failed to predict restricting directly; the link to restriction is only via the indirect path. Dropping "restricting" from the list of nulls lets a reader infer a direct effect that was not found.
- **Suggested fix:** "ASMC predicted rising body dissatisfaction but not binge eating, cognitive restraint or restricting directly."

### Study 6: People recovering from anorexia describe a phase of relentless hunger that clinical textbooks don't mention
**PMID:** 41983301 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

987 posts, r/AnorexiaRecovery, 25 July 2023–4 January 2024, 92 posts (9.3%), and all three themes are reported faithfully.

**Issue A. "Predictable, biologically plausible stage" goes beyond the data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A predictable, biologically plausible stage of recovery is going unexplained by clinicians"
- **Abstract says:** "Further research is needed to clarify its prevalence, underlying mechanisms, and clinical significance."
- **Problem:** The authors explicitly do not know how common extreme hunger is, why it occurs, or whether it is a normative phase. Describing it as a predictable stage with a known biological basis asserts exactly what the paper says is unresolved.
- **Suggested fix:** "An experience that recovering patients describe repeatedly has no established clinical account — its prevalence and mechanism are unknown — and the resulting fear appears to push some people back into restriction."

### Study 7: Ozempic has been absorbed into pro-eating-disorder Twitter as everyday, normalized talk
**PMID:** 42604953 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Netnography, "Ozempic" + "edtwt", August 2024–July 2025, 451 retrieved and 255 analysed, and every percentage (28.6, 19.2, 11.8, 11.4, 9.0, 3.5) matches the abstract. Author/institution correct.

**Issue A. Pitch reassigns a combined category to risk warnings alone** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The number to lead on is 3.5% — the share of posts containing any risk warning"
- **Abstract says:** "Information sharing and risk warnings accounted for 3.5% of posts."
- **Problem:** 3.5% is a combined category covering both information sharing *and* risk warnings; the risk-warning share alone is necessarily smaller and is not reported. The digest body states this correctly, but the pitch line does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "The number to lead on is 3.5% — the combined share of posts offering information or any risk warning"

### Study 8: Classmates can reliably name the school's "influencers" — and those teens are both kinder and more aggressive than their peers
**PMID:** 42552601 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=331, 7th and 8th graders, Midwestern school in a small community, peer-nomination measure, and the direction of the associations are all accurate. Author and institution correct.

**Issue A. "Reliably" overstates "moderate correspondence"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Classmates can reliably name the school's 'influencers'"
- **Abstract says:** "Peer-reported social media visibility showed moderate correspondence with various self-reports of social media use."
- **Problem:** Moderate correspondence with self-reported use is not "reliably." The digest body gets this right ("corresponded moderately"), so the headline is out of step with its own text.
- **Suggested fix:** "Classmates can broadly pick out the school's 'influencers'"

**Issue B. Causal verb and an aggression breakdown the abstract doesn't report** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "and predicted higher levels of prosocial behavior, higher physical, verbal and relational aggression, and more self-reported substance use"
- **Abstract says:** "It was also associated with higher levels of prosocial and aggressive behavior and substance use."
- **Problem:** Two things. "Predicted" implies temporal ordering in a cross-sectional design. And while physical, verbal and relational aggression were each *measured*, the abstract reports the association only for aggression in aggregate — separate significance for each subtype is unverifiable from the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "and was associated with higher prosocial behaviour, higher aggression, and more self-reported substance use"

**Issue C. "Most-followed kid" misdescribes the measure** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the most-followed kid in year 8"
- **Abstract says:** "Social media visibility was assessed via peer nominations"
- **Problem:** No follower counts were collected; the construct is peer-perceived visibility, which is not the same thing and is the whole methodological point of the paper.
- **Suggested fix:** "the classmate everyone names as the most visible online"

### Study 9: A substantial minority of people press "like" specifically to hurt someone
**PMID:** 42424914 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The four-study structure, the five-year archival trend, schadenfreude as the dominant stated motive, the correlation in Study 3, and the Study 4 finding about those bothered by receiving such likes are all accurately summarised. Author and institution correct.

**Issue A. Headline merges two different findings** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "A substantial minority of people press 'like' specifically to hurt someone"
- **Abstract says:** "Results indicate a substantial minority of subjects engage in 'liking' negative outcomes of opponents, with most indicating schadenfreude as primary motivation" and, separately, "a significant number of subjects indicate they engage in the behavior to hurt the other party."
- **Problem:** The "substantial minority" figure describes people who do counter-valenced liking at all, whose main stated motive was schadenfreude (enjoying the pain), not intent to inflict it. The "to hurt the other party" group is an unquantified subset from a different study. The headline welds the size of one group onto the motive of another.
- **Suggested fix:** "A substantial minority of people press 'like' on a rival's bad news — and some say they do it to hurt"

### Study 10: Adding a social network to an eating-disorder app didn't help teens — and unguided self-help worked about as well as coaching
**PMID:** 41992076 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=148, three arms, recruitment via social media and an online screen, follow-up to 6 months, the null on global ED psychopathology, d = −0.93, and the faster binge-eating reduction in the GSH arm are all correct. The declared consultancies (Rissana, PredictView, Woebot, Kooth, Google Health) are properly carried into the Caveats.

**Issue A. Headline's second clause sits awkwardly with the abstract's conclusion** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "and unguided self-help worked about as well as coaching"
- **Abstract says:** "with coaching possibly augmenting outcomes for some subgroups" and "the rate of reduction [in binge eating] was greatest in the GSH condition (p < 0.001)"
- **Problem:** True for the primary outcome, but the one significant differential effect favoured the coached arm. The headline states the equivalence without the qualifier the authors themselves attach.
- **Suggested fix:** "and unguided self-help matched coaching on the main outcome — though coaching led on binge eating"

**Issue B. "The simpler product" is ambiguous and points the wrong way** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this pilot finds no benefit, and hints the simpler product may be better for binge eating"
- **Abstract says:** "the rate of reduction was greatest in the GSH condition"
- **Problem:** The best-performing arm for binge eating was guided self-help — the arm *with* a human coach. A reader will most naturally read "the simpler product" as the plain unguided app, which was not the winner.
- **Suggested fix:** "this pilot finds no benefit from the social feature, and hints that the coached version without a social network did best on binge eating"

### Study 11: Four in five adolescents who self-harm or restrict say they sometimes do it to feel *worse*
**PMID:** 42010304 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=137, mean age 15.10, 75.18% female, online recruitment via social media from a larger longitudinal study, 81% endorsing at least one contra-hedonic function, and the NSSI/self-punishment findings are all accurately reported. Corresponding author at Yale is correct.

**Issue A. "Instead" turns an addition into a replacement** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The field has assumed these behaviors are pro-hedonic — done to reduce negative feeling. Instead, 81% endorsed at least one *contra*-hedonic function"
- **Abstract says:** "adolescents may not only engage in these behaviors to feel better, but also to make themselves feel worse"
- **Problem:** Pro-hedonic functions were also assessed and are not disconfirmed. The finding is that contra-hedonic motives coexist, not that they displace pro-hedonic ones — and the 81% figure is "at least one," not "primarily." The "Overturns prior research" label and the "targeting the wrong function" line in Why it matters both build on the exclusive reading.
- **Suggested fix:** "The field has treated these behaviours as pro-hedonic — done to reduce negative feeling. In addition to that, 81% endorsed at least one *contra*-hedonic function..." and relabel novelty as "Challenges a prevailing assumption."

### Study 12: Identifying with an online group makes you more likely to start measuring yourself against others
**PMID:** 42546360 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Country-by-country Ns (1541/1561/1529/1112/1533), age range 18–75, within- and between-person effects, and partial mediation by self-verification are all accurate.

**Issue A. Causal headline for observational panel data** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Identifying with an online group makes you more likely to start measuring yourself against others"
- **Abstract says:** "Online social identification might make us more susceptible to ability comparisons" (title) and "group identification was associated with positive within-person and between-person changes in ability comparison orientation"
- **Problem:** "Makes you" is a causal claim from correlational panel data; the authors hedge with "might" in their own title. The digest's own Caveats say the design "cannot establish causation," so the headline contradicts the caveat. "Start measuring yourself" also implies onset, whereas the outcome is a continuous comparison-orientation score.
- **Suggested fix:** "Identifying with an online group goes with a stronger pull to measure yourself against others"

### Study 13: The strongest predictor of compulsive short-video use isn't the pull of the videos — it's overestimating your own self-control
**PMID:** 42608804 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=842, PLS-SEM, overestimation of self-control as the strongest driver exceeding the total effect of flow, and flow's indirect route via that overestimation are all reported accurately. Author and institution correct.

**Issue A. Flow's dimensions are mislabelled** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "treating 'flow' not as the classic optimal experience but as a passive state with maladaptive components (affective reliance, loss of self-reflection, telepresence)"
- **Abstract says:** "flow is reconceptualized as a passive psychological state comprising both immersive and maladaptive dimensions (i.e., concentration, enjoyment, telepresence, heightened arousal, affective reliance, and loss of self-reflection)" and "concentration, telepresence, and affective reliance significantly intensifying compulsive use and/or overestimation of self-control"
- **Problem:** Telepresence is listed among the six dimensions without being designated maladaptive, and loss of self-reflection — which the digest lists as a maladaptive component — was *not* among the dimensions that significantly intensified compulsive use. Concentration, which was significant, is omitted.
- **Suggested fix:** "treating 'flow' as a passive state with six dimensions, both immersive and maladaptive; concentration, telepresence and affective reliance were the ones that significantly intensified compulsive use or self-control overconfidence"

### Study 14: Short-form video use predicts disordered eating four months later — in working nurses, not teenagers
**PMID:** 41968956 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=400 at baseline, 270 at four-month follow-up (32.5% attrition), single Wuhan hospital, female registered nurses, indirect effect via body dissatisfaction, moderation by perceived media pressure and thin-ideal internalisation — all accurate. Authors and institution correct.

**Issue A. "Increased body dissatisfaction" implies change that was not measured** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Baseline short-form video use predicted disordered eating at follow-up indirectly, through increased body dissatisfaction."
- **Abstract says:** "Measures assessed short-form video use and sociocultural attitudes at T1 and body dissatisfaction and disordered eating at T2."
- **Problem:** Body dissatisfaction was only measured at follow-up, so there is no baseline against which an increase could be observed, and mediator and outcome were assessed simultaneously. "Increased" implies a within-person change the two-wave design cannot show. Worth adding to Caveats, which currently only says "two waves."
- **Suggested fix:** "Baseline short-form video use predicted disordered eating at follow-up indirectly, via body dissatisfaction measured at the same follow-up point."

*Note: "shift-working" (Why it matters) and "after a night shift" (pitch) are plausible but unverifiable from the abstract, which reports no shift-work data.*

**Issue A. "The only predictor" is an inference, not a stated result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "identified *initiatory* self-control ... as the only predictor credibly associated with all four outcomes"
- **Abstract says:** "initiatory self-control was consistently identified as a relevant predictor and showed 95% Bayesian credibility intervals not including zero across all outcomes"
- **Problem:** The abstract says initiatory self-control was credible across all outcomes; it never says it was the *only* variable to achieve this. The other predictors listed happen to span fewer outcomes, but exclusivity isn't asserted and can't be confirmed from the abstract alone.
- **Suggested fix:** "identified *initiatory* self-control ... as the one process credibly associated with all four outcomes in the reported results"

### Study 18: Indigenous Wayuu people describe globalized beauty ideals arriving with the internet — and cultural pride pushing back
**PMID:** 42520348 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Five focus groups, 29 participants, ages 18–68, 23 women and 6 men, reflexive thematic analysis, all three themes, and the buffering role of cultural pride are accurately reported. Authors and institutions correct.

**Issue A. "Natural experiment" mischaracterises the design** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 5/10 — a rare natural experiment in real time on a badly underrepresented population" (echoed in the pitch: "we are watching a decades-old natural experiment run again")
- **Abstract says:** "Five focus groups of up to 9 participants were conducted with 29 Wayuu participants ... Using reflexive thematic analysis, three overarching themes were identified"
- **Problem:** This is a qualitative interview study collecting retrospective accounts, with no pre/post measurement, comparison community or exposure variation. Calling it a natural experiment implies quasi-experimental leverage the design does not have, and could seed a pitch built on that misreading.
- **Suggested fix:** "a rare qualitative window onto globalised appearance ideals arriving in real time, in a badly underrepresented population"

### Study 20: Vivid "what if" thoughts after sexual assault track with worse trauma symptoms months later
**PMID:** 42635305 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=452 (437 women, 15 men, mean age 26.2), follow-up Ns of 216 and 232, dual recruitment through Sexual Assault Care Centres and social media, latent growth curve models, and the launch-but-not-slope finding are all accurate. Author and institution correct.

**Issue A. Frequency is written off more firmly than the results allow** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Vividness — not frequency — was tied to time-specific spikes in symptoms" and, in the pitch, "thinking about it more isn't the problem; seeing it clearly is."
- **Abstract says:** "Higher baseline frequency and vividness of CFT were associated with higher PTSR levels at the first assessment ... Vividness of CFT showed time specific associations with elevated PTSR, whereas frequency effects were less consistent."
- **Problem:** Frequency *was* associated with persistently higher symptom levels; it was only the time-specific (snare) effects where frequency was "less consistent" — which is not the same as null. The pitch line states outright that frequency isn't the problem, which contradicts the launch-effect result and the digest's own preceding sentence.
- **Suggested fix:** Body: "Vividness showed time-specific associations with symptom spikes, where frequency effects were less consistent." Pitch: "how often the thoughts come matters — but how vividly they play out seems to matter in a different, time-specific way."

## Editor's note on patterns

Three recurring problems are worth fixing systematically rather than entry by entry:

**Headlines drifting past their own body text.** Studies 8, 12 and 23 all contain accurate body copy and accurate caveats, then a headline that contradicts them. Study 23 is the sharpest case: the abstract says the reductions "cannot be firmly attributed to the interventions," the digest's caveat repeats that verbatim in substance, and the headline says the exercise "reduced trauma symptoms." Headlines are the part of a digest that survives into a pitch email, so they need the same causal discipline as the body.

**Composite or combined statistics split apart.** Studies 7 and 9 both take a figure describing one thing and attach it to a narrower, more striking thing — 3.5% for "information sharing and risk warnings" becomes 3.5% for risk warnings; "a substantial minority" who like rival misfortune becomes "a substantial minority" who do it to hurt. Both would be embarrassing to have quoted back by a source.

**NS fit scores.** Only Study 1 needs a downward adjustment on my read (7→6). The remaining 7s — Studies 2, 3 and 4 — are defensible: Study 2 has N=6,153 across three waves, Study 3 has five studies including two experiments, Study 4 has 858 participants under intensive momentary sampling. Their weaknesses are self-report rather than sample size, and the digest names that weakness in each fit line. The scores of 6 and below are all appropriately calibrated, and Studies 16, 17, 19 and 22 in particular are scored honestly against thin or preliminary evidence.

**Items needing full-text or external verification before pitching:** the "samples are largely Chinese" caveat on Study 3; the shift-work framing on Study 14; the cumulative-neurological-effects angle on Study 21; and the field-wide claim in Study 2's wider angle that RI-CLPM has been "erasing 'bidirectional' screen findings across the field."
