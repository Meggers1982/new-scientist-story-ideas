### 1. Most children who feel lonely at 10 are still lonely at 14

**Journal:** *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 25 May 2026)
**PMID:** 42186177 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jcpp.70178
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — huge pre-existing cohort, prospective design and a genuinely sobering headline number; limited by the fact that the brain findings are the weakest part.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers screened 347 environmental exposures, 61 health indicators and 558 MRI measures (gray matter volume, white matter microstructure, resting-state connectivity) in 9,602 children from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study, all aged 9–10 at baseline, and tracked loneliness reports to age 14. Of the 12% who reported loneliness at baseline, 71.6% reported it again over the next three years. The strongest correlates were not neural but domestic: parental psychopathology, developmental history and family income, with general mental health the single largest health effect; MRI features that discriminated lonely from non-lonely children clustered in socioemotional processing systems.

**Why it matters:** Childhood loneliness is usually framed as a passing phase, and this is the largest prospective test of that assumption — it suggests loneliness at 10 is a fairly durable state, and that the measurable risk factors sit in the family and neighborhood rather than in the scanner.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the number — seven in ten lonely 10-year-olds are still lonely three years later. Open on the scale of the screen (nearly a thousand variables, nearly 10,000 children) and the slightly deflating result that the brain scans added less than the household income question did. Worth calling Tsz Yeung Wong at the Education University of Hong Kong, and Ruben Gur's group at Penn for the imaging side.
- **Wider angle:** A longer feature for an education or policy outlet on whether "loneliness screening" in schools is a good idea at all — this dataset identifies persistent loneliness but offers no validated intervention, and the risk factors it flags (parental mental illness, poverty) are ones schools can detect without asking children to self-label.

**Caveats:** Observational — cannot establish causation; self-reported (and in part caregiver-reported) outcomes; the neuroimaging component is a discriminant-analysis classification rather than a tested mechanism; ABCD is US-based and 55% White, so generalization is limited.

### 2. Brain scans during a failed night's sleep show what insomnia actually looks like

**Journal:** *Journal of Sleep Research* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 15 July 2026)
**PMID:** 42458639 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jsr.70397
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — scanning people while they try and fail to fall asleep is a vivid, highly relatable first; badly limited by 11 patients.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Eleven people with insomnia disorder and 17 good sleepers underwent arterial spin labelling MRI — which measures blood flow without a tracer — twice in the same night: once awake at their habitual bedtime, and once during an actual sleep attempt, with polysomnography confirming sleep state. People with insomnia showed higher relative perfusion in posterior sensory, affective and self-referential regions, and lower perfusion in frontal cortex and basal ganglia. Strikingly, insomnia participants who did manage to fall asleep showed *lower* frontal and basal ganglia perfusion than those who stayed awake — the opposite of the pattern in controls.

**Why it matters:** It is direct physiological evidence for the long-argued "hyperarousal" account of insomnia, and the inverted sleep-state pattern hints that the brain's switch into sleep is wired differently in insomnia rather than simply running late. With 28 people total, though, this is a proof-of-concept that opens a line of enquiry rather than settling one.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the experimental scene — someone lying in an MRI scanner at their usual bedtime, instructed to try to sleep, while the machine tracks blood flowing to the parts of their brain that will not stop thinking about themselves. The hook is the reversal: when insomniacs do fall asleep, their frontal control regions go quieter than when they are awake, while good sleepers' go busier. Call Daniel B. Kay at Brigham Young University, and Lars Michels in Zurich.
- **Wider angle:** A specialist piece for a clinical or sleep-medicine readership on whether nighttime imaging can finally separate "paradoxical insomnia" — people who sleep by polysomnography but experience wakefulness — from objective short sleep, and what that would mean for how insomnia is classified.

**Caveats:** Very small N (11 vs 17); single-center; relative rather than absolute perfusion maps; exploratory cluster-corrected voxelwise analysis with a lenient voxel threshold; cross-sectional.

### 3. Lab experiments missed a brain network that decides where you look

**Journal:** *eNeuro* | **Published:** 22 September 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42772940 | **DOI:** 10.1523/ENEURO.0203-26.2026
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a neat demonstration that decades of tidy attention experiments overlooked something, with a successful replication built in; limited by a modest sample and correlational pattern analysis.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Participants watched a movie in an fMRI scanner while their eye fixations were tracked. Comparing how fixation patterns and brain activation patterns shifted between scenes reproduced the classic dorsal and ventral attention networks — but the differences *between individuals* in where they chose to look were mirrored instead by activity in orbitofrontal and anterior temporal cortex, regions not previously implicated in human eye-movement control. The effect was strongest for scenes containing faces.

**Why it matters:** Attention research has been built on abstract stimuli and explicit instructions, and this suggests that design systematically hid the machinery behind free, socially motivated gaze choices — the "where shall I look" decisions that make up almost all real-world looking.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Sell the methodological twist: the same study both replicates the textbook attention networks and shows they are not what makes your gaze differ from the person next to you in the cinema. Open on two people watching the same scene and looking at completely different things. Call Nico Marek or Stefan Pollmann at Otto-von-Guericke University Magdeburg.
- **Wider angle:** A trend feature for a neuroscience or technology outlet on the broader shift to "naturalistic neuroimaging" — movies, conversations, video games in the scanner — and what established findings are now being quietly revised as a result.

**Caveats:** Sample size not reported in the abstract and likely small; correlational representational-similarity approach cannot establish that the orbitofrontal–anterior temporal network causes gaze decisions; single-center; one movie stimulus.

### 4. Impulsive suicidal thoughts leave a different mark on the brain than planned ones

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 15 December 2026 (online 8 September 2026)
**PMID:** 42710718 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122482
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — resolves a real puzzle in the ENIGMA literature with a decent clinical sample; limited by a single-item measure of ideation subtype.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Large consortium studies had found reduced thalamus and pallidum volumes in people who had attempted suicide, but nothing comparable for suicidal ideation — an awkward gap, since ideation precedes attempt. Analyzing structural MRI from 263 people with major depressive disorder, the authors split ideation into "hot" (affectively driven, unplanned) and "cold" (deliberate, planned) using the MADRS suicide item. Reduced thalamo-pallidal volume was associated with hot ideation only, and survived adjustment for depression severity, history of attempt and dorsolateral prefrontal volume.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the missing brain signature for suicidal thinking was hidden by lumping two very different mental states together, and gives researchers a concrete reason to stop treating ideation as one thing. It is an association in a depressed sample, not a predictive test.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is a measurement failure, not a brain discovery: a decade of imaging found nothing for suicidal ideation because "thinking about suicide" was treated as a single variable. Open on the hot/cold distinction — the difference between a sudden overwhelming urge and a slowly assembled plan — and what emotional-reactivity hubs like the thalamus and pallidum are thought to do. Call Armand Chatard at the Université de Poitiers. Avoid any suggestion that a scan could identify risk in an individual.
- **Wider angle:** A measurement-science feature for a psychiatry or health-policy outlet on how much of the field's biomarker disappointment traces back to coarse symptom scales — the suicide item of the MADRS was never designed to carve a phenotype, yet subtype-blind pooling is standard across consortium neuroimaging.

**Caveats:** Observational, cross-sectional; ideation subtype derived from a single rating-scale item; no healthy control comparison; single-site clinical sample of people with MDD, so may not generalize; sensitive subject requiring careful framing.

### 5. Catatonia may be the ancient freeze response jammed on

**Journal:** *Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology & Biological Psychiatry* | **Published:** 30 September 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42815707 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pnpbp.2026.111953
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a strong, testable idea with real explanatory reach, but it is a review-and-synthesis rather than new data.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** The authors systematically cross-referenced human neuroimaging of normal threat-induced freezing against imaging of catatonia across its many causes. Both literatures implicate the same three things: failure of orbitofrontal and medial prefrontal cortex, salience network dysregulation, and a basal ganglia–midbrain motor block. From this they build the "freeze-arrest model of catatonia", with three entry points — overwhelming emotion, toxic-metabolic network disruption, and subcortical short-circuiting — and argue that neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental catatonia is a mechanistically separate category.

**Why it matters:** Catatonia is one of psychiatry's strangest and most poorly defined syndromes, and framing akinetic catatonia as a maladaptive version of the freeze-to-tonic-immobility response conserved across vertebrates gives clinicians a mechanistic reason for why the presentations and treatment responses differ so much.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the evolutionary image — the same circuitry that freezes a rabbit in the open may, in humans, lock a person into immobility for days. Open on the clinical picture of akinetic catatonia and the puzzle of why a benzodiazepine can release it within minutes. Call Anthony Chabbouh at the Lebanese University / American University of Beirut and ask what observation would falsify the model.
- **Wider angle:** A history-of-medicine feature for a general-interest magazine on catatonia's disappearance and return — once thought a form of schizophrenia, now recognized across autism, encephalitis, mood disorders and critical illness — and what it says about how diagnostic categories are made and unmade.

**Caveats:** Narrative/critical review, not primary research; no quantitative meta-analysis; the model is explicitly untested; underlying imaging studies of catatonia are mostly small and heterogeneous.

### 6. Feeling rejected makes you lonely through a specific psychological step — and it looks different in older brains

**Journal:** *Cognitive, Affective & Behavioral Neuroscience* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 6 May 2026)
**PMID:** 42091836 | **DOI:** 10.3758/s13415-026-01454-w
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — decent sample spanning two age groups and open data, but the headline neural findings are exploratory and the design cannot order the causal chain.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** 249 people — 141 aged 18–34 and 108 aged 60–89 — completed measures of perceived rejection, withdrawal-related threat sensitivity and loneliness, and had structural MRI. Threat sensitivity partially accounted for the link between perceived rejection and loneliness in both age groups, but older adults reported lower average threat sensitivity *and* lower loneliness than the young. Exploratory analyses linked gray matter volume in self-referential and social-perceptual regions (left angular gyrus, precuneus, subparietal sulcus, planum temporale, right superior temporal sulcus) to this pathway, with precuneus and planum temporale effects appearing only in the younger group.

**Why it matters:** It pushes against the cliché of lonely old age: here the older adults were less socially threat-sensitive and less lonely than the under-35s, and the brain structure associated with the rejection-to-loneliness route was detectable mainly in the young.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the age inversion — the people reporting most loneliness were the 18-to-34s, not the 60-to-89s — and the proposed mechanism: rejection breeds loneliness by ratcheting up a habit of withdrawal, not directly. Open on an ambiguous social snub and the two ways a 25-year-old and a 70-year-old might read it. Call Lulu Liu at the University of Macau.
- **Wider angle:** A reanalysis-culture story for a research-practice or data-science outlet: this paper is built from an openly shared OSF/OpenNeuro dataset collected for an entirely different purpose ("Goal-Directed Cognition in Older and Younger Adults"). A good peg for a piece on what open neuroimaging archives are actually producing — and the multiple-comparisons hazards that come with them.

**Caveats:** Observational and cross-sectional — mediation here cannot establish causal order; neuroimaging analyses explicitly exploratory; all psychological measures self-reported; secondary analysis of an existing dataset; age differences confounded with cohort effects.

### 7. People who fear punishment are better at waiting for a reward

**Journal:** *Behavioural Brain Research* | **Published:** 26 September 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42800541 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bbr.2026.116502
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a tidy, surprising behavioral result with a structural and functional correlate; limited by unreported sample size and a statistically delicate mediation.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Combining behavioral measures, voxel-based morphometry and resting-state fMRI, researchers found that punishment sensitivity — the tendency to notice and react to negative outcomes — was *negatively* correlated with delay discounting, meaning more punishment-sensitive people devalued future rewards less. Gray matter volume in left dorsomedial prefrontal cortex tracked punishment sensitivity, as did its resting connectivity with right postcentral gyrus, and that connectivity partially mediated the behavioral link (with a suppressor effect).

**Why it matters:** Sensitivity to punishment is usually filed under anxiety-adjacent liabilities, so finding that it travels with greater patience for delayed rewards complicates the standard reward-and-punishment framing of self-control. Modest and correlational, but a genuinely counterintuitive peg.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook writes itself: the trait that makes you flinch at bad outcomes may be the same one that stops you taking the smaller-sooner payoff. Open on the classic choice — £50 now or £80 in a month — and who actually waits. Call Pan Feng or Tingyong Feng at Southwest University, Chongqing, and press on the suppressor effect, which is a sign the model is doing something subtle.
- **Wider angle:** A behavioral-economics feature for a business or psychology outlet on how personality fits into discounting models — pension saving, climate behavior and debt are all framed through impatience, yet this suggests fear of loss, not optimism about the future, may be what buys patience.

**Caveats:** Sample size not stated in the abstract; cross-sectional and correlational; self-reported trait measure of punishment sensitivity; resting-state connectivity–behavior correlations of this kind are known to require large samples for stability; single-center student-aged population likely.

### 8. Your sense of numbers runs through the part of the brain that moves your body

**Journal:** *Biological Psychology* | **Published:** 20 September 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42764054 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.biopsycho.2026.109388
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a clean behavior–brain dissociation that bears on the embodied-cognition debate; narrow topic and likely small sample hold it back.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Participants performed parity judgments (odd/even) and magnitude classifications in an fMRI scanner while researchers applied multivariate pattern analysis. Cardinal (how many) and ordinal (what position) number semantics could be decoded across tasks within sensorimotor cortex, implying shared neural substrates. But there was a dissociation: the classic SNARC effect — small numbers answered faster on the left, large on the right — showed up behaviorally only in the implicit parity task, while sensorimotor decoding was strongest in the explicit magnitude task.

**Why it matters:** The "mental number line" is usually described as an abstract spatial representation; this points instead to numerical evaluation being functionally coupled to motor preparation, and shows the famous behavioral signature of the number line and its neural correlate do not appear in the same conditions.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the strange, reproducible fact that your left hand is faster for small numbers and your right for large ones, then deliver the twist: the brain signature of that spatial mapping shows up when the effect is behaviorally absent. Good vehicle for explaining embodied cognition to readers who think arithmetic happens in some abstract symbol-processing module. Call Zhiwei Zhang at Guizhou Normal University.
- **Wider angle:** An education-focused piece for a specialist teaching or child-development outlet on finger counting and gesture in early math learning — if number sense is wired into action systems, the pedagogical arguments about whether to discourage finger counting look different.

**Caveats:** Sample size not reported in the abstract; single-center; decoding results are correlational and cannot show sensorimotor cortex is necessary for numerical semantics; the behavior–brain dissociation is itself unexplained and needs replication.

### 9. For Māori, the brain is sacred — and MRI practice has no rules for that

**Journal:** *Brain Impairment* | **Published:** 24 September 2026
**PMID:** 42503436 | **DOI:** 10.1071/IB25071
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a genuinely first-of-its-kind look at whose values are built into neuroimaging protocols; three participants means it carries no statistical weight at all.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using a Māori-centred approach, researchers conducted semi-structured interviews (uiuinga) with three Māori men aged 20, 21 and 46 who had previously taken part in an EEG session, co-led by Māori and non-Māori researchers. Reflexive thematic analysis identified needs around relational engagement, culturally grounded communication for shared decision-making, and integration of Māori values to support whānau (family) confidence and agency. A second collective analysis stage involving participants, te reo Māori and tikanga experts fed into co-designing the first draft neuroimaging guidelines for Aotearoa New Zealand.

**Why it matters:** In te ao Māori the head, and the roro (brain) in particular, is the most tapu part of the body — yet there are no national guidelines for conducting neuroimaging accordingly. This is a concrete example of how a technology presented as culturally neutral carries assumptions that affect who consents to be scanned, and therefore who is represented in brain datasets.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open with the collision: a procedure that routinely involves touching, gelling and electrode-capping the head, performed in a country where the head is the most sacred part of the body, with no agreed protocol. Then the constructive turn — participants, language experts and researchers co-writing guidelines. Call Ebony Komene at the University of Auckland or Hinemoa Elder at Te Whare Wānanga o Awanuiārangi.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a global-health or science-culture outlet on the representativeness crisis in brain imaging: datasets that underpin "the human brain" are overwhelmingly drawn from a narrow slice of humanity, and cultural barriers to participation are a rarely named reason why. Indigenous data sovereignty initiatives in New Zealand, Canada and Australia give it reach.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study with three participants, all male; findings are not generalizable in a statistical sense and are not intended to be; proposed guideline is explicitly described by the authors as an iterative starting point.

### 10. In gaming disorder, the brain's reward circuit reacts oddly to rewards that have nothing to do with games

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychiatric Research* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 22 June 2026)
**PMID:** 42335494 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2026.06.027
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — the intrinsic-reward angle is the interesting bit, but 44 participants and a complex connectivity analysis make it fragile.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** 21 people meeting criteria for internet gaming disorder and 23 recreational gamers viewed intrinsic reward, extrinsic reward, neutral and gaming cues during fMRI. The gaming disorder group responded faster to gaming cues and reported stronger craving, as expected — but also evaluated intrinsic rewards differently and showed excessive striatum–thalamus synchronization (notably left thalamus to right caudate) specifically while processing intrinsic rewards. Under gaming cues, they showed stronger coupling between the default mode network and reward hubs such as the nucleus accumbens–prefrontal pathway.

**Why it matters:** Most addiction imaging looks at how the brain responds to the addictive cue itself; the suggestion here is that gaming disorder also distorts how non-gaming intrinsic rewards — curiosity, mastery, enjoyment for its own sake — are processed. That is a different and more interesting claim, if it replicates.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the counterintuitive bit: the differences were not only about games. Open on the recreational-gamer comparison group, which is the study's real strength — these are not gamers versus non-gamers but heavy gamers versus people with impaired control. Call Guang-Heng Dong at Yunnan Normal University, who has spent a decade on this question.
- **Wider angle:** A critical examination for a technology or games-industry outlet of whether "internet gaming disorder" is a coherent diagnosis at all — it sits in the ICD-11 and the DSM-5 research appendix, and the underlying neuroimaging base consists largely of studies with a few dozen participants. Good place to interview sceptics as well as proponents.

**Caveats:** Small N (21 vs 23); cross-sectional, so cannot tell whether altered reward processing precedes or follows heavy gaming; seed-based connectivity with multiple conditions invites false positives; single-center, likely young adult Chinese sample; the proposed "self-efficacy-sensory drive" model is speculative.

### 11. Depressed people who are sleepy by day lose pleasure in physical things

**Journal:** *Depression and Anxiety* | **Published:** 15 September 2026
**PMID:** 42746369 | **DOI:** 10.1155/da/9599586
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a specific and testable symptom link with objective sleep recording, but the sample is small and the mediation claim outruns the design.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** 22 patients with major depression and daytime sleepiness (Epworth score ≥7) were compared with 45 depressed patients without it, all undergoing resting-state fMRI, polysomnography and symptom scales. The two groups were matched on depression and anxiety severity *and* on objective sleep parameters, yet the sleepy group scored higher specifically on physical anhedonia — not social or anticipatory anhedonia. They also showed reduced connectivity between default mode and frontoparietal networks (ventrolateral prefrontal cortex to posterior cingulate and precuneus) and widely reduced global and local network efficiency; the vlPFC–PCC link statistically mediated the sleepiness–anhedonia association.

**Why it matters:** Daytime sleepiness in depression is usually explained away as poor sleep, but here the polysomnography was equivalent between groups — suggesting sleepiness is tracking something about brain network organization and the capacity to enjoy physical sensation, rather than simply sleep quantity.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the clinical puzzle: two patients with identical depression scores and identical sleep studies, one of whom cannot stay awake through the afternoon and has stopped enjoying food, warmth and touch. The hook is the dissociation between objective sleep and subjective sleepiness. Call Dao-Min Zhu at Anhui Mental Health Center and ask whether the effect survives in a larger sample.
- **Wider angle:** A piece for a clinical or neurology-focused outlet on anhedonia as a measurement problem — physical, social and anticipatory pleasure are routinely bundled into one depression item, and studies like this suggest they come apart and point to different circuits.

**Caveats:** Small N (22 vs 45) and unbalanced groups; cross-sectional, so mediation analysis cannot establish a causal sequence; patients were medicated (covariate-adjusted only); anhedonia and sleepiness both self-reported; single-center.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- *Loneliness keeps coming back as a neuro-target, and keeps resisting.* The previous digest's transdiagnostic marker — reduced dorsomedial prefrontal connectivity tracking social dysfunction across schizophrenia, depression and Alzheimer's ("A brain marker for loneliness shows up across depression, schizophrenia and Alzheimer's", PMID 42044556) — now sits alongside two further loneliness papers: "Most children who feel lonely at 10 are still lonely at 14" (PMID 42186177) and "Feeling rejected makes you lonely through a specific psychological step" (PMID 42091836). Both locate the action in self-referential and social-perceptual cortex, consistent with the dmPFC result.
- *Behavioral addiction imaging continues to drift off the dopamine-reward script.* "In gaming disorder, the brain's reward circuit reacts oddly to rewards that have nothing to do with games" (PMID 42335494) extends last run's two entries from the same literature — the cerebellum-as-top-discriminator classifier (PMID 42727812, same senior author, Guang-Heng Dong) and the digital-addiction meta-analysis implicating serotonergic and GABAergic systems rather than dopamine (PMID 42190596).
- *Symptom lumping is the recurring explanation for null imaging results.* "Impulsive suicidal thoughts leave a different mark on the brain than planned ones" (PMID 42710718) and "Depressed people who are sleepy by day lose pleasure in physical things" (PMID 42746369) both find signal only after splitting a standard symptom construct — exactly the move that separated diminished motivation from diminished expressivity in last run's schizophrenia covariance study (PMID 42176687).
- *Small-N, single-center positives still dominate the softer end.* The insomnia perfusion study (11 vs 17, PMID 42458639), the gaming-disorder scan (21 vs 23, PMID 42335494) and the depression-sleepiness study (22 vs 45, PMID 42746369) sit in the same fragility bracket as last run's trust game (N=31, PMID 42341477) and NODDI sleep apnea groups (n≈24, PMID 42687385).

**Advances**
- Sleep imaging moves from daytime structure to the night itself: last run's NODDI grey-matter study of obstructive sleep apnea (PMID 42687385) scanned awake patients, whereas "Brain scans during a failed night's sleep show what insomnia actually looks like" (PMID 42458639) images people mid-attempt at sleep with polysomnography confirming state — a genuine methodological step, on a much smaller sample.
- Last run's heritage-language work used a naturalistic story stimulus to escape the lab paradigm (PMID 42667920); "Lab experiments missed a brain network that decides where you look" (PMID 42772940) pushes that further by showing a free-viewing movie design recovers the textbook attention networks *and* reveals an orbitofrontal–anterior temporal system that instructed-cue experiments never saw.

**Counterpoints**
- The sharpest tension in the two digests is about how much the scanner adds. Last run's dmPFC loneliness marker (PMID 42044556) was framed as a transdiagnostic neural correlate worth chasing; this run's ABCD screen of 558 MRI measures against 347 exposures (PMID 42186177) finds parental psychopathology and family income outperform anything in the imaging data. Read together, they ask whether the loneliness biomarker literature is measuring the household.
- Age framing also flips: nothing in the previous digest contradicted the standard lonely-old-age picture, but PMID 42091836 reports lower threat sensitivity *and* lower loneliness in the 60–89 group than the 18–34 group.

**New themes**
- *Whose values are in the protocol.* "For Māori, the brain is sacred — and MRI practice has no rules for that" (PMID 42503436) reframes last run's "imaging leaving the undergraduate pool" thread (coal miners, organ donors, bilingual children) as a question about consent, tapu and data sovereignty rather than recruitment reach.
- *Evolutionary and embodied explanations.* The freeze-arrest model of catatonia (PMID 42815707), numerical semantics decoded in sensorimotor cortex (PMID 42764054) and punishment sensitivity predicting patience (PMID 42800541) are all mechanism-first arguments with no counterpart in the previous batch.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Four studies in this batch report that a neural signal appears only once a standard symptom label is cut in half — reduced thalamo-pallidal volume tracks "hot" unplanned suicidal ideation but not planned ideation (PMID 42710718); daytime sleepiness in depression maps onto physical anhedonia specifically, not social or anticipatory (PMID 42746369); gaming disorder shows striatal–thalamic abnormality while processing intrinsic rewards rather than game cues (PMID 42335494); and individual differences in free gaze are carried by orbitofrontal and anterior temporal cortex, invisible to the group-level attention networks that instructed tasks produce (PMID 42772940). The counterweight is the ABCD loneliness screen (PMID 42186177), where 558 imaging measures were beaten by the family-income question — a reminder that splitting phenotypes can also simply reveal there was never much there.

**Why pitch this now:** Psychiatric neuroimaging has spent a decade explaining its null results by appealing to sample size, and these papers advance a cheaper and more awkward explanation — that the rating-scale items used to define the phenotype, in one case a single MADRS line, were never built to carve a brain-relevant category.

**Angle:** A reported piece on where psychiatric phenotypes actually come from: who wrote the scale items that consortium studies now pool millions of scans against, what happens when researchers split them, and whether the field's biomarker disappointment is a measurement problem wearing a biology costume. For readers who follow psychiatry and research methods and have already heard the "brain imaging doesn't replicate" story.

**Potential headlines:**
