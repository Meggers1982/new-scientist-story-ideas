### 1. Talking therapy rooted in Freud improves daily functioning in people with schizophrenia

**Journal:** *The Lancet Psychiatry* | **Published:** October 2026
**PMID:** 42716057 | **DOI:** 10.1016/S2215-0366(26)00240-3
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 8/10 — a preregistered, assessor-blinded RCT with a three-year horizon testing the one therapy tradition most clinicians had written off for psychosis.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers at the Charité in Berlin randomized 130 outpatients aged 18-64 with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder to treatment as usual, or treatment as usual plus at least 30 sessions of Modified Psychodynamic Psychotherapy for Schizophrenia (MPP-S), a manualized adaptation of psychoanalytic technique for people with interpersonal vulnerability. Blinded assessors measured psychosocial functioning at 24 and 36 months. The therapy group functioned significantly better at both time points, with the gap slightly wider at three years than at two, and adverse event rates were similar between groups.

**Why it matters:** Psychodynamic therapy has been largely excluded from schizophrenia guidelines for decades on the grounds that it lacks evidence and might even destabilize patients; this is the first adequately powered, long-horizon trial to suggest otherwise, and the benefit was in everyday functioning rather than symptom counts.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The therapy that guidelines quietly abandoned just passed a randomized trial — and the advantage was still growing three years later. Open on what an MPP-S session actually looks like, which is nothing like the stereotype of a silent analyst, and on what "better functioning" means in practice: holding a conversation, keeping a job, staying in a relationship. Call Christiane Montag at Charité - Universitätsmedizin Berlin, and get a guideline committee member to say whether one trial changes anything.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a health-policy or long-read outlet on how psychotherapy evidence gets built and un-built — why a treatment used clinically for 70 years took until 2026 to be properly trialed, and what else in the psychiatric toolkit has never been tested because nobody would fund it.

**Caveats:** Single-center; participants and therapists were not blinded (only assessors); the control arm received no equivalent amount of therapist contact, so attention effects cannot be ruled out; funders include two psychoanalytic bodies (Deutsche Gesellschaft für Psychoanalyse, Psychotherapie, Psychosomatik und Tiefenpsychologie; International Psychoanalytic University Berlin); two suicides occurred during the trial; people with lived experience were not involved in design.

### 2. Comfortable therapy sessions beat gruelling ones for getting better

**Journal:** *Journal of Counseling Psychology* | **Published:** October 2026
**PMID:** 42530984 | **DOI:** 10.1037/cou0000897
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — directly contradicts the folk belief that therapy has to hurt to work, with session-by-session data, though the clinic population is narrow.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using 1,338 session ratings from 84 clients seen by 33 trainee therapists at a university clinic, the researcher separated two things usually conflated: how emotionally deep a session felt and how smooth and comfortable it felt. Clients who generally had smoother, easier sessions had substantially better symptom trajectories overall. Sessions that were deeper than a client's own average did add incremental long-term benefit — but a session that was smoother than usual was followed by a slight *uptick* in symptoms the next week.

**Why it matters:** The idea that productive therapy means confronting painful material — "no pain, no gain" — shapes what clients expect and what trainees do; this suggests relational safety, not emotional excavation, is the stronger predictor of recovery, at least in short-term work with young adults.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** You walked out of therapy feeling fine and worried you'd wasted the hour. The data say that's the session doing its job. Lead on the split finding — depth helps cumulatively, comfort helps more overall — and the awkward implication for trainee therapists taught to push. Call İbrahim Volkan Gülüm at TOBB Economy and Technology University; pair with a UK alliance researcher for an outside read on within- versus between-person effects.
- **Wider angle:** A workplace and relationships piece for a general-interest outlet on the wider "productive discomfort" myth — in therapy, coaching, performance training and management feedback — and where the evidence actually sits on whether feeling bad is a marker of progress.

**Caveats:** Small N (84 clients); single training clinic with novice therapists; client self-report for both session quality and symptoms; observational within a naturalistic clinic, so direction of effect between feeling better and rating sessions smoother cannot be settled; findings may not generalize beyond short-term work with young adults.

### 3. Therapy for childhood OCD works — but not by fixing how children think

**Journal:** *Journal of Anxiety Disorders* | **Published:** 22 September 2026
**PMID:** 42800322 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.janxdis.2026.103235
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a well-controlled negative result against a dominant neurocognitive model, but the story is a mechanism being ruled out rather than a new one found.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Within the randomized TECTO trial, 130 children aged 8-17 with OCD were assigned to 14 sessions of family-based CBT or to psychoeducation plus relaxation training; 90 had usable neurocognitive data, compared against 87 matched children without psychiatric diagnoses, tested before and after treatment. Children with OCD improved modestly on decision-making and processing speed relative to controls — but the two therapies did not differ, and neurocognitive change was unrelated to symptom improvement. Baseline cognitive performance neither predicted nor moderated who got better.

**Why it matters:** Executive-function deficits are widely invoked as the engine of OCD and as a target for treatment; if cognitive change and symptom change float free of each other, the circuit-based story needs revising — and cognitive testing is not going to tell clinicians which child will respond.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** CBT helped these children, and relaxation training helped them too — and neither worked through the brain mechanism the textbooks name. Open on a child in the trial doing a cognitive battery that turned out to predict nothing about their recovery. Call Melanie Ritter or Anne Katrine Pagsberg at the Child and Adolescent Mental Health Center in Copenhagen, and ask a cortico-striatal modeling group for the rebuttal.
- **Wider angle:** A specialist psychiatry or science-policy piece on the broader failure of "mechanism" research to deliver treatment-matching biomarkers, and whether the field's investment in cognitive and imaging markers of therapy response has paid for itself.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of an RCT; 90 of 130 randomized children had usable data; short follow-up (end of treatment at week 16); null findings on mechanism do not exclude mechanisms operating at a sub-group or individual level; author declarations include Lundbeck Foundation support and Novo Nordisk employment for one author.

### 4. Shame drives symptoms — and makes kindness hard to accept

**Journal:** *Psychotherapy Research* | **Published:** 23 September 2026
**PMID:** 42775480 | **DOI:** 10.1080/10503307.2026.2732028
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — unusually large weekly-measurement dataset and a clean directional result, though all outcomes are self-reported and the setting is inpatient.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers tracked 1,134 inpatients at the Modum Bad psychiatric center in Norway with weekly self-reports of distress, shame, and fear of receiving compassion from other people, then modeled how each predicted the others within individuals over the course of treatment. Shame and distress fed each other reciprocally, as did fear of compassion and distress. Crucially, a rise in a person's shame predicted a later rise in their fear of being treated kindly — but not the reverse.

**Why it matters:** It puts an order to two processes that clinicians usually describe as a tangle, suggesting shame is upstream: if you cannot bear being cared for, that may be shame's doing rather than a separate trait.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Some people flinch at kindness. This dataset — more than a thousand patients, measured every week — suggests shame is what builds the flinch. Open on the clinical puzzle of a patient who deflects every compassionate remark, and use the directional finding as the twist. Call Ingar Finsrud at Modum Bad's Research Institute; a compassion-focused therapy researcher would give the counterpoint on whether fear of compassion can be treated directly.
- **Wider angle:** A psychology feature for a general-interest magazine on why compliments, sympathy and gifts make some people deeply uncomfortable — pulling in work on self-criticism, social rank and the awkwardness of being helped.

**Caveats:** Observational within treatment (no randomization to anything, so causality is inferred from temporal order only); entirely self-reported measures; inpatients at a single specialist center may not generalize to outpatient or community therapy; sensitivity analyses showed the fear-of-compassion relationships varied across treatment units.

### 5. People believe in therapy more than they believe in their own therapy

**Journal:** *American Journal of Psychotherapy* | **Published:** 28 September 2026
**PMID:** 42789775 | **DOI:** 10.1176/appi.psychotherapy.20250079
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a neat, immediately recognizable psychological gap with a decent sample, limited by a one-off cross-sectional design.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Two hundred and seventy people currently in psychotherapy rated what they believed therapy does for people in general, then rated the same processes for their own treatment. For every single process, personal expectations were lower than general ones — the biggest gap being the belief that therapy gives you tools to control your thinking. The gap was widest among the most distressed people and among those with the most prior therapy experience.

**Why it matters:** Expectancy is one of the better-evidenced predictors of therapy outcome, and this suggests clinicians are measuring the wrong kind: people can endorse therapy wholeheartedly in the abstract while quietly doubting it will work for them.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Therapy helps people — just not me." Lead on the inverse of the usual optimism bias: here, people are pessimistic about themselves specifically, and the more therapy they've had, the bigger the gap. Open on the moment a patient says they're "probably a difficult case". Call Tomer Asor at the University of Haifa, or Michael Constantino at UMass Amherst, who has spent years on outcome expectations.
- **Wider angle:** A behavioral-science piece for a business or general-interest outlet on self-exemptionism — the mirror image of optimism bias — across dieting, exercise programs, language apps and self-help, and what it predicts about who sticks with anything.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional self-report with no outcome data, so the gap is not yet linked to whether anyone actually got better; order effects possible since general ratings always came first; sample recruited among people already in therapy; small effect sizes for the moderators (η² ≈ 0.02-0.03).

### 6. Borderline personality disorder doesn't blunt the effect of ketamine's cousin

**Journal:** *Psychiatry Research* | **Published:** October 2026
**PMID:** 42322927 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117288
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — contradicts a widely held clinical assumption with six-month real-world data, but it is non-randomized and modest in size.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Across four Italian centers, 90 outpatients with treatment-resistant depression starting intranasal esketamine were followed for six months — 45 with comorbid borderline personality disorder and 45 with no personality disorder. Depression scores fell steeply in both groups, with the BPD group improving faster early on and showing higher response rates from one month onward; six-month remission was statistically indistinguishable (48.9% vs 57.8%). Anxiety and impulsivity fell, cognitive scores did not worsen, and nobody dropped out.

**Why it matters:** Patients with borderline traits are routinely excluded from depression trials and often informally deprioritized for newer treatments on the assumption they respond poorly; this real-world series finds no such penalty.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The patients most often excluded from the trials did at least as well as everyone else. Lead on the exclusion problem — why "comorbid personality disorder" became a near-automatic disqualifier — then the result. Open on an outpatient clinic in Naples or Pavia on dosing day. Call Miriam Olivola at the University of Pavia; get a BPD specialist to discuss whether the faster early response reflects mood reactivity rather than true antidepressant effect.
- **Wider angle:** An investigative or long-form health piece on who gets written out of psychiatric trials — personality disorder, substance use, suicidality — and how exclusion criteria designed for clean data end up shaping who is offered new treatments in the clinic.

**Caveats:** Observational, non-randomized, so group differences in baseline course cannot be excluded; small N (90) across four centers; outcome rater blinding not described; six-month follow-up is short for a relapsing condition; esketamine is a commercial product, though the authors declare no competing interests.

### 7. Depressed patients who can't stand up for themselves are more likely to be readmitted

**Journal:** *BMC Psychiatry* | **Published:** 11 September 2026
**PMID:** 42736557 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12888-026-08490-3
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — large clinical sample and a genuinely relatable construct, but observational and built around a measure few outside Germany use.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers assessed 416 inpatients with major depression on admission and discharge using a German inventory measuring inhibition of "constructive aggression" — the capacity to assert your needs and boundaries without hostility — alongside standard depression scores. Patients were markedly inhibited on admission (mean z = 1.40), and both depression and inhibition fell during treatment. How inhibited a patient still was at discharge was associated with how much they had improved, with their depression six months later, and with whether they were readmitted within a year.

**Why it matters:** It reframes a trait usually filed under "personality" — chronic non-assertiveness — as a potentially modifiable marker of relapse risk in depression, though causality is wide open.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Niceness as a clinical signal. Lead on the counterintuitive observation that the inability to push back — not anger — tracked who stayed well after leaving hospital, and be explicit that this is correlation, not a prescription to go and be assertive. Open on the discharge interview. Call Reinhard Maß at the University of Hamburg and the Center for Mental Health Marienheide; seek an independent view on whether the measure captures anything distinct from residual depression.
- **Wider angle:** A cultural piece for a general-interest outlet on the long argument that depression is anger turned inward — a Freudian claim that fell out of fashion and is now being tested with questionnaires and readmission records.

**Caveats:** Naturalistic observational design; cannot establish causation and the authors say so explicitly; inhibition at discharge may simply index residual illness severity; single center; self-report measures (AHI, BDI-II); the CABS construct is novel and not widely validated outside this group.

### 8. Giving people feedback on a two-week eye-movement task cuts binge eating — briefly

**Journal:** *International Journal of Eating Disorders* | **Published:** 4 September 2026
**PMID:** 42698100 | **DOI:** 10.1002/eat.70198
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a clean three-arm randomized design whose most interesting result is that the do-nothing arm improved too.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** The MIND BINGES trial randomized 67 patients with bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, or related conditions involving regular binge eating to six sessions over two weeks of a food-based eye-movement inhibitory control task with performance feedback, the same task without feedback, or no training. All three groups binged less at 4 and 12 weeks. Only the feedback group beat the others at 4 weeks, and that advantage had vanished by 12.

**Why it matters:** It isolates feedback — not the training itself — as the active ingredient in impulse-control training for binge eating, and simultaneously shows that simply enrolling in a study reduced bingeing in untrained controls, which should give pause to anyone reading uncontrolled app trials.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The training didn't matter; being told how you did, did — and even then only for a month. Lead on the control group's improvement as the sharper finding, and what "non-specific effects of taking part" means for the booming market in self-monitoring eating apps. Open on the antisaccade task itself: being asked to look *away* from a photograph of cake. Call Kathrin Schag or Katrin Giel at the Centre of Excellence for Eating Disorders Tübingen.
- **Wider angle:** A digital-health feature for a technology or consumer outlet on why so many behavior-change apps post impressive before-and-after numbers: a story about what a proper control arm reveals, using this trial as the worked example.

**Caveats:** Small N (67 across three arms); proof-of-principle design not powered for durable effects; short follow-up (12 weeks); binge frequency largely self-reported; single center.

### 9. Jungian therapy, long dismissed as unevidenced, shows measurable gains

**Journal:** *Zeitschrift für Psychosomatische Medizin und Psychotherapie* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42779379 | **DOI:** 10.13109/zptm.2026.72.3.210
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — strong curiosity value and respectable effect sizes, badly limited by the absence of a control group.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers measured symptoms, personality structure, and quality of life before and after Jungian analytic psychotherapy in 104 patients treated at a German training institute, using standardized instruments (ISR, FLZ, OPD-SFK). Depression, anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms improved with moderate to large effect sizes (d = 0.56-1.17), as did self-perception and capacity for relationships. Eating disorder symptoms did not budge.

**Why it matters:** Jungian therapy — archetypes, dream work, individuation — is almost entirely absent from evidence tables, and this adds data where there was effectively none, while falling well short of showing it outperforms waiting or anything else.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Carl Jung's therapy has barely been measured in a century; here is a rare attempt, done by trainees, with effect sizes in the range reported for mainstream approaches. Be upfront that there was no control group, and use that as the story's spine: what does it take to prove a psychotherapy works, and why have some traditions never tried? Call Christian Roesler, who has spent years compiling outcome data on analytical psychology.
- **Wider angle:** A history-of-ideas feature for a general-interest or arts-leaning outlet on the strange survival of Jung — in therapy rooms, in personality testing, in popular self-help — and what happens when a mystical system is finally handed a symptom questionnaire.

**Caveats:** No control group; pre-post design cannot separate treatment from natural recovery or regression to the mean; single training institute; modest N (104); no long-term follow-up; authors are associated with the Jungian training context.

### 10. A sniff of oxytocin changes how vividly people with BPD imagine compassion

**Journal:** *Psychiatry Research* | **Published:** October 2026
**PMID:** 42372695 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117294
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a genuinely novel drug-plus-therapy pairing, but 18 people and a single sub-scale is thin.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Eighteen people with borderline personality disorder received intranasal oxytocin (24 IU) or placebo before a guided compassion meditation, once a week for five weeks, with the quality of their practice rated at the start and end. Oxytocin was associated with higher scores on the imagination dimension of compassion practice — the vividness of the imagery — but not on the total score or the bodily dimension. No adverse effects were reported.

**Why it matters:** It is an early test of whether a drug can be used to unlock a specific psychological process rather than to treat symptoms directly — "pharmacologically enhanced psychotherapy" — in a group that typically finds self-compassion exercises hardest.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Not a drug for a disorder, a drug for a *therapy exercise*. Lead on the targeting logic: people with BPD often say compassion practice feels impossible, and oxytocin seems to make the imagery come. Open on what a guided compassion meditation asks you to picture. Call Joaquim Soler or Juan Carlos Pascual at Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau in Barcelona; include a skeptic on intranasal oxytocin's replication record.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a science or health outlet on the wave of drug-assisted psychotherapy beyond psychedelics — propranolol for memory reconsolidation, MDMA, now oxytocin — and the shared problem of how you blind a trial when participants can feel the drug.

**Caveats:** Very small N (18); pilot study; effect confined to one sub-scale of a practice-quality measure with no clinical outcome; intranasal oxytocin research has a well-documented replication and dosing problem; no long-term follow-up.

### 11. Why people from minority ethnic backgrounds walk away from therapy

**Journal:** *Community Mental Health Journal* | **Published:** 11 September 2026
**PMID:** 42726438 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10597-026-01720-8
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — reframes dropout as a structural rather than motivational problem, though it is a synthesis of existing qualitative work rather than new data.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** A systematic review synthesized 13 qualitative and mixed-methods studies covering 286 adults from ethnically minoritized backgrounds, asking why they left psychotherapy early — a group that drops out at disproportionate rates in a field where around half of all clients end therapy prematurely. Six themes emerged: expectations of what therapy is, cultural and societal pressures, practical access barriers, the therapeutic relationship, service-level obstacles, and control over one's own treatment. Across studies, leaving was described not as giving up but as a reasoned response to care that did not fit.

**Why it matters:** Services routinely record early endings as patient non-engagement, which shapes how resources are allocated; this reframes much of that attrition as a property of the service, and points to shared decision-making and flexible delivery rather than motivational interventions.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Half of all therapy clients quit early, and services call it "disengagement". In this synthesis, people describe something closer to an informed exit. Lead on a single participant account of a rupture with a therapist who misread a cultural cue, then widen. Call Anam Elahi or Peter Kinderman at the University of Liverpool; pair with an NHS Talking Therapies service lead on what the dropout statistics are actually used for.
- **Wider angle:** A health-inequalities investigation for a national newspaper or policy outlet: pull UK service-level completion data by ethnicity and ask what commissioners do with it — a numbers-and-accountability piece rather than a lived-experience one.

**Caveats:** Qualitative synthesis, not new primary data; only 13 studies and 286 participants in total; heterogeneous populations and health systems pooled under one label; publication and language bias likely; no quantitative estimate of effect.

### 12. Older people who use cannabis for pain are more careful with it than those using it for mood

**Journal:** *Psychiatry* | **Published:** Fall 2026
**PMID:** 42341079 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00332747.2026.2680652
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a relatable split in a fast-growing user group, but cross-sectional, self-reported, and recruited through social media.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 471 adults aged 55 and over who reported legal medical cannabis use, measuring why they used it, how much, which self-protective habits they employed, and symptoms of cannabis use disorder. Those using cannabis for physical reasons used more protective strategies, consumed less, and scored lower on problem-use measures; those using it for emotional reasons showed the opposite pattern on all three, with the protective-strategy pathway statistically mediating the link to problem use.

**Why it matters:** Medical cannabis use is rising fastest among older adults, and this suggests the reason someone reaches for it — ache versus anxiety — predicts their risk profile better than how much they take.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Same drug, same dose, different risk — depending on what you're treating. Lead on the over-55s as the fastest-growing cannabis demographic, and on the protective habits (fixed limits, not using alone, avoiding mornings) that separate the two groups. Open on a 60-something who started with a bad back and now uses it to sleep. Call Daniel Paulson or Robert Dvorak at the University of Central Florida. Avoid any suggestion that this licenses treatment decisions.
- **Wider angle:** A consumer-health or ageing-focused feature on medical cannabis in later life — drug interactions, falls, driving, and the near-total absence of older adults from the trials that underpin prescribing.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional, so causal direction between motives, protective strategies and problem use is undetermined; entirely self-reported, including cannabis quantity; recruited via social media, so the sample is unlikely to represent older cannabis users generally; no clinical verification of diagnoses.

### 13. The word "I" in your messages tracks how well you get on with your therapist

**Journal:** *Psychotherapy Research* | **Published:** 17 September 2026
**PMID:** 42755118 | **DOI:** 10.1080/10503307.2026.2734089
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — an eye-catching language signal and a sensible negative headline result, but wholly exploratory post-hoc analysis.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Three pilot randomized trials had already found that giving clinicians dashboards built from patients' texts, emails and other electronic communications did not improve depression outcomes over usual care. Pooling them (N≈289-301), the researchers hunted for who, if anyone, benefited. Patients using many different communication platforms did poorly under usual care but caught up with the dashboard; and across the whole sample, greater use of first-person singular pronouns was associated with a stronger therapeutic alliance (partial r = .39).

**Why it matters:** It is a useful corrective to enthusiasm for digital-phenotyping dashboards — the main effect was nothing — while flagging a surprisingly strong language correlate of alliance that could be tested prospectively.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The dashboards didn't work. The pronouns might. Lead on the null result for passive smartphone monitoring in therapy, then pivot to "I" as a candidate marker of how connected a patient feels — and be clear this is hypothesis-generating, not a tool. Call Mary Beth Connolly Gibbons at the University of Pennsylvania; a computational linguistics researcher could say how reliably first-person pronoun counts mean anything.
- **Wider angle:** A privacy and ethics piece for a technology outlet: three trials already handed clinicians feeds from patients' private messaging, and the field is debating moderators rather than whether patients want this at all — the authors themselves call for measuring patient preference next time.

**Caveats:** Post-hoc, exploratory, pooled secondary analysis of three pilot trials; one key moderator finding rests on just seven intervention patients, which the authors flag; multiple comparisons; clinician engagement with the dashboards was neither standardized nor measured; no prospective replication.

### 14. A system for classifying group therapy sessions fails to replicate

**Journal:** *Journal of Counseling Psychology* | **Published:** October 2026
**PMID:** 42295247 | **DOI:** 10.1037/cou0000890
**Novelty:** Failed replication
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a clean, honestly reported replication failure, but the underlying subject is narrow and hard to make vivid.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers attempted to reproduce a published five-category typology of group therapy sessions in a new sample: 98 people with a history of problematic alcohol use, attending one of 19 six-session groups, rating the group's climate after every meeting. The original five-cluster solution could not be recovered. An exploratory analysis instead produced three types — "cohesively collaborative", "unenthusiastically detached" and "politely aloof" — and found no evidence that a session's character carried over to the next one.

**Why it matters:** Group therapy is delivered at scale precisely because it is cheap, yet the tools for describing what happens inside a group turn out not to transfer between group types — a quiet warning about taxonomies built on single samples.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Three kinds of group therapy session, and "politely aloof" is one of them. Lead on the replication failure, then on the finding that a terrible session doesn't doom the next one — there is no momentum, good or bad. Open inside a six-week alcohol group. Call Robinder Bedi at the University of British Columbia, and ask the original typology's authors for their response.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-and-credibility piece for a science or academic outlet on cluster analysis as a replication minefield: these techniques will always return clusters, which is exactly why so few survive contact with a second dataset.

**Caveats:** Small N (98 participants across 19 groups); groups were structured and time-limited around identity transitions or careers, so may differ fundamentally from open-ended therapy groups; climate measured by participant self-report only; conceptual rather than direct replication, so failure is ambiguous between the original being wrong and the populations differing.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Patients clinicians hesitate to treat do no worse.** Last run's pooled Project Harmony analysis found trauma-focused therapy for PTSD plus substance use did not destabilize patients and helped them ("Trauma-focused therapy for PTSD plus addiction helps veterans more than civilians", PMID 41114957). This run, "Borderline personality disorder doesn't blunt the effect of ketamine's cousin" (PMID 42322927) finds the same for a group routinely screened out of depression trials, and "Why people from minority ethnic backgrounds walk away from therapy" (PMID 42726438) reframes another kind of exclusion — attrition recorded as patient non-engagement — as a property of the service. Two runs now point the same way: exclusion criteria and engagement statistics are describing clinics, not patients.
- **Benefits keep arriving after the measurement window closes.** "Social life doesn't improve during therapy — it improves for years afterward" (PMID 42695247) is directly echoed by "Talking therapy rooted in Freud improves daily functioning in people with schizophrenia" (PMID 42716057), where the advantage over treatment as usual was wider at 36 months than at 24. Both argue that discharge-day endpoints systematically understate what therapy does.
- **Computational tools fail at their advertised job but keep throwing off a relationship signal.** Last run, automated sentiment analysis showed near-zero turn-level agreement with human coders yet still predicted alliance (PMID 42708250). This run, "The word 'I' in your messages tracks how well you get on with your therapist" (PMID 42755118) reports that digital-phenotyping dashboards did nothing for depression outcomes across three trials, while first-person pronoun use correlated with alliance at partial r = .39. The pattern is now consistent enough to be worth naming: language analytics are poor instruments for moments and middling ones for relationships.
- **Service-level, not motivational, explanations of who drops out.** "Muslim clients say they censor their faith in the therapy room" (PMID 42068016) and "Indigenous Australians reach mental health services more often but get fewer therapy sessions" (PMID 42592892) are joined by PMID 42726438, which synthesizes 13 studies describing early endings as reasoned exits from care that did not fit.

**Advances**

- Last run's measurement-dependence thread — sudden gains identified differently by different scales (PMID 40955789) — advances this run into outright replication failure: "A system for classifying group therapy sessions fails to replicate" (PMID 42295247) could not recover a published five-cluster typology of group sessions in a new sample.
- Last run put a biological variable alongside therapy as a passive moderator (progesterone on session day, PMID 42700422). This run moves to deliberate manipulation: "A sniff of oxytocin changes how vividly people with BPD imagine compassion" (PMID 42372695) uses a drug to target a specific therapeutic process rather than a symptom.

**Counterpoints**

- Mechanism research is splitting by type. Last run's "Children's therapy works better when the therapist unconsciously mirrors them" (PMID 42721331) found a clean mediated pathway through bodily synchrony and in-session emotion regulation. This run, "Therapy for childhood OCD works — but not by fixing how children think" (PMID 42800322) finds neurocognitive change entirely decoupled from symptom change, and baseline cognition predicting nothing. Relational and bodily mechanisms are surviving testing better than cognitive and circuit-based ones.
- The psychedelic strand that dominated last run (PMIDs 42678038, 42668404) is absent here; the drug-plus-therapy work in this batch is oxytocin (PMID 42372695) and esketamine (PMID 42322927) instead.

**New themes**

- **Depth traditions acquiring outcome data** — PMIDs 42716057, 42779379, 42736557 (see feature pitch below). Nothing comparable in the previous digest beyond a three-patient hypnosis case series (PMID 42685357).
- **What clients privately believe and feel about their own sessions** — "Comfortable therapy sessions beat gruelling ones for getting better" (PMID 42530984), "People believe in therapy more than they believe in their own therapy" (PMID 42789775) and "Shame drives symptoms — and makes kindness hard to accept" (PMID 42775480). The previous digest covered expectancy only indirectly, via naturalness bias toward psilocybin (PMID 42678038).
- **Non-specific effects of taking part** — "Giving people feedback on a two-week eye-movement task cuts binge eating — briefly" (PMID 42698100), where the untrained control arm also improved. No analogue last run.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** The psychodynamic tradition, exiled from guidelines for decades on the grounds that it had no evidence, is suddenly generating evidence — of very uneven quality. "Talking therapy rooted in Freud improves daily functioning in people with schizophrenia" (PMID 42716057) is an assessor-blinded RCT with a widening three-year advantage in the one diagnosis where analytic work was considered risky; "Jungian therapy, long dismissed as unevidenced, shows measurable gains" (PMID 42779379) reports respectable effect sizes with no control group at all; and "Depressed patients who can't stand up for themselves are more likely to be readmitted" (PMID 42736557) tests the century-old claim that depression is anger turned inward using discharge questionnaires and readmission records. "Shame drives symptoms — and makes kindness hard to accept" (PMID 42775480) adds a classically psychodynamic construct — shame as upstream of everything else — measured weekly in 1,134 inpatients.

**Why pitch this now:** A *Lancet Psychiatry* trial finding for psychodynamic therapy in schizophrenia is the first result in this tradition that guideline committees cannot simply ignore, and it lands alongside three other papers trying to convert clinical folklore into numbers — which makes this the moment to ask why a treatment used for 70 years is only being properly trialed in 2026, and who pays for that.

**Angle:** Follow one question — what does it take to prove a psychotherapy works, and why did some traditions never try? — from the Berlin RCT through the uncontrolled Jungian pre-post study to the questionnaire-and-readmission test of Freud's oldest hypothesis, examining how funding, training institutes and manualization decide which therapies ever reach an evidence table. For general readers who have been offered CBT and wondered why that was the option.

**Potential headlines:**
- Psychoanalysis is finally being put through randomized trials
- The therapy psychiatry wrote off was still pulling ahead three years later
- Why the therapy you are offered depends on which traditions bothered to run trials
