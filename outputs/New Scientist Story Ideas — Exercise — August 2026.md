### 1. Vigorous exercise fails to protect memory suppression, contradicting an earlier finding

**Journal:** *Psychological Research* | **Published:** August 11, 2026
**PMID:** 42579016 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s00426-026-02355-w
**Novelty:** Failed replication
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a large, well-powered direct replication that comes out negative, from the same lab that reported the original positive effect; limited only by how niche the memory phenomenon is.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Retrieval-induced forgetting (RIF) is the well-documented effect where recalling some facts actively suppresses related facts you didn't recall. Earlier work suggested a bout of hard aerobic exercise between memory tests kept that suppression locked in. Here 372 healthy young adults did a standard category-exemplar RIF task and were randomized between two final tests to treadmill running at vigorous intensity (80% heart rate reserve), standing on a stationary treadmill, or combined sequences varying duration and order. The classic RIF effect replicated cleanly, but exercise did nothing to preserve it — RIF shrank from test one to test two in every group, because previously suppressed items came back.

**Why it matters:** It trims back one of the more eye-catching claims in the "exercise sharpens your memory" literature, and it shows something interesting in its own right: memory suppression is temporary, and the material you thought you'd pushed aside recovers with repeated retrieval.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the recovery, not the null: the things your brain suppresses when you cram come back on their own within a single session. Open on a student revising one topic and inadvertently burying its neighbor — then reveal that the burial doesn't stick, and that a hard run doesn't make it stick either. Call Paul Loprinzi's Exercise & Memory Laboratory at the University of Mississippi (who ran both the original and this replication) and Benjamin Storm at UC Santa Cruz on what "forgetting" really means.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on self-replication as a scientific virtue — labs that publicly fail to reproduce their own headline result, and what the incentive structure does to them. Fits a research-culture outlet such as *Nature* news, *Science*, or *Undark*.

**Caveats:** Healthy young adults only; a single laboratory memory task that may not map to real-world studying; single-center.

### 2. Telling people what others do now beats telling them what others are changing

**Journal:** *Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42615122 | **DOI:** 10.1111/aphw.70209
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — two pre-registered experiments overturn a fashionable behavior-change tactic, with a behavioral (not just self-reported) outcome in one of them; sample sizes are modest.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)

**The study:** "Dynamic norm" messaging — telling people that a behavior is on the rise ("more and more people are exercising") — has become a favored nudge, said to outperform plain statements of current norms. Two pre-registered mixed-design experiments tested it on exercise: Study 1 (N=105 young adults) measured distance covered and heart rate during a cycling task; Study 2 (N=163 older adults) measured interest, intentions and self-reported weekly activity. Static norm messages beat dynamic ones on behavior in both — more distance cycled, more total weekly activity — with motivation and self-efficacy as partial pathways.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The nudge that public health has adopted is the weaker one, at least for exercise. Open on the gym poster or council leaflet announcing that "more people than ever are getting active," then explain why a flat statement of what the majority already does — a target to hit, or a sense of being outdone — moves bodies further. Call Mark Stevens at the Australian National University.
- **Wider angle:** A trend piece on the quiet unraveling of behavioral-science exports: which nudges survive pre-registration and which don't, and why departments of health keep buying the ones that don't. Fits a policy or business outlet — *The Economist*, *Financial Times*, or a specialist behavioral-science publication.

**Caveats:** Small-to-moderate N in both studies; Study 2 activity outcome self-reported; single laboratory cycling task in Study 1; short-term outcomes only.

### 3. Drinking, smoking and exercise in youth all predict less loneliness later — not the reverse

**Journal:** *Psychological Medicine* | **Published:** August 3, 2026
**PMID:** 42544718 | **DOI:** 10.1017/S0033291726105029
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — 28 years of prospective data and a causal-inference design produce a genuinely uncomfortable result; still observational.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Researchers followed 3,072 Norwegians from adolescence to midlife across 28 years, using a propensity-score-based causal inference approach to test both directions of the loneliness–health behavior link (alcohol, BMI, smoking, exercise). Loneliness predicted health behavior only weakly: less exercise in adolescence (−14 minutes/week) and slightly higher BMI in young adulthood. The reverse direction was stronger and stranger — moderate drinking, smoking and moderate exercise in adolescence and emerging adulthood all predicted *reduced* loneliness later.

**Why it matters:** It reframes youth health behaviors, healthy and risky alike, as socially embedded activities that buy company. That has awkward implications for anti-smoking and anti-drinking campaigns aimed at teenagers, and it suggests loneliness interventions might work better by supplying shared activities than by targeting loneliness directly.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The cigarette break and the five-a-side game may be doing the same psychological job. Open on the social geometry of a teenage smoking circle, then lay out the 28-year Norwegian data showing loneliness follows behavior more than it drives it. Call Lukáš Potočár or Tilmann von Soest at the PROMENTA Research Center, University of Oslo. Be explicit that this is observational and no one should take up smoking.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on the "social scaffolding" theory of adolescent risk-taking — why interventions that strip away a risky behavior without replacing its social function tend to fail. Fits a public-health or long-read outlet such as *The Atlantic* or *BMJ* features.

**Caveats:** Observational, so causation cannot be established despite the causal-inference framing; single national cohort (Norway) may not generalize; self-reported exercise, smoking and loneliness; long recall intervals between waves.

### 4. In 214,000 teenagers, a little exercise tracks with more suicidal thinking after violence — a lot tracks with less

**Journal:** *Suicide and Life-Threatening Behavior* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42605073 | **DOI:** 10.1111/sltb.70134
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — enormous sample and a non-monotonic pattern that breaks the standard "more activity, better mental health" story; cross-sectional and self-reported, which caps it.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Four waves of the Korea Youth Risk Behavior Survey (2021–2024; n=214,218) were analyzed to see whether physical activity buffers the link between violence victimization and suicidal ideation. Suicidal ideation was reported by 13.3% overall but 38.9% of victimized adolescents. Aerobic activity interacted non-monotonically with victimization: odds of ideation were *elevated* at 1–3 days a week and attenuated only at 4 or more days. Muscle-strengthening activity showed elevated odds at higher frequencies (3+ days), though not significantly in continuous models.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the single-threshold, dose-dependent framing behind youth activity guidelines as a mental health tool, and hints that context — team sport versus solitary weight training, or exercise as escape — matters more than volume. Hypothesis-generating rather than actionable.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Why the "exercise is good for teenage mental health" message needs a curve, not a line. Open on a bullied 15-year-old in a school gym, then walk through the U-shaped signal in a survey of a fifth of a million adolescents. Call Yeonjin Lee (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia). No clinical advice — the author explicitly labels the findings associational.
- **Wider angle:** A piece on what national youth risk-behavior surveys can and cannot tell us, and the growing habit of mining them for interaction effects. Fits an epidemiology or data-journalism outlet, or a specialist mental health publication.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional secondary analysis of self-report survey data; cannot establish causation or direction; activity type and setting not characterized; Korean adolescents may not generalize; some key associations not statistically significant in continuous models.

### 5. Pilates, yoga and qigong split the podium for cancer patients' mental health — and there is a dose ceiling

**Journal:** *Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42412001 | **DOI:** 10.1111/aphw.70187
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — first combined network plus dose-response analysis in this field, with an inverted-U that contradicts "more is better"; heterogeneity across 73 trials limits confidence.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** A pairwise, Bayesian network and dose-response meta-analysis of 73 randomized controlled trials (5,130 participants) compared yoga, tai chi, qigong, pilates and dance for quality of life, depression and anxiety in cancer patients and survivors. Mind-body exercise improved all three (quality of life g=0.56; depression g=−0.41; anxiety g=−0.89). Rankings diverged by outcome: pilates first for quality of life, yoga for depression, qigong for anxiety. The dose-response curve was an inverted U, peaking around 490 MET-minutes a week, with modality-specific optima of 300–520.

**Why it matters:** If real, the inverted U means prescribing more mind-body exercise past a moderate weekly dose stops helping and may reverse — a useful correction for a field that mostly reports "any is better than none."

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** There appears to be a sweet spot for mind-body exercise, and it is lower than you'd guess — roughly a few hours a week of moderate movement. Open on an oncology patient handed a generic "try yoga" leaflet, then show that the modality that helps most depends on whether the problem is mood, anxiety or day-to-day quality of life. Call Xiaoyu Wang's group at the School of Physical Education, Sichuan University.
- **Wider angle:** A skeptical explainer on network meta-analysis and SUCRA rankings: how confident can anyone be in a league table built from indirect comparisons of small, unblinded exercise trials? Fits an evidence-based-medicine or statistics-literate outlet.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of largely small, unblinded trials with high heterogeneity; outcomes self-reported questionnaires; dose-response estimates are modeled, not tested experimentally; publication bias plausible in exercise-intervention literature.

### 6. Morning outdoor exercise does nothing for sleep-deprived teenagers — bright classroom light and mindfulness do

**Journal:** *Journal of Sleep Research* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 41761836 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jsr.70307
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a rare head-to-head of school-based sleep fixes with a surprising null arm, but classroom-level allocation and an all-female single-site sample hold it back.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)

**The study:** Five classes of tenth-grade female students in Taiwan spent a 19-week semester in one of five conditions: control, 30 minutes of morning outdoor exercise, a mindfulness-based sleep course, four hours of morning simulated sunlight from a smart lighting system, or mindfulness plus lighting. Sleep, emotion, concentration and academic outcomes were tracked. Morning outdoor exercise produced no improvement over control. Smart lighting and the mindfulness course each independently increased sleep duration and improved mood and concentration, and the combination produced the largest gains, including in academic achievement.

**Why it matters:** It punctures the intuitive assumption that a morning burst of outdoor activity is the obvious lever for adolescent sleep, and points instead at sustained daytime light dose and taught sleep skills.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The most effective school sleep intervention may be the ceiling. Open in a classroom at 8am — one group jogging outside, another sitting under engineered light — and reveal which group slept longer by December. Call Jamie Zeitzer at Stanford, a circadian specialist on the author list, and Yu-Chi Yang at National Cheng Kung University.
- **Wider angle:** A design and architecture story on lighting as public health infrastructure: retrofitting schools, offices and care homes for circadian dose rather than lumens. Fits a built-environment or technology outlet, or a *Wired*-style feature.

**Caveats:** Allocation by classroom, not individual, with one class per condition — confounding by class and teacher is unavoidable; single school, all female, Taiwan; no blinding; short-ish follow-up (one semester); several outcomes self-reported.

### 7. VR gym classes lift teenagers' fitness and interest — and the effect outlasts the novelty

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** September 2026 (Epub August 5, 2026)
**PMID:** 42556203 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107585
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a properly randomized, decently sized trial with accelerometer and objective fitness outcomes, plus interviews; single author and no blinding possible.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** An explanatory sequential mixed-methods randomized trial assigned 360 Chinese adolescents aged 13–16 from three middle schools to VR-supported physical education or conventional PE for 12 weeks, with 4-week follow-up. The VR group gained more in exercise interest (d=0.78), coordination (d=0.62), cardiorespiratory endurance (d=0.55) and sprint speed (d=0.48), but not explosive power. Effects were partially retained at follow-up, which the author argues is inconsistent with a purely novelty-driven explanation; interviews pointed to immersion, feedback and self-efficacy.

**Why it matters:** The perennial objection to gamified exercise is that it wears off. Partial retention after four weeks plus real shuttle-run and coordination gains suggests something more durable, though four weeks is a thin test of durability.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Headsets in PE improved actual cardiorespiratory fitness, not just enthusiasm — and the enthusiasm looks like the mechanism. Open on the child who has spent years dreading the shuttle run. Call Yang Bao at Nanjing Sport Institute, and get an independent VR-exercise researcher to interrogate the novelty argument.
- **Wider angle:** An education feature on the commercialization of school PE — who sells the hardware, what happens to unhealthy-but-cheap conventional PE budgets, and equity between schools that can and cannot afford headsets. Fits an education or technology-policy outlet.

**Caveats:** No blinding and strong expectancy effects likely; follow-up only 4 weeks; single-country sample; mediation analysis explicitly non-causal by the author's own admission; single-author trial with no registration reported in the abstract.

### 8. A compassion exercise made people angrier

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** September 2026 (Epub July 28, 2026)
**PMID:** 42520720 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107540
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a clean randomized experiment where the wellness staple backfires; scenario-based provocation online is a weak proxy for real anger.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** In an online 3×3 mixed design, 229 participants were randomized to a brief compassion, shared-joy or control writing exercise, then guided through an imagined provocation, with measures at baseline, post-intervention and post-provocation. Neither prosocial exercise buffered anger after provocation. The compassion exercise raised anger across the whole sample and worsened both positive and negative affect immediately after the intervention; the joy exercise raised positive affect.

**Why it matters:** Compassion practice is marketed as universally soothing. This suggests it can be an emotionally costly exercise, at least in single short doses, and that shared joy may be the safer entry point.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Ten minutes of compassion practice left people feeling worse and slightly angrier. Open on the app prompt telling a user to send loving-kindness to someone difficult. Call Nicole Geschwind and Jill Lobbestael at Maastricht University, and press them on dose: does the cost invert with sustained practice?
- **Wider angle:** An investigation into the adverse-effects blind spot in the mindfulness and compassion industry — how rarely trials measure harm, and what the emerging harm literature shows. Fits a health-features desk or a magazine like *Mosaic*/*Wellcome*-style long-read.

**Caveats:** Online, single-session, imagined rather than experienced provocation; self-reported affect; immediate outcomes only; student-heavy convenience sample likely; effects may not generalize to trained practitioners.

### 9. Chronic pain patients rate massage and psychotherapy above the pills they actually take

**Journal:** *Pain Management* | **Published:** August 17, 2026
**PMID:** 42605847 | **DOI:** 10.1080/17581869.2026.2718883
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a striking mismatch between what patients use and what they say works, across 56 options; retrospective single-clinic self-report keeps it modest.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)

**The study:** Intake questionnaires from 226 patients (mean age 45; 81% female) at a multidisciplinary pain clinic rated 56 treatments as helpful, unhelpful, never tried or unsure. The most-tried treatments were acetaminophen (90%), physical therapy (88%) and ibuprofen (83%) — yet fewer than half of users called the common analgesics helpful. Massage therapy topped the helpfulness table at 77%, followed by oxycodone (74%), psychotherapy (67%), hydromorphone (67%), physical therapy (62%), aquatic therapy (62%) and hydrocodone (61%).

**Why it matters:** It documents a real-world gap between treatment volume and perceived benefit, and puts hands-on and psychological therapies — plus water-based exercise — near the top of patients' own rankings. It cannot tell us what works, only what people say helped them.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The drugs everyone with chronic pain has tried are the ones fewest say helped. Open on the medicine cabinet of a patient who has cycled through 20 of the 56 listed treatments. Call Michael Bushey at Indiana University School of Medicine. Frame carefully as perceived helpfulness, not efficacy — this is not a trial.
- **Wider angle:** A story about aquatic therapy and massage as the underprescribed middle ground in pain care, and why insurers and health systems fund pills and injections instead. Fits a health-policy outlet or a specialist pain/rehabilitation publication.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, single-center, no control or comparison group; entirely self-reported perceptions with recall and selection bias; patients at a tertiary pain clinic are a filtered population; ratings conditional on having tried each treatment.

### 10. Giving stressed doctors a $45 monthly gym-class pass reduced their anxiety

**Journal:** *Academic Psychiatry* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42362858 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40596-026-02384-y
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a randomized test of a commercial fitness subscription as a mental health intervention, with a large effect size but only 28 participants.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Twenty-eight psychiatry residents were randomized to a 16-week ClassPass membership (20 credits, roughly $45, per month) or a waitlist; 26 completed follow-up. Engagement was high (77% used most or all credits; 100% recommended expansion). Anxiety fell significantly relative to control (p=0.012, d=1.07), with large but non-significant effects for depression (d=1.06) and loneliness (d=1.12), and nothing for burnout. Qualitative feedback emphasized flexibility, choice and social connection.

**Why it matters:** Physician wellness programs are notoriously unloved. The signal here is less about exercise per se than about autonomy — letting people pick any class, any time — and about a hint that burnout, a workload phenomenon, doesn't budge for perks.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Burnout didn't move, anxiety did — what a small trial in exhausted doctors says about the limits of wellness perks. Open on a resident booking a 6am boxing class between shifts. Call Kevin Yang at UC San Diego School of Medicine, and someone from the physician-burnout literature to argue that hours, not memberships, are the variable.
- **Wider angle:** A workplace-health piece on employers buying subscription fitness for staff: what the evidence base actually supports, and the ethics of individualizing a structural problem. Fits a business or HR-facing outlet.

**Caveats:** Very small N (28), single-center, waitlist control with no attention placebo, self-reported outcomes, short follow-up; two senior authors report unrelated industry ties (Compass Pathways; Alkermes advisory board) — the intervention itself was not company-funded per the abstract.

### 11. Feeling hungry predicts skipping your workout, not just breaking your diet

**Journal:** *Psychology & Health* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 40415394 | **DOI:** 10.1080/08870446.2025.2509070
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — intensive real-time data with a neat cross-domain surprise; small N and self-report cap it.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Eighty-one Dutch adults trying to lose weight completed two seven-day ecological momentary assessment weeks, answering six semi-random prompts a day about lapses, emotion, stress, hunger and self-efficacy. For exercise, hunger raised the odds of lapsing (OR=1.14) while feeling relaxed lowered them (OR=0.83); coping and recovery self-efficacy were the strongest protective factors for both exercise (ORs 0.68, 0.78) and diet (both 0.81). Emotional states other than relaxation and stress showed little.

**Why it matters:** It suggests exercise and eating lapses share a psychological engine — momentary confidence in your ability to cope and to get back on track — and that hunger leaks across domains to undermine activity, not only food choices.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The reason you skipped the gym may have been in your stomach. Open on 5pm on a diet day, then use the moment-by-moment data to show hunger and low coping confidence as the real lapse triggers. Call Maartje van Stralen at Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on just-in-time adaptive interventions: apps that detect the moment before a lapse from phone and wearable signals, and whether real-time nudging works or simply annoys. Fits a technology or digital-health outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (81); all outcomes self-reported in-the-moment; observational within-person associations, not causal; motivated weight-loss volunteers in one country may not generalize; two one-week windows only.

### 12. Being tuned in to your heartbeat says nothing about how well you read your gut

**Journal:** *Biological Psychology* | **Published:** July 29, 2026
**PMID:** 42526739 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.biopsycho.2026.109360
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 5/10 — undercuts a common assumption behind a large interoception literature; explicitly a pilot with 33 participants.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Thirty-three healthy adults completed comparable magnitude-estimation and sustained-stimulation tasks in three bodily domains within subjects: breathing (inspiratory resistive loads), esophagus (balloon inflation) and heart (graded cycle ergometer exercise). Interoceptive sensitivity — how well intensity ratings tracked actual stimulation — did not correlate across domains at all, with Bayesian analyses giving anecdotal support for the null. Affective responses partly converged: respiratory unpleasantness correlated with both esophageal and cardiac unpleasantness.

**Why it matters:** A great deal of work on emotion, anxiety and body awareness treats "interoception" as one general ability measured through heartbeat tasks. If sensitivity is organ-specific while unpleasantness generalizes, those single-organ measures cannot stand in for the whole system.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** There may be no such thing as being "good at listening to your body." Open on the heartbeat-counting task that underpins hundreds of studies, then explain why the same person can be sharp about their breath and hopeless about their gut. Call Andreas von Leupoldt at KU Leuven.
- **Wider angle:** A critical feature on interoception's rapid rise as an explanatory concept in psychiatry and wellness — from anxiety models to breathwork marketing — and how shaky the measurement foundations are. Fits a psychology-focused specialist outlet or a science long-read.

**Caveats:** Explicit pilot, N=33 healthy adults; null correlations in a small sample are weak evidence (Bayes factors only "anecdotal"); esophageal balloon and exercise paradigms are hard to equate in intensity or unpleasantness; single-center.

### 13. When people are told to train according to how they feel, half the time they deliberately don't

**Journal:** *Journal of Sport & Exercise Psychology* | **Published:** August 1, 2026 (Epub April 25, 2026)
**PMID:** 42034345 | **DOI:** 10.1123/jsep.2025-0211
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a rich look at how exercise decisions really get made, but 24 participants and qualitative design make it a soft story.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Twenty-four participants were invited to practice flexible nonlinear periodization for 14 days — matching each session's demand to their current physical and mental resources — completing pre- and post-exercise surveys and then being interviewed while viewing visualizations of their own data. Resource-concordant choices supported recovery, overload and trying new activities. Resource-discordant choices, made anyway, were driven by wanting distraction from stress, social commitments, or self-imposed expectations. Experiences were neither reliably pleasant nor unpleasant.

**Why it matters:** It complicates the "listen to your body" prescription now baked into recovery-score wearables: people knowingly override their own readiness for reasons that are often good ones.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Sometimes the right workout is the wrong one — a hard session on a depleted day because you need the distraction. Open on someone whose watch tells them to rest and who trains anyway. Call Kelley Strohacker at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville.
- **Wider angle:** A consumer-technology story on readiness scores from Whoop, Oura and Garmin: what they measure, what they ignore about motive and mood, and whether obeying them makes people fitter or more anxious. Fits a technology or fitness outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (24), no control condition, 14 days only, self-selected participants, qualitative and hypothesis-generating; no fitness outcomes measured.

### 14. Psychiatry's bipolar disorder body finally puts exercise on the treatment agenda

**Journal:** *Bipolar Disorders* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42545207 | **DOI:** 10.1111/bdi.70164
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — the first ISBD position statement on movement in bipolar disorder, notable for what it admits is missing; it is a synthesis, not new data.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** The International Society for Bipolar Disorders Nutrition and Exercise Task Force conducted a best-evidence synthesis across five databases to inception (March 2026), in question-and-answer format. People with bipolar disorder sit far more and move far less than the general population, with large gaps between self-reported and objectively measured activity. Evidence that activity protects against onset is mixed across prospective and Mendelian randomization studies. Early clinical trials suggest exercise improves functioning, reduces mood episodes and hospitalizations and eases depressive symptoms — including one pilot reporting an 82% antidepressant response rate — but robust data are scarce.

**Why it matters:** It marks the point at which a mainstream psychiatric society treats sedentary behavior as part of bipolar disorder's clinical picture rather than a lifestyle footnote, while conceding the trial base is thin.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Movement is being written into the bipolar treatment conversation on strikingly little evidence — and the field says so out loud. Open on the exercise caution specific to bipolar disorder: the worry, largely untested, about intense activity and mania. Call Beny Lafer (University of São Paulo) or Elisa Brietzke (Queen's University), and ask what a definitive multicenter trial would need.
- **Wider angle:** A story on psychiatric medication, weight gain and the sedentary spiral — how drug side effects and low activity compound cardiovascular risk and shorten lives in severe mental illness. Fits a health-policy or medical outlet.

**Caveats:** Narrative best-evidence synthesis, not a systematic meta-analysis; the standout 82% response figure comes from a single uncontrolled pilot; heavy reliance on self-reported activity in source studies; extensive pharmaceutical consultancy and honoraria disclosures across the large author list (declared, unrelated to this work).

### 15. "Drunkorexia" splits into two distinct patterns — and doing both marks the highest-risk students

**Journal:** *Eating Behaviors* | **Published:** August 25, 2026
**PMID:** 42641412 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.eatbeh.2026.102120
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a large multi-site sample and a clean motive-based split, but a cross-sectional survey of a well-studied behavior.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)

**The study:** Among 2,801 undergraduates at six universities who drank in the past month (mean age 19.5; 78% female), researchers separated food and alcohol disturbance by motive: restricting to intensify alcohol's effects (FAD-AE), compensating for alcohol calories (FAD-CC), both, or alcohol only. Adjusting for sex, age and drinking quantity, the both-motives group scored higher across most of eight eating-disorder domains. Among single-motive students, the calorie-compensation group showed more body dissatisfaction, cognitive restraint and excessive exercise, whereas the effect-enhancement group showed more dietary restriction.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the widely reported "drunkorexia" phenomenon is at least two behaviors with different psychological signatures, and that punishing exercise after drinking clusters specifically with body-image distress.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Skipping dinner to get drunk faster and running it off the next morning look like the same habit but sit on different psychological tracks — and students who do both are the ones to worry about. Open on the pre-night-out calorie calculation. Call Kelsey Berry at the University of Wyoming.
- **Wider angle:** A campus-culture piece on fitness-tracking apps and calorie budgeting for alcohol — how the quantified-self toolkit hands students the arithmetic that drives compensatory behavior. Fits a general-interest or student-facing outlet.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional, self-reported survey; cannot establish direction or causation; heavily female and predominantly White US undergraduate sample; group categorization by endorsed motive may be unstable over time.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Across this batch, the strongest results are subtractions rather than additions — the exercise-and-wellness evidence base is being trimmed by its own researchers. A well-powered self-replication finds vigorous exercise does nothing to preserve memory suppression (PMID 42579016); pre-registered experiments show the fashionable "dynamic norm" nudge loses to plain static norms on actual cycling distance (PMID 42615122); a 73-trial network and dose-response meta-analysis finds an inverted U for mind-body exercise in cancer patients, peaking near 490 MET-minutes a week (PMID 42412001); 214,218 Korean teenagers show elevated suicidal ideation at 1–3 days of aerobic activity and attenuation only at 4+ (PMID 42605073); morning outdoor exercise is the null arm in a school sleep trial won by classroom lighting and mindfulness (PMID 41761836); and a brief compassion exercise made people angrier and worse off on affect (PMID 42520720). Even the positive findings come hedged — a $45 gym-class pass moved anxiety but not burnout (PMID 42362858).

**Why pitch this now:** Physical activity is being written into clinical and public health guidance faster than the trial base can support it — the ISBD's first position statement on movement in bipolar disorder (PMID 42545207) says so explicitly while leaning on a single uncontrolled pilot for its most quotable number — and this month's crop shows the correction arriving from inside the field rather than from outside critics.

**Angle:** A reported feature on what happens when the "movement is medicine" literature starts auditing itself: dose ceilings instead of dose-response lines, nulls published by the labs that found the original effects, and nudges that fail pre-registration — written for readers who have been told for a decade that more exercise is always better, with researchers like Paul Loprinzi, Mark Stevens and the ISBD task force authors on the record about why the correction is coming now.

**Potential headlines:**
- The exercise research that is being taken back
- Where the dose curve bends: more movement stops helping sooner than the guidelines assume
- Your gym habit is fine — the science telling you why it works is being rewritten
