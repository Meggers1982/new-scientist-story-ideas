### 1. Demanding perfection from your partner quietly erodes their desire for you

**PMID:** 42411312 | **DOI:** 10.1080/0092623X.2026.2694398
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 9/10 — Strong topic fit for NS Mind (relationships, sex, personality), counterintuitive split finding (self-perfectionism helps, other-perfectionism harms), longitudinal dyadic design with decent sample, and a clear societal hook around perfectionism culture.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers in Canada recruited 308 cohabiting couples who completed questionnaires on perfectionism, emotional intimacy, and relationship and sexual satisfaction at two time points one year apart. The study found that people high in other-oriented perfectionism — holding their partner to excessively high standards — were associated with lower relationship satisfaction in themselves and lower relationship and sexual satisfaction in their partners one year later, mediated by the partner reporting reduced emotional intimacy. Interestingly, self-oriented perfectionism was associated with higher sexual satisfaction and, through that, greater emotional intimacy in oneself.

**Why it matters:** The findings suggest that directing perfectionist standards outward toward a partner — rather than inward — may be a quietly corrosive relationship behaviour, with consequences that ripple through intimacy and sexual connection over time.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Holding your partner to high standards is quietly killing your sex life — but holding yourself to them isn't" A year-long study of over 300 couples found that perfectionism directed at a partner eroded their sexual satisfaction — while the same trait turned inward had the opposite effect. We tend to think of perfectionism as a personality flaw that mainly hurts the person who has it — but new longitudinal research tracking 308 couples over a year tells a more complicated story. When people held themselves to exacting standards, their own sexual satisfaction tended to be higher, and their emotional intimacy followed. But when they turned those same standards on their partner, the effects were the reverse: partners felt less emotionally close, and that distance predicted lower relationship and sexual satisfaction a year later. The finding reframes perfectionism not just as a personal struggle but as an interpersonal force — one that can quietly reshape the intimacy of a relationship from the outside in. Why it fits: Perfectionism is a buzzword in self-help and therapy culture; NS Mind readers will be gripped by evidence that its relational consequences depend entirely on which direction it points.

**The study:** This article summarises key presentations from the 2023 Workshop on Neurobiology of Epilepsy (WONOEP XVII), attended by an international group of researchers, covering novel biomarkers and treatment strategies that go beyond traditional synaptic targets. Topics included plasma biomarkers for post-traumatic seizures, perivascular spaces in epilepsy, imaging markers of brain scarring, microRNA biomarkers, immunotherapy for drug-resistant epilepsy, and the effects of seizure medications on cardiac injury. Approximately 30% of epilepsy patients remain resistant to current medications, motivating the search for disease-modifying approaches. This is a conference summary, not an original empirical study.

**Why it matters:** With roughly 70 million people worldwide living with epilepsy and nearly a third unhelped by existing drugs, identifying new biological targets — from the immune system to the blood-brain barrier — could open doors to treatments that prevent or modify the disease rather than just suppress seizures.

- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "One in three epilepsy patients can't be helped by any current drug — scientists are searching in new places" After decades of targeting the same brain processes, epilepsy researchers are turning to the immune system, blood-brain barrier, and even the heart for answers. Despite more than 20 licensed anti-seizure medications, about 30% of epilepsy patients find none of them work. A 2023 international workshop heard evidence that plasma proteins, brain-fluid channels, and inflammatory pathways may hold the key to the next generation of treatments. The shift from 'suppress the seizure' to 'change the disease' marks a significant moment in neurology.
