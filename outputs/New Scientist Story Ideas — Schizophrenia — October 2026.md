### 1. Most people have intrusive fears of "going crazy" — and OCD makes them stick

**Journal:** *Journal of Behavior Therapy and Experimental Psychiatry* | **Published:** 23 September 2026
**PMID:** 42800306 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbtep.2026.102139
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — vividly relatable and the first prevalence estimate for a fear patient communities already talk about, but it is a student analogue sample, not a clinical one.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers at Memorial University of Newfoundland surveyed 154 university students about unwanted intrusive thoughts and compulsions centered on the fear of becoming psychotic — informally known as "schizophrenia OCD". Some 83 percent reported at least one such thought or behavior in their lifetime; the most common intrusion was fear of losing control of your thoughts or actions (50.6 percent), while fear of actually developing a psychotic illness (18.3 percent) was rated the most distressing. Health anxiety and negative beliefs about losing control predicted these fears over and above overall OCD severity.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that fearing you are going mad is close to a universal human experience rather than a rare symptom, and that what turns it into a clinical problem is not the thought itself but what you believe losing control would mean.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the 2am thought almost everyone has had — *what if I'm losing my mind?* — and the finding that four in five young adults have had it, complete with the quiet rituals (half the sample "replaced bad thoughts with better ones") they use to make it go away. The hook is that the content of the obsession is near-universal; the pathology lies in the meaning assigned to it. Call Emily Fawcett's group at Memorial University, and ask an OCD clinician why this subtype goes unrecognized in standard checklists dominated by contamination and symmetry.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a health or culture outlet on how psychiatric diagnoses leak into lay anxiety — people with OCD now obsess about having schizophrenia, people with health anxiety google dementia symptoms. How does public mental-health literacy create new obsessional content? Fits a long-read in a general-interest magazine.

**Caveats:** Non-clinical student sample (N=154) with self-reported measures and OCD defined by cut-off scores, not diagnostic interview; cross-sectional, so no evidence on what comes first; population unlikely to generalize beyond young, educated adults.

### 2. People in early psychosis say romance is central to recovery — their clinics almost never ask

**Journal:** *Journal of Mental Health* | **Published:** October 2026 (Epub 27 March 2026)
**PMID:** 41891676 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09638237.2026.2646293
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a genuine blind spot in psychosis care told in patients' own words, though qualitative work with 20 people can only describe, not quantify.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers at Queen's University conducted in-depth interviews with 20 people attending early psychosis intervention programs in Ontario, Canada, and analyzed them using reflexive thematic analysis. Participants described intimacy, romance and sexuality as both a goal of recovery and a yardstick for measuring it — yet reported that clinicians rarely raised the subject, and when they did, it often came with stipulations or warnings. Participants also described stigma, symptom interference, lost relationships and narrowed opportunities as the main barriers.

**Why it matters:** Services built around symptom reduction may be ignoring the outcome patients themselves use to judge whether they are getting better, which has direct implications for how recovery is defined and measured.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead on the mismatch — clinicians track voices and medication adherence; patients track whether anyone will date them. Open on a participant describing being told relationships were something to postpone until they were "stable". The surprise is that a dimension clinicians treat as a risk to be managed is the one patients treat as the measure of recovery. Call Christopher Bowie at Queen's University; worth also approaching an early-intervention service lead on why the topic is avoided.
- **Wider angle:** A reported piece for a health-policy or disability-focused outlet on sexual citizenship in mental health care — who gets to be assumed capable of consent, desire and parenthood, and how capacity assessments and institutional risk-aversion shape that. Different story, broader stakes.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (N=20), single region and service model, self-report by design, no quantitative outcome data; findings are exploratory and describe experiences rather than testing an intervention.

### 3. Women's verbal memory advantage vanishes after a first episode of psychosis

**Journal:** *Spanish Journal of Psychiatry and Mental Health* | **Published:** 7 October 2026
**PMID:** 42843653 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.sjpmh.2026.08.001
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a tidy sex-by-genotype interaction in a decent family-based cohort, but single-SNP findings need replication and the functional story is admittedly speculative.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** A Spanish team genotyped 486 people — patients with first-episode psychosis, their unaffected siblings and controls — and tested them on the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test. Among controls and siblings, women carrying the C allele of rs9896243 (in the NSF gene) outperformed men on immediate, short-delay and long-delay recall. In patients with first-episode psychosis, that female advantage disappeared entirely, and male patients carrying the C allele showed the most pronounced recognition deficits.

**Why it matters:** It is early evidence that a genetic variant's effect on memory is not fixed but depends on both sex and illness state — which would complicate the common assumption that cognitive risk genes act the same way in everyone.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Start with the well-replicated observation that women with schizophrenia tend to have better verbal memory than men, then the twist: in this cohort the female advantage was present in healthy relatives but gone in patients, and the same allele that helped women appeared to hurt men with psychosis. The hook is a gene whose effect flips sign depending on who carries it. Call Rosa Ayesa-Arriola at IDIVAL in Santander, who runs the first-episode cohort.
- **Wider angle:** A broader feature for a science or medicine outlet on sex-stratified psychiatric genetics — why most risk-gene studies pool men and women, what gets lost, and whether sex-informed polygenic scores are coming. Fits a specialist genomics or precision-medicine publication.

**Caveats:** Observational and cross-sectional (no causal inference); single candidate SNP in a modest cohort, needs independent replication; functional annotation is explicitly described by the authors as hypothesis-generating; single-center Spanish sample.

### 4. Genetic evidence points to clozapine itself driving diabetes risk

**Journal:** *The World Journal of Biological Psychiatry* | **Published:** October 2026 (Epub 19 July 2026)
**PMID:** 42472444 | **DOI:** 10.1080/15622975.2026.2702424
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — Mendelian randomization lends causal weight to a long-standing clinical worry, but the biomarker list is a hypothesis-generating pipeline, not a validated test.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** An Adelaide-based team used Mendelian randomization — exploiting genetic variants that influence how fast people metabolize clozapine — to probe whether the drug causes cardiometabolic harm rather than merely accompanying it. Higher genetically-predicted plasma clozapine levels and a higher clozapine-norclozapine ratio were associated with increased risk of type 2 diabetes and higher blood pressure. A phenome-scan-colocalization-MR pipeline flagged 28 markers tied to clozapine metabolism loci, 16 of which showed associations with cardiometabolic outcomes, including hematological and excretory traits.

**Why it matters:** Clozapine remains the only drug that works for many people with treatment-resistant schizophrenia, and the possibility of identifying in advance who will suffer metabolic harm would change a brutal risk-benefit calculation for hundreds of thousands of patients.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the trade-off a psychiatrist makes every time they start clozapine: the most effective antipsychotic we have, and the one most likely to give you diabetes. The news is that genetic instruments — not just correlational patient records — now point toward drug exposure itself, and toward blood markers that might flag vulnerable patients before weight gain starts. Call Dan Stacey or Scott Clark at Adelaide University. Avoid implying anyone should change their dose: the authors explicitly say prospective validation is missing.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a pharmacology or health-systems outlet on why clozapine is simultaneously underprescribed (because of mandatory blood monitoring) and dangerous, and whether predictive genotyping could unlock access for patients currently denied it. Fits a medicine-focused trade or policy publication.

**Caveats:** Genetic-epidemiological inference, not a clinical trial; authors state findings do not establish causation without prospective validation; relies on existing GWAS summary data whose ancestry composition limits generalizability; no patient-level outcomes.

### 5. There may be no single recipe for recovering from schizophrenia

**Journal:** *Psychiatric Rehabilitation Journal* | **Published:** 14 September 2026
**PMID:** 42734987 | **DOI:** 10.1037/prj0000701
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a neat explanation for why the recovery literature keeps contradicting itself, limited by a small single-country sample.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Japanese researchers assessed 101 people with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder on symptoms, depression, cognition, social functioning and metacognition, then used finite mixture regression to test whether a single model of "personal recovery" fits everyone. It does not: the analysis found distinct latent classes with different association structures. In one, recovery tracked social functioning, depression, positive symptoms and metacognition; in another — with comparable symptom and functioning levels — no dominant predictor emerged at all. Negative symptoms mattered in both.

**Why it matters:** It offers a concrete statistical reason why studies of what helps people recover from psychosis keep disagreeing — the field may have been averaging across groups for whom recovery works in fundamentally different ways.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the problem readers can feel: two people with the same diagnosis, the same symptom scores and the same level of functioning, for whom completely different things determine whether life feels worth living. The hook is a methodological result with a human payoff — "average patient" models may be an artifact. Call Hiroki Okada at Hyogo Medical University, and get a rehabilitation clinician's view on whether services can realistically individualize.
- **Wider angle:** A broader piece for a statistics-literate or health-research outlet on the quiet crisis of averaging in psychology and medicine — ergodicity, mixture models, and why group-level findings so often fail the individual in front of you. Fits a data-science or research-methods publication.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=101) for a latent-class method that is data-hungry; cross-sectional and observational, so classes are associative rather than causal; one of the three classes too small to interpret; single-country sample with a specific rehabilitation service context.

### 6. Two ways reality can go wrong: the philosopher's case for comparing borderline and schizophrenia

**Journal:** *Psychopathology* | **Published:** 9 October 2026
**PMID:** 42853770 | **DOI:** 10.1159/psp/aelag012
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a genuinely arresting idea about what the world feels like in two very different conditions, but it is conceptual scholarship with no new data.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Two phenomenologists contrast the lived experience of borderline personality disorder with that of schizophrenia, arguing that both involve a disturbed relationship to reality but in opposite directions. Schizophrenia, they argue, is marked by an "evanescence of reality" — the world thins out and the person searches obliquely for hidden meaning — whereas borderline experience, especially in acute distress, involves an "excess of reality": encounters, bodily sensations and perceptions arrive as raw, abrasive, inescapable fact. They describe this as "ontic gravity", a crushing inability to step back from what is immediately given.

**Why it matters:** Not much changes clinically yet, but it reframes "derealization" — usually treated as one symptom — as potentially two opposite failures of the same system, and gives a vocabulary to an experience patients often struggle to describe.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on what it means to be unable to take anything with a pinch of salt — to have every comment, ache and glance land as unmediated fact. The surprise is the inversion: we assume psychiatric distress means losing touch with reality, but one condition may involve too much contact with it. Call Giovanni Stanghellini, and pair him with a clinician who works with both diagnoses to test whether patients recognize the description. Be explicit that this is theory, not a finding.
- **Wider angle:** A philosophy-of-mind feature for a literary or ideas outlet on the revival of phenomenological psychiatry — Husserl's epoché repurposed as a clinical tool, and whether first-person description can earn a place next to biomarkers in a field dominated by neuroimaging and genomics.

**Caveats:** Conceptual/theoretical paper with no participants, no data and no empirical test; claims are not falsified or quantified here; the comparison risks essentializing two heterogeneous diagnostic categories.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- **The "average patient" problem keeps surfacing, now in recovery research.** Last run's psychometric reassurance ("Computational models of memory turn out to be as reliable as the simple scores they replace", PMID 42765980) and the negative EEG result where group separation failed to identify individuals (PMID 41124326) framed the gap between describing groups and describing people. The new "There may be no single recipe for recovering from schizophrenia" (PMID 42734987) attacks the same gap from the other side: fit a mixture model and the single "predictors of personal recovery" equation dissolves into classes with different association structures.
- **Phenomenology continues its run.** Last run carried self-disturbance as a mediator from childhood neglect to psychotic experiences (PMID 42740395) and voice hearers negotiating autonomy rather than simply losing it (PMID 41354941). "Two ways reality can go wrong" (PMID 42853770) is the theoretical companion piece, proposing an "evanescence of reality" in schizophrenia against an "excess of reality" in borderline personality disorder — the same intellectual revival, now arguing at the level of concepts rather than questionnaires.

**Advances**
- **Clozapine's metabolic harm moves from management to causal attribution.** Last run compared counter-measures in real-world care ("Metformin, but not the standard add-on drug, curbs weight gain in people taking clozapine", PMID 42742489) and described a disturbed gut community in clozapine-treated patients with no unmedicated comparator (PMID 42758012). "Genetic evidence points to clozapine itself driving diabetes risk" (PMID 42472444) uses Mendelian randomization to argue the exposure itself — not the illness or lifestyle it travels with — raises type 2 diabetes risk and blood pressure, and flags candidate markers for identifying vulnerable patients in advance.
- **Cognition in genetic risk gains a sex dimension.** The 22q11.2 consortium study (PMID 42765380) found a consistently shaped cognitive profile with no domain-specific signature in those who developed psychosis. "Women's verbal memory advantage vanishes after a first episode of psychosis" (PMID 42843653) adds a moderator that pooled analyses would erase: an NSF-gene allele associated with better verbal recall in women among controls and siblings, with that advantage absent in patients and male carriers showing the worst recognition deficits.

**New themes**
- **Patient-defined recovery outcomes.** "People in early psychosis say romance is central to recovery — their clinics almost never ask" (PMID 41891676) is the first item in this topic memory on intimacy and sexuality as an outcome domain, and sits naturally alongside last run's voice-hearing work (PMID 41354941) on meaning rather than symptom suppression.
- **Psychiatric vocabulary as obsessional content.** "Most people have intrusive fears of 'going crazy' — and OCD makes them stick" (PMID 42800306) is new territory for this topic: schizophrenia as an object of public fear rather than a clinical population, with 83 percent of a student sample reporting lifetime intrusions about losing their mind.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Four studies in this batch converge on the subjective life of psychosis as something services neither measure nor ask about. People in early psychosis programs use intimacy and romance as their yardstick for recovery while clinicians avoid the topic (PMID 41891676); a mixture-model analysis shows that what predicts personal recovery differs fundamentally between patients with identical symptom and functioning scores (PMID 42734987); phenomenologists argue that "derealization" may be two opposite failures, a thinning of reality in schizophrenia versus a crushing excess of it in borderline states (PMID 42853770); and the fear of going mad turns out to be close to universal in the general population, with the pathology lying in what people believe losing control would mean (PMID 42800306).

**Why pitch this now:** Services are being rebuilt around "personal recovery" as a stated goal at the same moment the evidence suggests there is no single thing that drives it and no routine way to ask about the outcomes patients actually care about — a gap that is about to be inherited by outcome-based commissioning and app-based symptom tracking.

**Angle:** A reported feature on what recovery from psychosis means to the people in it, built from patient accounts and set against the symptom scales, remission criteria and averaged regression models that define success on the clinical side — for a general readership that assumes psychiatric improvement means fewer voices.

**Potential headlines:**
- What people with psychosis count as getting better, and what their clinics count instead
- Two patients, the same symptom scores, and nothing in common about what helps them
- The questions nobody asks you after a first episode of psychosis
