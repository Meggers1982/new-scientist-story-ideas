### 1. Copying a smoker's body language makes them smoke more — and want to quit less

**Journal:** *International Journal of Psychology* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42427246 | **DOI:** 10.1002/ijop.70248
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — two experiments (one in the wild) showing a purely social, non-pharmacological driver of nicotine consumption; limited by thin reporting of sample details in the abstract.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)

**The study:** Polish researchers ran two experiments on nonverbal mimicry — the unconscious copying of gestures, posture and hand movements that makes people like each other. In a laboratory study, smokers who were subtly mimicked by a confederate smoked more cigarettes, an effect carried by increased liking for the mimicker. In a second, naturalistic study, having their smoking behaviour mimicked reduced smokers' stated desire to quit.

**Why it matters:** It reframes smoking maintenance as partly a problem of social bonding rather than only of nicotine pharmacology, and suggests the company you smoke in may be as sticky as the drug.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the cigarette break — two people falling into the same posture without noticing, and the finding that this invisible choreography is quietly making both of them smoke more. Hook: "social glue" as a relapse risk. Call Wojciech Kulesza at SWPS University, who has built a career on mimicry effects, and ask what it implies for group-based cessation programmes.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on mimicry as an underexamined lever in all consumption behaviour — drinking rounds, shared vapes, group eating, even sales technique — for a general-interest outlet or a psychology-focused magazine.

**Caveats:** Sample sizes not stated in the abstract; short-term outcomes; laboratory cigarette consumption may not map onto long-term smoking; single research group, no independent replication.

### 2. People quitting sugar report cravings, low mood and body aches that look like withdrawal

**Journal:** *Appetite* | **Published:** 1 August 2026
**PMID:** 41905576 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.appet.2026.108549
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — applies DSM-5 substance use disorder criteria in real time to sugar quitting, a genuinely contested question; observational and self-reported.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 203 New Zealand adults who wanted to quit free sugars completed five smartphone surveys a day for two weeks, reporting sugar intake and DSM-5 substance use disorder symptoms. Only 31% stayed sugar-free throughout; the commonest symptoms were craving, preoccupation and difficulty staying abstinent, with withdrawal-like low energy, body aches and low mood. Trajectory modelling split participants into a low-burden (47%) and high-burden (53%) group, with the high group predicted by baseline food-addiction scores, higher BMI, craving, anxiety and stress.

**Why it matters:** The "is sugar addictive?" argument has been fought mostly with rodent data and retrospective questionnaires; this puts moment-by-moment human symptom data into it — without settling it.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on someone in the evening slump at 8pm — the peak time participants broke, mostly on desserts and baked goods. The surprise: roughly half of people quitting sugar carry a symptom profile that ticks recognisable substance-withdrawal boxes, and their evening is the danger window. Call Simone Rodda at AUT University on whether "sugar withdrawal" deserves the label.
- **Wider angle:** A sceptic's feature for a health or food-policy outlet on why the food-addiction construct keeps failing to reach diagnostic consensus, and what would actually be needed to prove it — a different story from the symptom-diary one.

**Caveats:** Self-reported intake and symptoms; no pre-quit baseline period, so symptoms cannot be cleanly attributed to abstinence; two-week follow-up only; self-selected motivated quitters; no control group.

### 3. Brains of people recovering from heroin use fail to sync up when solving problems together

**Journal:** *Biological Psychiatry* | **Published:** 1 August 2026
**PMID:** 41941931 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.biopsych.2026.03.1007
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — first hyperscanning study of social cognition in heroin abstainers, published in a top journal; still modest N and cross-sectional.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 38 pairs of people with heroin use disorder in abstinence and 30 healthy control pairs did a turn-based group problem-solving task while both partners' brains were recorded simultaneously with functional near-infrared spectroscopy. The heroin group performed worse individually and as pairs, took their partner's perspective less, and showed reduced right inferior frontal gyrus and left orbitofrontal activity — plus disrupted interpersonal neural synchronisation between partners. The behavioural and neural deficits tracked with emotional and sleep-related protracted withdrawal symptoms.

**Why it matters:** Addiction research has focused overwhelmingly on solitary reward circuitry; this suggests the damage extends to the machinery of coordinating with another person, which is precisely what recovery — jobs, family, mutual-aid groups — demands.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the image of two brains failing to fall into step. The hook: recovery is usually measured in abstinence days, but this hints at a social deficit that persists into abstinence and could quietly undermine reintegration. Call Hongjun Duan at Shaanxi Normal University.
- **Wider angle:** A trend piece on hyperscanning itself — measuring two or more brains at once — and what "brain-to-brain synchrony" can and cannot tell us, for a neuroscience-literate outlet or a longer feature.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational — cannot separate pre-existing traits from drug effects; fNIRS measures only cortical surface activity; participants are abstinent heroin users in one region of China and may not generalise; group sizes modest.

### 4. Four out of five trading card packs don't tell buyers their odds of a rare card

**Journal:** *Journal of Behavioral Addictions* | **Published:** 18 August 2026
**PMID:** 42611551 | **DOI:** 10.1556/2006.2026.00197
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — timely audit of a gambling-like product children buy freely; a content analysis rather than a psychology experiment.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Researchers sampled 50 popular physical card pack products on sale in Hong Kong — trading card games and collectible sports cards — and checked whether the packaging disclosed the probability of getting rare cards. 80% gave no probability information or link at all; the 20% that did were incomplete, sometimes hard to find and occasionally misleading. Not one pack let a buyer work out their odds of any individual card.

**Why it matters:** Loot boxes in video games are now regulated or at least scrutinised in several countries; their physical ancestors, bought by children with pocket money, are not — despite evidence linking pack spending to problem gambling.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a child tearing open a Pokémon pack, or an adult opening a Labubu blind box. The surprise: the digital loot box debate has completely bypassed the cardboard version, which discloses even less. Call Leon Y. Xiao (City University of Hong Kong / BeClaws), the leading academic on loot-box regulation.
- **Wider angle:** A consumer-affairs investigation for a business or consumer outlet on the blind-box economy — Labubu, sneaker drops, mystery boxes — and whether existing consumer law, rather than gambling law, is the enforcement route regulators have overlooked.

**Caveats:** Single jurisdiction (Hong Kong), 50 products; no participant data, so nothing here demonstrates harm — the link to problem gambling comes from other, weaker correlational work.

### 5. Teenage gambling is falling overall — but rising among gamers and esports fans

**Journal:** *Journal of Gambling Studies* | **Published:** 14 August 2026
**PMID:** 42599567 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10899-026-10545-9
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — very large national sample and a genuinely divergent trend, though cross-sectional and self-reported.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Analysis of 60,331 Spanish adolescents from the nationwide ESTUDES surveys in 2019 and 2021. Gambling with money declined overall across the period, but increased in specific subgroups: video game players (+2.4%), esports spectators (+1.3%) and players (+1.8%), and those with internet gaming disorder (+1.0%). Being male tripled the odds of gambling (OR 3.71); among girls, a new higher-risk profile emerged defined by gaming, esports and internet gaming disorder rather than traditional gambling routes.

**Why it matters:** The headline "youth gambling is declining" masks a migration of risk into gaming culture, and the appearance of a female risk profile that existing prevention messaging is not designed for.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the counterintuitive split: the overall curve is going down while the gaming curve goes up. Frame around the girl gamer who is now a demographic that public health hasn't been looking at. Call Pilar Carrasco-Garrido at Universidad Rey Juan Carlos.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a sports or tech outlet on esports and betting — sponsorship, skin gambling, in-stream odds — and how the boundary between competitive gaming and wagering has effectively dissolved for teenagers.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional at two time points, cannot establish causation; self-reported gambling in a school survey; the 2021 wave straddles pandemic disruption, which may confound trends; Spain-specific.

### 6. Short videos cut depression in older adults through one route and raise it through another

**Journal:** *Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42374855 | **DOI:** 10.1177/21522715261458458
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — large national sample and a nicely opposed dual-pathway result; cross-sectional modelling limits causal claims.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)

**The study:** A national survey of 5,039 Chinese adults aged 50 and over used structural equation modelling to test how short-video use relates to depressive symptoms. Two opposing paths emerged: use predicted *fewer* depressive symptoms via more positive attitudes to ageing, and *more* symptoms via internet addiction. Using more different platforms was associated with higher addiction risk and weaker cognitive benefit; urban and rural patterns diverged.

**Why it matters:** It complicates the flat "screens are bad for older people" narrative — the same behaviour appears protective or harmful depending on how it is structured, not how much of it there is.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a 65-year-old scrolling Douyin. The surprise: the content may be improving how they feel about their own ageing, while the scrolling habit works against them — two effects that partly cancel out in any single-number study. Call Rui Jing at Renmin University of China.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a general-interest or ageing-focused outlet on how older adults are becoming the fastest-growing short-video demographic worldwide, and what platform design aimed at them looks like.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional — the direction of every arrow is assumed, not demonstrated; self-reported depressive symptoms and addiction; Chinese platform ecosystem may not generalise; mediation models of this kind are easy to over-read.

### 7. Thirteen years of forced addiction treatment: most people relapse, 15% are dead within six months

**Journal:** *Australian & New Zealand Journal of Psychiatry* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42017394 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00048674261438065
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — the complete record of a rarely used coercive legal power, with stark outcomes; small N and no control group.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** An audit of every admission under Victoria's Severe Substance Dependence Treatment Act from 2011 to 2023 — 70 admissions covering 60 patients. Alcohol was the substance of concern for 91%, 43% had liver disease and 78% had another psychiatric diagnosis. Six months after the end of involuntary treatment, 15% had died and 48% had returned to their previous level of use; 27.6% were abstinent or using less, and early improvement at one month was overwhelmingly predictive of that (OR 70).

**Why it matters:** Several jurisdictions are debating expanding involuntary addiction treatment; this is the first full picture of how such a power actually plays out, and the answer is: rarely used, on an extremely sick group, with a minority benefiting.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the number that stops readers: one in seven dead within six months of a treatment imposed to save their life. The honest framing is that this population is desperately unwell rather than that treatment failed. Call Yvonne Bonomo at St Vincent's Hospital Melbourne.
- **Wider angle:** A comparative policy piece for a legal or current-affairs outlet on coercive care across jurisdictions — Sweden, the Netherlands, Canadian provinces, Australian states — and the ethics of detaining someone for their own drug use.

**Caveats:** N=60 patients; no control group, so outcomes cannot be attributed to the treatment; single state, single legal framework; retrospective chart audit; population is at the extreme severity end and not representative of substance use disorders generally.

### 8. Nearly one in five students shows "maladaptive daydreaming" — and it tracks with ADHD and autism traits

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychiatric Research* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42102505 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2026.05.001
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a large sample and a reframing of a condition usually filed under dissociation; entirely self-report and cross-sectional.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 848 Turkish university students (mean age 21.5) completed measures of maladaptive daydreaming, ADHD symptoms, autistic traits, social media and gaming addiction, anxiety, depression, self-esteem, narcissism and loneliness. 18.3% scored above the maladaptive daydreaming cut-off. Low self-esteem, ADHD symptoms and autistic traits were the strongest predictors, with anxiety, social media addiction and narcissistic traits also contributing; severity fell with age and year of study.

**Why it matters:** Maladaptive daydreaming — hours lost to immersive, compulsive fantasy — has been theorised mainly as dissociation; this positions it at the intersection of neurodevelopmental traits and behaviourally reinforced habit, which changes who might be looking for it.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a student who paces their room for two hours a day inside an elaborate imagined life and is late to everything because of it. Hook: this may be less a dissociative curiosity than an under-recognised companion of ADHD and autistic traits. Call Nesrin Serkut Bulut at Marmara University.
- **Wider angle:** A cultural feature for a general-interest magazine on the online communities that named and self-diagnosed maladaptive daydreaming years before psychiatry took it seriously — a case study in patient-led nosology.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional; all measures self-reported, including ADHD and autism traits (no clinical diagnosis); 73% female; single university population; prevalence figure depends entirely on an arbitrary cut-off score.

### 9. Young people rate nicotine pouches more favourably than vapes or cigarettes

**Journal:** *Journal of Health Psychology* | **Published:** 11 August 2026
**PMID:** 42581660 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13591053261473711
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — first head-to-head comparison of the psychological determinants of three nicotine products in the same young sample; cross-sectional and Finland-specific.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 1,194 Finnish people aged 15–25 were surveyed on socioeconomic status, health literacy, theory-of-planned-behaviour beliefs, intentions, behaviour and addiction across nicotine vaping, nicotine pouches and cigarettes. Pouches were viewed most favourably of the three, and male gender was more strongly associated with pouch use than with vaping or smoking. Health literacy — but not socioeconomic status — predicted all three behaviours; affective attitudes ("does it feel good?") were the strongest correlate throughout, while instrumental attitudes ("is it useful?") predicted nothing. Friends' norms outweighed family norms, especially for smoking.

**Why it matters:** Nicotine pouches are the fastest-moving new nicotine product in Europe and this suggests prevention aimed at facts and consequences is targeting exactly the wrong kind of attitude.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the finding that rational arguments — instrumental attitudes — had zero predictive power, while gut feeling had the most. Frame around a 17-year-old with a pouch under their lip who knows all the health information and uses it anyway. Call Kyra Hamilton or Martin Hagger.
- **Wider angle:** A public-health and industry piece for a policy or business outlet on how nicotine pouches slipped into a regulatory gap across Europe, and how they are marketed — especially to young men.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and self-reported, including addiction; single country with an unusual nicotine market (Nordic snus culture); wide age band 15–25; cannot establish causation.

### 10. Trying harder to self-regulate makes short-video addiction worse — for some people

**Journal:** *Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42374874 | **DOI:** 10.1177/21522715261464753
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a "backfire effect" for self-control strategies is a strong hook, but it rests on cross-sectional moderated mediation.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Using focus groups with 169 addicted and non-addicted short-video users to define constructs, then a survey of 1,274 people, researchers tested a moderated mediation model within the I-PACE framework. Among people with low inhibitory control, greater use of self-regulatory strategies was associated with *higher* addiction severity — a possible backfire effect — while among those with high inhibitory control such strategies were largely redundant. Pathways also differed by group: addicted users were driven mainly by pursuit of positive emotion, non-addicted users by self-regulation acting as a defence.

**Why it matters:** If confirmed prospectively, it would suggest that the standard advice — set limits, monitor yourself, use screen-time tools — may be counterproductive for precisely the people who most need help.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on someone who has installed four app blockers and is scrolling more than ever. Hook: self-control advice may need to be triaged by whether the person has the inhibitory capacity to act on it. Be explicit that this is correlational. Call Xin Li at Shanghai Polytechnic University.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a technology outlet on the screen-time-tools industry — Screen Time, Digital Wellbeing, blocker apps — and the thinness of the evidence that any of them work.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and self-reported; the "backfire" is equally consistent with reverse causation (people who are struggling most try the most strategies); Chinese sample; no clinical diagnosis of addiction.

### 11. Most teenagers who self-harm stop within two years — and for boys, cutting internet use tracks with stopping

**Journal:** *Actas Españolas de Psiquiatría* | **Published:** 15 August 2026
**PMID:** 42633531 | **DOI:** 10.62641/aep.v54i4.2086
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — genuinely longitudinal with good retention and a striking remission rate; single-city sample and wide confidence intervals.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 652 first-year junior high students in Jingzhou, China were surveyed in 2021 and 585 (89.7%) followed up in 2023. Non-suicidal self-injury fell from 22.1% to 8.6%, with 82.2% of those affected at baseline having stopped. Remission was strongly predicted by falling depressive symptoms (aOR 8.43) and stable rather than worsening anxiety. Gender-stratified analysis found that among boys a reduction in internet addiction predicted remission (aOR 8.87), while among girls the depression and anxiety pathways dominated.

**Why it matters:** The dominant public narrative is of relentlessly rising adolescent self-harm; this shows high natural remission over two years, and points to different mechanisms in boys and girls.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the remission figure — four in five stopped — as a counterweight to catastrophising coverage, then the gender split in what accompanied stopping. Frame around the two-year window between ages 12 and 14. Call Xiaoping Deng at Yangtze University.
- **Wider angle:** A methodological feature for a science or health outlet on why cross-sectional prevalence snapshots systematically mislead about adolescent mental health, and what cohort data show instead.

**Caveats:** Two schools in one Chinese city; self-reported self-harm and internet addiction; very wide confidence intervals on the gender-stratified odds ratios; observational — reduced internet use may be a marker of recovery rather than a cause; follow-up spans a period of pandemic disruption.

### 12. Women who smoke are twice as likely to develop postpartum depression

**Journal:** *Archives of Women's Mental Health* | **Published:** 17 August 2026
**PMID:** 42604887 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s00737-026-01753-8
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a clear meta-analytic effect across 38 studies with a timing moderator; the bolt-on in silico genomics adds little and the design cannot address causation.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** A meta-analysis of 38 primary epidemiological studies found that women who smoke are roughly twice as likely as non-smokers to develop postpartum depression, with higher depression scores overall. Timing mattered: current and gestational smoking were more strongly linked to PPD than past smoking, while geographical and cultural setting did not moderate the effect. A companion network analysis identified shared hub genes between nicotine addiction and PPD pathways (including IL6, TNF, IL1B, AKT1, ESR1).

**Why it matters:** It sharpens a link that previous reviews called inconclusive and makes smoking status during pregnancy a candidate flag for postpartum depression risk — though nothing here shows that quitting reduces that risk.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the timing moderator: it is current and pregnancy smoking, not a smoking history, that carries the association — which points away from "smokers are just different people" and towards something active. Do not imply that quitting prevents PPD. Call Faraz Ahmad at Vellore Institute of Technology.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a health or women's-health outlet on how much maternal mental health research still treats smoking as a nuisance covariate to adjust away rather than a variable worth studying.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of observational studies — confounding by socioeconomic status, prior depression and stress is very likely; heterogeneity across 38 studies not detailed in the abstract; the genomics component is purely computational with no experimental validation.

### 13. Severe social withdrawal explains the link between autistic traits and problem gaming

**Journal:** *International Journal of Social Psychiatry* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 41742036 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00207640251405460
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — first study to test all three constructs together in a large sample; cross-sectional mediation cannot establish the sequence it implies.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 2,168 Italian university students completed measures of internet and computer game addiction, subthreshold autistic traits and hikikomori-style social withdrawal. Students classified as pathological gamers scored higher on both autistic traits and hikikomori tendencies, and mediation analysis found that withdrawal partly explained the route from autistic traits to gaming addiction, hours played and gaming-related academic problems.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that gaming may sit downstream of withdrawal rather than causing it in this group — with implications for whether you treat the gaming or the retreat from social life.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the reversal of the usual causal story parents assume: the gaming may be the symptom, not the cause, in young people with high autistic traits. Note honestly that a cross-sectional design cannot prove the ordering. Call Barbara Carpita or Liliana Dell'Osso at the University of Pisa.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a general-interest outlet on hikikomori going global — how a phenomenon first named in Japan is now being measured in Italian universities, and whether the concept survives export.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional, so the mediation direction is assumed; all self-report, including autistic traits (subthreshold, not diagnosed); university students only; single-country sample.

### 14. People with cocaine use disorder have two separate kinds of impulsivity — with different brain wiring

**Journal:** *Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology & Biological Psychiatry* | **Published:** 6 August 2026
**PMID:** 42562118 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pnpbp.2026.111883
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 4/10 — challenges the treatment of impulsivity as one thing, but the abstract omits sample size and the material is technical for a 600-word piece.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Combining self-report measures, clinical assessment and behavioural tasks with structural connectome modelling, researchers found that disinhibition in cocaine use disorder splits into two partially separable dimensions — trait disinhibition and cognitive disinhibition. These map onto largely distinct structural connectivity signatures, sharing only a single overlapping connection between the left amygdala and right medial prefrontal cortex. Cognitive disinhibition involved widely distributed connectivity across visual, somatomotor, default mode and subcortical networks; trait disinhibition involved reduced somatomotor–control connectivity and increased nodal clustering.

**Why it matters:** If "impulsivity" in addiction is at least two things with different wiring, then measuring it with one questionnaire — as most studies do — is averaging over a real distinction.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the near-total non-overlap: one shared connection between two things psychiatry calls by the same name. Frame around the person who is impulsive on a lab task but not in life, or vice versa. Call Yuanyuan Wang at Jinan University.
- **Wider angle:** A specialist piece for a neuroscience or psychiatry outlet on connectome-based predictive modelling and the transdiagnostic push to replace diagnostic labels with measurable dimensions.

**Caveats:** Sample size not stated in the abstract; cross-sectional; connectome-based predictive models frequently fail to replicate in independent samples; single site; cannot separate cause from consequence of cocaine use.

### 15. Anxious ultramarathon runners get injured more — and for women, a lot more

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** 21 August 2026
**PMID:** 42628162 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107662
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — an unexpected psychological predictor of physical injury in an extreme-endurance group; cross-sectional, self-reported, wide confidence intervals.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 495 Brazilian ultramarathon runners (mean age 44.5) completed an online survey covering training parameters, injury history, generalised anxiety and exercise addiction. Injury prevalence was 27.7%. Higher BMI, low-level anxiety (OR 1.62) and severe anxiety (OR 3.26) were all associated with injury; among women, severe anxiety carried a very large but imprecisely estimated association with injury (OR 15.92, CI 2.47–102.31), while among men only low-level anxiety reached significance.

**Why it matters:** Sports medicine models injury from load, biomechanics and recovery; this adds mental state as a candidate variable, and suggests the addiction-adjacent psychology of extreme endurance deserves proper study.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on an ultrarunner strapping up an old injury for another 100km. Hook: the strongest predictor in the data wasn't weekly mileage, it was anxiety. Be careful — recall bias could produce this pattern in reverse. Call Mabliny Thuany at State University of Pará or Volker Scheer at the Ultra Sports Science Foundation.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a sports or general-interest outlet on exercise addiction as the one compulsion society still applauds, and where endurance-sport culture draws the line between dedication and disorder.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional web survey with self-selected respondents; injury and anxiety both self-reported and retrospective (injured runners may recall or report more anxiety); the female severe-anxiety confidence interval spans nearly two orders of magnitude; exercise addiction measured but not reported as a predictor.

### 16. Exercise addiction and eating disorders look similar but come from different emotional places

**Journal:** *International Journal of Eating Disorders* | **Published:** 27 July 2026
**PMID:** 42504448 | **DOI:** 10.1002/eat.70173
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a useful dissociation between two co-occurring syndromes, but cross-sectional, sample size unreported and the authors themselves flag it as preliminary.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)

**The study:** A community sample of recreational exercisers completed measures of eating-disorder symptoms, exercise addiction symptoms, adult attachment anxiety and avoidance, and emotion-regulation difficulties. Both symptom sets were correlated, but showed partly different profiles: eating-disorder symptoms aligned more with attachment anxiety and a lack of emotional awareness, while exercise addiction symptoms aligned more with attachment *avoidance* and non-acceptance of one's own emotions.

**Why it matters:** It suggests compulsive exercise is not simply an eating disorder wearing trainers — the emotional strategy behind it (avoidant self-reliance, control through movement) may be distinct, which matters for how it is recognised.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the distinction: one pattern is about not noticing your feelings, the other about refusing to accept them. Open on a gym regular whose training is a way of not sitting with distress. Call Dalit Lev Arey at the Academic College of Tel Aviv Yaffo.
- **Wider angle:** A piece for a fitness or lifestyle outlet on the wellness industry's blind spot — how "discipline" branding rewards exactly the avoidant regulatory pattern described here.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional; sample size not stated in the abstract; community volunteers, not a clinical sample; all self-report; authors describe path analyses as exploratory and call for replication.

### 17. "Love addiction" is linked to distress and impulsivity — but only through a loss of emotional control

**Journal:** *Psychological Reports* | **Published:** 30 July 2026
**PMID:** 42533520 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00332941261473827
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a relatable construct with a specific, gender-invariant mechanism; cross-sectional self-report on a contested diagnostic category.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)

**The study:** 714 participants (mean age 26, 68% women) completed measures of psychological distress, motor impulsivity, emotion-regulation difficulties and love addiction. Both distress and impulsivity were directly associated with love addiction, and both were also indirectly associated with it through one specific pathway only — lack of emotional control — with the distress route the stronger of the two. Multigroup analysis showed the model was strictly invariant across genders.

**Why it matters:** It gives a testable mechanism to a folk concept, and the gender invariance undercuts the stereotype that compulsive romantic attachment is a distinctively female pattern.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on someone who can't stop checking whether their partner has read the message. Hook: the same model fits men and women identically, and the active ingredient is losing control of emotions rather than distress itself. Call María Sánchez-Fernández at the University of Cadiz — and ask her directly whether "love addiction" should be a clinical category at all.
- **Wider angle:** A sceptical feature for a general-interest outlet on the proliferation of behavioural addictions — love, shopping, exercise, work — and the debate over whether the addiction framework is being stretched past usefulness.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional, so mediation direction is assumed; all self-report; "love addiction" is not a recognised diagnosis and the measure's validity is contested; Spanish sample skewed female and young.

### 18. Employees who use generative AI think more creatively — until tolerance sets in

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42364395 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107324
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — extremely topical framing of AI dependence, but a small cross-sectional survey with self-reported everything.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)

**The study:** 194 Chinese knowledge workers were surveyed on generative AI use, creative self-efficacy, AI addiction (tolerance and withdrawal dimensions) and creative thinking. AI use was positively associated with creative thinking, mediated by creative self-efficacy — but the *tolerance* dimension of AI addiction was negatively associated with creative thinking, while withdrawal was not. Creative self-efficacy also negatively predicted both addiction dimensions, and satisfaction with AI use strengthened the positive path.

**Why it matters:** Not much yet, but it opens a line of enquiry: whether escalating reliance on generative AI — needing more of it to feel the same benefit — erodes the very capability it initially boosts.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the split result: the same tool appears to help and hinder the same skill, depending on whether the person has developed tolerance to it. Open on a designer who now can't start a brief without a prompt. Flag the design limits prominently. Call Wenping Zhang at Anhui University of Technology.
- **Wider angle:** A workplace feature for a business or technology outlet on "AI deskilling" — the emerging evidence on cognitive offloading and whether organisations should be measuring dependence alongside productivity.

**Caveats:** N=194, cross-sectional, single country, all self-reported including creativity; "GAI addiction" is a newly minted construct with no established validity; no objective measure of creative output; effects could easily run in the opposite direction.

### 19. Across 15 countries, conflict with others is the hub connecting internet addiction to teen mental health

**Journal:** *Psychiatry Research* | **Published:** 9 August 2026
**PMID:** 42612478 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117368
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a big, geographically underrepresented sample and a specific symptom-level target; network analyses of cross-sectional data are descriptive.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 6,204 young people aged 15–24 across 15 Eastern Mediterranean countries completed the Compulsive Internet Use Scale and the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire between June and December 2024. In gender-stratified network models, "conflict" was the most central internet-addiction domain in both sexes, and "nervous in new situations" the most influential psychological symptom. The strongest cross-domain bridge was internet-related withdrawal; at item level, preferring online engagement to being with others linked most strongly to solitary behaviour.

**Why it matters:** Most internet-addiction network research comes from East Asia and Europe; this fills a large regional gap and converges on interpersonal conflict rather than time online as the connective tissue.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is that the symptom binding compulsive internet use to distress is not hours or craving but conflict with the people around you. Frame around a household argument. Call Noha Fadl at Alexandria University.
- **Wider angle:** A global-health piece for a development or international-affairs outlet on how digital mental health research is geographically skewed, and what changes when the same instruments are run in the Middle East and North Africa.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational; recruitment method across 15 countries unclear from the abstract and likely convenience-based online sampling; all self-report; network centrality does not identify causes or intervention targets despite being routinely described that way.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** A large share of this batch treats addiction as something that happens *between* people rather than inside a reward circuit — and keeps finding that the interpersonal signal is the strongest one. Mimicking a smoker's body language makes them smoke more and want to quit less (PMID 42427246); recovering heroin users' brains fail to synchronise with a partner during joint problem-solving, with deficits tracking protracted withdrawal (PMID 41941931); across 15 Eastern Mediterranean countries, "conflict with others" — not hours online or craving — is the central hub linking compulsive internet use to adolescent distress (PMID 42612478); hikikomori-style social withdrawal mediates the route from autistic traits to problem gaming (PMID 41742036); and among Finnish 15–25s, friends' norms outweigh family norms and gut-level affective attitudes predict nicotine pouch use while rational "is it useful?" beliefs predict nothing at all (PMID 42581660). Exercise addiction's alignment with attachment *avoidance* rather than anxiety (PMID 42504448) and love addiction's routing through loss of emotional control (PMID 42533520) extend the same theme into the relational dimension of behavioural compulsions.

**Why pitch this now:** Addiction coverage and addiction policy are both still organised around the solitary brain — dopamine, craving, abstinence day counts — while a cluster of 2026 studies using new tools (dual-brain hyperscanning, symptom network models, naturalistic mimicry experiments) is independently converging on social bonds, conflict and withdrawal from others as the load-bearing variables, which points at completely different intervention targets.

**Angle:** "The social brain of addiction": a feature arguing that the field's neurocentric, individual-level model is measuring the wrong unit of analysis — following two brains in a scanner, a cigarette break, and a household argument to ask what treatment would look like if the target were the relationship rather than the receptor. Best for a neuroscience-literate general readership that has already absorbed the "addiction is a brain disease" framing and is ready to hear it complicated.

**Potential outlets:**
- **New Scientist Features** — the Mind section has the room to run the hyperscanning imagery alongside the mimicry experiment, and the "your brain isn't the unit of addiction" reframe is exactly its house contrarian-but-evidenced register.
- **BBC Future** — strong fit for a long explanatory piece on social contagion of everyday behaviours (cigarette breaks, drinking rounds, shared vapes), with the mimicry study as a concrete, reproducible hook.
- **Knowable Magazine** — built for "here's how a field is quietly changing its model," and could carry the methodological throughline of hyperscanning and symptom-network analysis without dumbing it down.
- **Undark** — would take the policy sting: if conflict and social reintegration are the active ingredients, abstinence-counting and coercive treatment regimes (PMID 42017394) are aimed at the wrong outcome.
