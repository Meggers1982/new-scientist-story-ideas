### 1. Ageing brains are better at using experience to guide attention than scientists assumed

**Journal:** *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* | **Published:** 2026 Jun 24
**PMID:** 42342549 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.tics.2026.06.002
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Strongly counterintuitive — directly overturns the widespread assumption that ageing inevitably degrades attention — with a clear societal hook around cognitive ageing; published in a high-profile review journal; loses one point because it is a review rather than primary empirical data, and another because no new human participant data are presented.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** In a review article published in Trends in Cognitive Sciences, researchers synthesised existing literature on how attentional guidance — the brain's ability to direct visual search using salience, goals, and prior experience — changes across adulthood. Rather than finding a broad decline, the review found that experience-based guidance (from short-term priming to lifetime knowledge) and goal-directed attention toward stable priorities are largely preserved in older adults once perceptual slowing is accounted for. Age-related difficulties appear to be specific: older adults struggle primarily when attention must rapidly switch between competing priorities, suggesting the deficit is in resolving conflicts between past habits and new goals rather than in attention itself.

**Why it matters:** Reframing cognitive ageing as a problem of priority-switching rather than generalised attentional decline could reshape how researchers and clinicians assess older adults' real-world functioning, from driving to workplace performance.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Most of what you know about ageing and attention is wrong, a new review suggests" The story we tell about ageing brains — that attention steadily erodes — turns out to be far too simple, and in some ways flat-out wrong. A review of the literature on visual attention across adulthood finds that older adults are surprisingly good at using accumulated experience and stable goals to guide where they look — abilities that hold up even when perceptual slowing is stripped out of the equation. The real vulnerability, researchers say, is a narrower one: difficulty rapidly reshuffling attention when past habits conflict with new demands. That reframing matters enormously, because it shifts the narrative from inevitable decline to a specific, potentially trainable bottleneck — and raises fresh questions about how we test and support older adults in fast-changing environments. Why it fits: In an ageing population anxious about cognitive decline, NS Mind readers will be genuinely surprised — and reassured — to learn that much of what looks like attentional deterioration is actually something far more specific and limited.

### 2. Your brain solves creative blocks better when you stop trying to think

**Journal:** *Behavioural Brain Research* | **Published:** 2026 Jun 25
**PMID:** 41912090 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bbr.2026.116191
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — Strong counterintuitive hook (unconscious beats conscious effort for creativity) with clean neural imaging data, but a small sample of 54 and single-center design limit confidence.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers used functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) to track brain activity in 54 participants while they completed a creative thinking task (the Alternative Uses Task) under conditions of unconscious semantic priming versus no priming. Participants were also primed with either typical or novel examples to induce varying degrees of cognitive fixation. Unconscious priming — meaning participants were exposed to idea-relevant cues without conscious awareness — significantly improved both the uniqueness and semantic distance of creative responses. Neural data showed increased prefrontal activity and stronger connectivity between the salience network and basal areas, alongside reduced fixation-related activation in temporal regions, suggesting unconscious processing helps the brain break free from habitual thinking patterns.

**Why it matters:** These findings suggest that deliberate incubation periods — stepping away from a problem — may work partly by allowing unconscious semantic processes to loosen cognitive fixation, with potential implications for how creative workplaces and education might be structured.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Your brain solves creative blocks better when you stop trying to think" Letting your mind wander — or at least go unconscious on a problem — appears to boost creative thinking more than deliberate effort, according to new brain-imaging research. A study of 54 people scanned with fNIRS found that unconscious semantic priming — exposing people to creativity-relevant cues they weren't consciously aware of — significantly improved divergent thinking scores and changed measurable brain activity in prefrontal and salience networks. The surprise: conscious priming with 'typical' examples actually entrenched fixation, whereas unconscious exposure helped people break free from it. This challenges the common assumption that more conscious effort equals better creative output, and reframes the neuroscience of the classic 'incubation effect.' Why it fits: NS Mind readers are fascinated by counterintuitive findings about how the mind works, and 'try less to create more' has immediate lifestyle resonance for anyone who has ever had a great idea in the shower.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** small sample (N<100), single-center, observational design. Small laboratory sample (N=54), single-center Chinese university setting limiting generalisability, and the artificial priming task may not reflect real-world creative challenges

### 3. Women psychiatrists on six continents describe the same microaggressions — comments on appearance, dismissed expertise, violated boundaries

**Journal:** *BMJ Mental Health* | **Published:** 2026 Jun 24
**PMID:** 42342368 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bmjment-2025-302360
**Novelty:** Included for relevance
**NS fit:** 6/10 — Strong societal and workplace relevance with a genuinely global cross-cultural dimension, but the perspective/qualitative format and very small contributor group (N=21) limit evidential weight for NS Mind's typically data-driven audience.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Twenty-one women psychiatrists and psychologists from 15 countries across six continents contributed personal experiences and perspectives to this qualitative perspective article examining microaggressions in clinical and academic psychiatry. Drawing on existing literature and collective personal accounts, the authors identified recurring patterns of gender-based discrimination including commentary on physical appearance, dismissal of professional contributions, boundary violations, and structural marginalisation. Cross-regional analysis found these patterns were remarkably consistent across diverse cultural and healthcare contexts. The authors argue that the persistence of these behaviours across regions suggests they are driven by entrenched systemic and cultural norms rather than local factors alone.

**Why it matters:** The finding that the same subtle forms of gender-based discrimination recur across six continents suggests that microaggressions in psychiatry are a structural global problem, not a cultural anomaly — with implications for workforce retention and the mental health of healthcare providers themselves.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Women psychiatrists worldwide face the same microaggressions — even as they treat patients for the damage discrimination causes" Women psychiatrists from Nigeria to Australia to El Salvador describe an almost identical set of daily indignities at work — raising the question of why the profession that studies mental harm is so slow to address it in its own ranks. A new perspective piece in BMJ Mental Health brings together 21 women psychiatrists and psychologists from 15 countries and six continents to document the microaggressions they encounter at work. Despite vast cultural differences, the patterns are strikingly uniform: comments on appearance, dismissal of clinical expertise, personal boundary violations. The piece argues this consistency is evidence of global structural failure — not isolated bad behaviour — and calls for systemic reform in one of medicine's most gender-imbalanced specialties. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are engaged by psychology of the workplace, gender, and systemic bias — and the irony of mental health professionals experiencing institutionalised discrimination is a compelling angle.

**Caveats:** small sample (N<100), self-reported outcomes, observational design.
