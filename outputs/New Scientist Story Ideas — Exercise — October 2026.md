### 1. Being told you did worse than average makes you exercise harder — and feel worse about your body

**Journal:** *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* | **Published:** 28 September 2026
**PMID:** 42805545 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychsport.2026.103269
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — pre-registered experiment with a clean trade-off result, but a student lab sample and no follow-up beyond the session.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** In a pre-registered lab experiment, 120 participants did the same no-equipment workout twice. Between trials they were told their first performance was 30% below average, 30% above average, or given no feedback. Those told they were below average improved their objective performance and exertion far more than the other groups — but also showed the largest drops in body satisfaction and the largest rises in negative mood, with both effects strongest in people who identified most closely with the comparison group.

**Why it matters:** Social-comparison nudges are built into fitness apps, leaderboards and gym classes on the assumption that they are harmlessly motivating; this is the first direct evidence that the motivational boost and a body-image cost arrive together in the same session.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the leaderboard at the end of a spin class, or the app notification telling you you're in the bottom third this week. The surprise: the feedback works — and that's the problem. Call Olivia Davies and Tegan Cruwys at the Australian National University, whose social-identity framing explains why the sting is worst among people who care most about the group they're being ranked against.
- **Wider angle:** A consumer-tech feature on the ethics of behavioral nudges in fitness and wellness products — who audits a feature that raises engagement metrics while lowering mood? Fits a technology or business outlet covering app design and "dark patterns" in health.

**Caveats:** Single-center lab study; N=120 with 40 per condition; outcomes measured immediately, so no evidence on whether either the performance gain or the body-image hit persists; student population may not generalize to older or clinical groups.

### 2. Three minutes a day beats two half-hour workouts a week

**Journal:** *Behavioral Medicine* | **Published:** 7 September 2026
**PMID:** 42704303 | **DOI:** 10.1080/08964289.2026.2726309
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — randomized, directly testable by any reader, but short and reliant on self-tracked activity.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** 142 participants were randomized to a "micro goal" (3 minutes of exercise every day plus two 20-minute sessions a week) or a standard goal (two 30-minute sessions a week) and tracked their exercise, enjoyment and ease of adherence for three weeks. The micro goal group accumulated significantly more total exercise time, and the effect was mediated by how easy people found it to stick to the goal.

**Why it matters:** The micro-habit idea is everywhere in self-help but barely tested; this is a randomized test showing the mechanism is not motivation or enjoyment but sheer ease of compliance — which has implications for how public health guidance is worded.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the arithmetic that shouldn't work — a goal of three minutes a day ends up producing *more* exercise in total than a goal built around proper workouts. Open on someone who has abandoned a gym membership twice. Worth calling Katy Tapper's group at City St George's, University of London, who ran the trial and can speak to whether the gain survives beyond three weeks.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on the evidence base — or lack of one — behind the habit-formation industry: atomic habits, streaks, two-minute rules. Which of these popular claims have ever been randomized? Good fit for a long-read in a general-interest magazine or a behavioral-science podcast.

**Caveats:** Self-reported daily exercise duration; three-week follow-up only, so no evidence on durable habit formation; no objective activity measurement; participants knew which condition they were in.

### 3. We judge people's health habits as moral failings only when we think they hurt someone else

**Journal:** *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* | **Published:** 3 November 2025 (issue October 2026)
**PMID:** 41185114 | **DOI:** 10.1177/01461672251372823
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — five studies, experimental manipulation and a clean theoretical result; limited by largely online samples.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Across five studies with 2,055 participants, researchers tested why people treat health behaviors — smoking, eating well, exercising — as moral rather than merely practical. Perceived *interpersonal* harm, the sense that your poor health hurts other people, consistently predicted moralization, and experimentally increasing a behavior's apparent interpersonal harmfulness caused participants to moralize it. Disgust, a leading rival explanation, had no unique effect.

**Why it matters:** It offers a testable account of why the gym-going, clean-eating virtue narrative has the grip it does, and why health campaigns framed around burdening others can tip into stigma.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Why does skipping the gym feel like a minor sin? Open on the moral vocabulary people use about exercise — "being good", "earning" a dessert — and the finding that it tracks perceived harm to others, not disgust. Call Kurt Gray (Ohio State) or Sophie Pratt and A. Janet Tomiyama (UCLA); Tomiyama's weight-stigma work gives the piece its sharpest edge.
- **Wider angle:** A public-health policy story: campaigns that emphasize the cost of unhealthy behavior to families, the health service or society are, on this evidence, directly manufacturing moral condemnation. Suited to a health-policy or current-affairs outlet.

**Caveats:** Mostly online samples of uncertain demographic breadth; vignette-based manipulations measure judgments, not real-world behavior toward stigmatized people; cross-cultural generalizability untested.

### 4. Ten minutes of "exercise snacks" a day lowers office workers' blood pressure and stress

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** 29 September 2026
**PMID:** 42810116 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107895
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a real 12-week workplace RCT with objective blood-pressure outcomes, but only 52 participants and no blinding.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** 52 office workers were randomized to 12 weeks of "Exercise-Snacks" — three to six daily bouts of stair climbing, brisk walking, squats, push-ups or stretching lasting 1–3 minutes each, around 10 minutes a day in total — or to no intervention. By week 12 the intervention group's systolic blood pressure had fallen 4.6 mmHg and diastolic 2.4 mmHg, with perceived stress down 2.5 points on the PSS-10. BMI did not change.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the psychological and cardiovascular benefits of breaking up sedentary work do not require a lunchtime workout or any weight loss — a genuinely low-friction target for employers, though one small trial is not a basis for policy.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Pair this with the micro-goals trial above as evidence that the smallest credible dose of exercise is doing real work. Open on the stairwell at 3 p.m. The hook: the blood-pressure drop arrived with no change in body weight at all, which undercuts the assumption that exercise benefits run through the scales. Contact Kai Liu at Korea University.
- **Wider angle:** An occupational-health piece on whether the hybrid-work era has quietly made the standing-desk debate obsolete — the intervention that actually interrupts sitting is movement, not furniture. Fits a business or workplace-focused outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (52) and single-site; no attention-control group, so expectancy effects cannot be excluded; perceived stress is self-reported; adherence monitoring not described in the abstract; short follow-up with no post-intervention measurement.

### 5. For older adults, being shown how to exercise matters more for mood than how much they exercise

**Journal:** *Depression and Anxiety* | **Published:** 28 September 2026
**PMID:** 42807680 | **DOI:** 10.1155/da/7595212
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — large national longitudinal sample, but observational and self-reported throughout.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Using three waves of the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (2020–2023), researchers analyzed 5,643 adults aged 60–75. Both physical activity volume and receiving exercise guidance were associated with fewer depressive symptoms, but the association for sheer activity volume was inconsistent across subgroups, while guidance held up more robustly — and was more strongly conditioned on how often people used the internet and how digitally capable they felt.

**Why it matters:** It hints that the mood benefit older people get from exercise may depend less on minutes accumulated than on whether anyone is showing them what to do — and that digital exclusion may be quietly gating access to that guidance.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is the ranking: volume of activity was the shakier predictor, guidance the sturdier one. Open on a rural respondent with no smartphone and no local class. Call the Soochow University team; worth pressing them on whether "guidance" is a proxy for social contact rather than instruction.
- **Wider angle:** A demographics and digital-divide story — as exercise advice migrates to apps and video, who is left out, and what does that cost in later-life mental health? Fits an aging, technology or international-development outlet.

**Caveats:** Observational — cannot establish causation; physical activity, exercise guidance, digital proficiency and depressive symptoms all self-reported; Chinese young-old population may not generalize; residual confounding by health status and social contact is likely.

### 6. Fitness Instagram is linked to more exercise — but only in people who don't see themselves as exercisers

**Journal:** *Journal of Health Psychology* | **Published:** 28 September 2026
**PMID:** 42803216 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13591053261484122
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — an interesting moderation result readers will recognize, but cross-sectional and self-reported.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** In a cross-sectional survey of emerging adults, higher fitness-related Instagram use was associated with more moderate-to-vigorous and total physical activity. But the association split by self-concept: among people with an "exercise self-schema" — those who already see being an exerciser as central to who they are — fitness-related Instagram use showed no relationship with activity at all. The positive association was carried entirely by people without that self-image.

**Why it matters:** It complicates the standard worry that fitness content mainly preaches to the converted or demoralizes everyone else, suggesting instead that its only measurable correlate with activity sits in the group least likely to be targeted by it.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on someone who has never thought of themselves as "sporty" doom-scrolling gym reels. The hook: the people for whom fitness content appears to do nothing are the committed exercisers. Call Sarah Grace and Daheia Barr-Anderson at the University of Minnesota, and ask the obvious causal question out loud — does the feed drive the activity, or the reverse?
- **Wider angle:** A media-studies feature on how fitness influencer content is actually consumed, and whether platforms could be designed to reach non-identifying exercisers. Suited to a digital-culture or sports-media outlet.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional — direction of the association is indeterminate, and reverse causation is highly plausible; self-reported physical activity and Instagram use; sample limited to emerging adults; no measure of content type.

### 7. Eating disorder clinicians say they lack any guidance on the compulsive exercise they treat daily

**Journal:** *Scandinavian Journal of Psychology* | **Published:** 14 April 2026 (issue October 2026)
**PMID:** 41982074 | **DOI:** 10.1111/sjop.70102
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a stark, quotable treatment-gap finding, but a modest survey from one country.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** A cross-sectional survey of 102 clinicians working in Swedish eating disorder services found that although most regard maladaptive exercise as a symptom that worsens outcomes, few had received formal training and only moderate confidence in managing it. Assessment was routine but structured intervention was rare; just 42% had access to any clinic-level practice or informal protocol, while 84% said national guidance was needed.

**Why it matters:** Compulsive exercise is one of the most common and most dangerous features of restrictive eating disorders, and this suggests it is being routinely detected and then left untreated for want of any agreed method.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the 42% figure. Open on a clinician who records a patient's compulsive running at intake and then has nothing in the protocol to do about it. Call Elin Monell at Karolinska Institutet or Elisabeth Forsén Mantilla at the Swedish School of Sport and Health Sciences; the companion Italian work on acute "urges to move" (PMID 42490250) gives the piece a second limb.
- **Wider angle:** A health-services investigation into how exercise is handled inside eating disorder treatment across the UK or US — including whether patients are told to stop entirely, which clinicians increasingly question. Fits a health-reporting outlet or a specialist psychiatry publication.

**Caveats:** Small survey (N=102), single country, self-reported knowledge and practice; responders may be those most interested in the topic; no patient outcomes measured.

### 8. "Realistic" postpartum workout images still leave new mothers feeling worse about their bodies

**Journal:** *Journal of Health Psychology* | **Published:** 22 February 2026 (issue October 2026)
**PMID:** 41724692 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13591053261419659
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a genuinely unexpected null-plus-backfire, but tiny and exploratory.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers created imagery depicting safe, appropriate postpartum exercise — deliberately "realistic" rather than aspirational — and showed it to 33 women within 12 months of giving birth, with 21 followed up in interviews. Women reported body dissatisfaction *after* viewing the imagery, and asked for imagery that was more realistic still. Body dissatisfaction and activity levels did not differ between women who had and had not retained weight.

**Why it matters:** The standard fix for harmful fitness imagery — make it more representative — did not work as intended here, suggesting the problem for postpartum women may be the act of being shown an exercising body at all rather than which body is shown.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the "MomBod" of the study's title and the gap between what new mothers say they want to see and how they feel once they see it. The hook is the backfire against the obvious remedy. Call Iris Lesser at the University of the Fraser Valley, and ask what imagery, if any, cleared the bar.
- **Wider angle:** A reported feature on the postpartum fitness industry — six-week "bounce-back" programs, pelvic-floor neglect, and who actually supplies new mothers with exercise advice. Fits a parenting or women's-health outlet.

**Caveats:** Very small N (33 quantitative, 21 interviews); no control imagery condition; cross-sectional exposure with no follow-up; self-selected Canadian sample; qualitative findings not generalizable.

### 9. Replaying a relaxation exercise to sleeping nightmare sufferers changes their sleep spindles — but not their nightmares

**Journal:** *Journal of Sleep Research* | **Published:** 9 September 2026
**PMID:** 42714131 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jsr.70443
**Novelty:** Failed replication
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a well-designed, blinded, crossover null that deflates a fashionable idea; limited by N=25 and two lab nights.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Twenty-five people experiencing at least one nightmare a week practiced a deep-breathing relaxation exercise for a week while paired with a distinctive odor. In a randomized, blinded crossover design, that odor or a control odor was then wafted at them across two laboratory nights. The reactivation did not reduce the high-frequency beta or gamma EEG activity thought to index cortical hyperarousal, and nightmare symptoms were unchanged — though it did unexpectedly reduce sleep spindle count and density.

**Why it matters:** Targeted memory reactivation during sleep has been widely reported as a way to strengthen learning and soften emotional memories; this is a careful test in a clinical population where it simply did not do what the model predicted.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Scent-cued learning during sleep is one of the most seductive findings in sleep science. Open on the lab, the odor dispenser and a volunteer who still woke from a nightmare. Lead with the honest negative result and the orphan finding — fewer spindles — that nobody can yet explain. Call Ines Wilhelm's group at the University of Luebeck.
- **Wider angle:** A broader feature on the replication record of sleep-based interventions, from targeted memory reactivation to acoustic slow-wave stimulation to sleep-learning apps: which effects survive blinding? Fits a science long-read or a neuroscience-focused outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (25), predominantly young women; only two laboratory nights of reactivation, which the authors themselves flag as likely insufficient; nightmare symptoms self-reported; single-center.

### 10. Teaching exercise through "feel it" cues beats instruction by demonstration on fitness and movement quality

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** 28 September 2026
**PMID:** 42804848 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107890
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a 16-week randomized trial with objective kinematics, but a narrow sample and a loosely defined intervention.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** 63 female university students aged 18–25 were randomized to 32 sessions over 16 weeks of either conventional aerobics instruction or embodied cognition-based training emphasizing exploratory learning and sensorimotor awareness. Both improved on every fitness measure, but the embodied group gained significantly more in trunk and abdominal endurance, upper-body strength, agility and overall performance, and showed better joint kinematics — notably greater ankle range of motion and reduced knee flexion.

**Why it matters:** It is an unusually long randomized test of the claim that how an instructor directs attention — to internal sensation rather than to copying a model — changes what the body physically learns.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The gym-class question nobody tests: does it matter whether the instructor says "watch me" or "notice what your ankle is doing"? Open on 16 weeks of identical aerobics routines taught two ways, with video motion capture to settle it. Call Maryam Ghorbani and Rasoul Yaali at Kharazmi University, and press on how the instruction scripts actually differed.
- **Wider angle:** A sports-science feature on the long-running internal-versus-external focus of attention debate in motor learning, and what coaches of everyone from rehab patients to elite athletes currently do with it. Fits a sports or physiotherapy specialist outlet.

**Caveats:** Young female university students only — unlikely to generalize; single-center; instructors not blinded and likely differed in enthusiasm as well as method; kinematics extracted from 2D video software; no retention test after training ended.

### 11. Prescribing exercise for pleasure gets people back to the gym — but not via the motivations theory predicts

**Journal:** *Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology* | **Published:** 22 September 2026
**PMID:** 42772728 | **DOI:** 10.1123/jsep.2025-0349
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a genuine theoretical puzzle, but a small ancillary analysis with a two-week intervention.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** 46 insufficiently active adults (mean age 32) were randomized to three supervised gym sessions over two weeks following either standard guideline-based prescription or a pleasure-oriented version tailored to intensity preference, self-selected intensity and repeated affective monitoring. Both groups improved on intention, habit, perceived choice, competence, intrinsic motivation and vitality — with no significant difference between them, implying the parent trial's increase in exercise frequency ran through some other, unidentified mechanism.

**Why it matters:** Hedonic approaches to exercise prescription are a fast-growing idea in behavior change; this says the effect may be real while the textbook explanation for it is wrong.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** An honest negative mechanism paper: making exercise feel nicer got people exercising more, but none of the motivational constructs it was supposed to work through moved. Open on a first-time gym user being asked, mid-set, how they feel. Call Diogo Teixeira at Lusófona University.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on the quiet crisis in behavior-change theory — interventions that work while their mediators refuse to budge, across diet, smoking and exercise research. Fits a specialist psychology or research-methods publication.

**Caveats:** Small N (46); ancillary secondary analysis of a larger trial and not powered for these outcomes; two-week intervention with three sessions; all mediators self-reported; null results from an underpowered design do not rule out real effects.

### 12. Team sport, solo spinning or karate — the mood lift after exercise looks much the same

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** 27 September 2026
**PMID:** 42801814 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107908
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a useful real-world time-course of post-exercise mood, but the headline comparison is explicitly underpowered and the authors say so.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** 60 amateur athletes in three habitual training groups — cheerleading (interactive), spinning (coactive) and karate (hybrid) — had affect measured before, 15 minutes after and 60 minutes after a session. Positive affect rose and negative affect fell after exercise in all three, with arousal dropping below baseline an hour later while felt valence stayed elevated. No significant differences by sport type emerged, but the authors are unusually candid that the study could only detect large effects and that equivalence tests failed for all 24 contrasts.

**Why it matters:** The practical finding is about timing rather than type: the pleasant feeling outlasts the physiological arousal by at least an hour, which is a tidy, checkable claim about what exercise actually does to mood.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the hour after a workout — arousal already below where you started, but mood still lifted. Treat the sport-type comparison as the non-story the authors themselves say it is. Call Attila Szabo at ELTE, whose transparency about power and equivalence makes him a good interviewee on how null results should be reported.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-focused piece for a research or academic-publishing outlet on authors who refuse to launder an underpowered null as "no difference" — this abstract is a model of the genre.

**Caveats:** Small N (60) and non-randomized, with groups differing in age, sex, experience and session intensity; predominantly female sample with menstrual-cycle phase unrecorded; self-reported affect; explicitly underpowered for the main between-group comparison.

### 13. Confidence in runners builds in one direction: believing in yourself shapes what you think others think of you

**Journal:** *Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology* | **Published:** 9 September 2026
**PMID:** 42722364 | **DOI:** 10.1123/jsep.2025-0226
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a well-powered longitudinal design with a clear directional result, but a narrow psychological construct that is hard to make vivid.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** 350 runners completed surveys at three points over two months, analyzed with a random-intercept cross-lagged panel model — a design that separates within-person change from stable between-person differences. Self-efficacy at Time 1 predicted relation-inferred self-efficacy (your belief about how capable others think you are) at Time 2, and indirectly at Time 3. The reverse path did not hold, and social identification with the running group neither predicted nor followed from efficacy beliefs despite correlating with them at every time point.

**Why it matters:** It is the first longitudinal evidence on the direction of this loop, and it suggests the common coaching intuition that group belonging builds confidence may be a within-moment correlation rather than a process that unfolds over weeks.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The counterintuitive bit is what *didn't* move — feeling part of the running club went along with confidence but never preceded it. Open on a Parkrun. Call Samuel Stephen at the University of Calgary or Pete Coffee at Heriot-Watt, and ask whether two months is long enough for identity effects to show.
- **Wider angle:** A methods and sports-psychology piece on what happens when cross-lagged panel models are rerun with random intercepts — a quiet statistical correction that has been deflating a lot of previously confident directional claims across psychology. Fits a specialist psychology outlet.

**Caveats:** All self-reported; observational, so the cross-lagged path is suggestive rather than causal; two-month window may be too short; runners who volunteer for three surveys are a self-selected group; no behavioral or performance outcome.

### 14. Most people with hard-to-treat epilepsy or dissociative seizures do no exercise at all

**Journal:** *Epilepsy & Behavior* | **Published:** 5 September 2026
**PMID:** 42700754 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.yebeh.2026.111255
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a striking inactivity statistic in a rarely measured group, but the paper is primarily instrument development.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Researchers built a seizure-specific physical activity questionnaire (QESA) and short exercise and perceived-fitness scales, then applied them to 240 inpatients — 146 with epilepsy, 94 with functional/dissociative seizures with or without epilepsy — most of whom were not seizure-free and two-thirds of whom had psychiatric comorbidities. Physical activity was very low: 60% reported none at work and 41% none in leisure time. Strikingly, people with epilepsy exercised *less* than those with functional/dissociative seizures (57.2% versus 42.6% reporting no exercise).

**Why it matters:** Exercise is one of the few interventions with evidence across both seizure control and the depression that so often accompanies it, yet this population appears almost entirely sedentary — and the within-group difference hints that fear of seizures, not physical incapacity, may be doing the work.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a patient who stopped swimming, then stopped cycling, then stopped walking far from home. The hook is that the group whose seizures are not epileptic in origin were the *more* active ones — suggesting the barrier is as much perceived risk and medical caution as pathology. Call Anne Hagemann at the Bethel Epilepsy Center.
- **Wider angle:** A disability and health-inequality story about how risk-averse medical advice can produce a second, chronic health problem — sedentary behavior — in people already managing a neurological condition. Fits a health-reporting or disability-focused outlet.

**Caveats:** Single-center inpatient sample, likely skewed toward severe and refractory cases; all activity self-reported via a newly developed instrument with only fair-to-good retest reliability (ICC 0.54–0.75); cross-sectional baseline data; no comparison with the general population.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- *Volume is the wrong unit.* The previous digest's dose ceiling for mind-body exercise in cancer patients (PMID 42412001) and the non-monotonic activity–suicidal-ideation curve in 214,218 Korean teenagers (PMID 42605073) are now joined by three studies pushing the same way from the low end: "Three minutes a day beats two half-hour workouts a week" (PMID 42704303), "Ten minutes of 'exercise snacks' a day lowers office workers' blood pressure and stress" (PMID 42810116), and "For older adults, being shown how to exercise matters more for mood than how much they exercise" (PMID 42807680), in which activity volume was the shakier predictor of depressive symptoms and received guidance the sturdier one.
- *Nulls published honestly by the people who had most to lose.* Last run's self-replication failure on exercise and retrieval-induced forgetting (PMID 42579016), the overturning of dynamic-norm messaging (PMID 42615122) and the interoception null (PMID 42526739) have close company here: the blinded crossover showing scent-cued relaxation reactivation does nothing for nightmares (PMID 42714131), the pleasure-prescription trial whose mediators refused to move (PMID 42772728), and an affect study whose authors state outright that equivalence tests failed for all 24 contrasts (PMID 42801814).
- *Exercise as a clinical blind spot.* The ISBD position statement conceding how thin the bipolar evidence base is (PMID 42545207) now has two concrete analogues: Swedish eating disorder clinicians who detect compulsive exercise but have no protocol for it (PMID 41982074), and the finding that most inpatients with refractory epilepsy or dissociative seizures do no exercise at all (PMID 42700754).

**Advances**
- Workplace exercise moves from perk to protocol: the previous digest's ClassPass trial in psychiatry residents shifted anxiety but not burnout (PMID 42362858); the new 12-week exercise-snacks RCT (PMID 42810116) delivers objective blood-pressure falls with no weight change, shifting the question from what employers buy to how the working day is broken up.
- The fitness-technology thread deepens. Previously it was readiness scores being deliberately overridden (PMID 42034345) and momentary lapse triggers (PMID 40415394); now it is who the technology reaches and at what cost — fitness Instagram correlating with activity only in people without an exercise self-schema (PMID 42803216), digital proficiency gating access to exercise guidance in older Chinese adults (PMID 42807680), and leaderboard-style social comparison raising effort while lowering body satisfaction (PMID 42805545).

**Counterpoints**
- The social-ingredient story gets trimmed. The 28-year Norwegian cohort suggested health behaviors buy company and reduce later loneliness (PMID 42544718); this batch finds social identification with a running group never preceded self-efficacy over two months (PMID 42722364), and no difference in post-exercise mood between interactive, coactive and hybrid sports (PMID 42801814) — though that comparison is explicitly underpowered.
- Previously, psychological nudges mostly underperformed (PMID 42615122, PMID 42520720). The new social-comparison experiment (PMID 42805545) shows one that works on objective performance — the catch is the simultaneous body-image hit, which relocates the problem from efficacy to ethics.

**New themes**
- Moralization: why health behaviors are judged as virtue or sin tracks perceived harm to other people, not disgust (PMID 41185114) — no antecedent in the previous digest.
- Instruction quality as an outcome variable: 16 weeks of identical aerobics taught with internal-sensation cues beat demonstration-based teaching on strength, agility and joint kinematics (PMID 42804848), pairing with the older-adult guidance finding (PMID 42807680).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch converge on the psychological packaging of exercise — the feedback, imagery and moral framing wrapped around it — and find that the packaging carries a measurable cost that nobody is auditing. Being told you performed below average improved objective effort while producing the largest drop in body satisfaction in the same session (PMID 42805545); deliberately "realistic" postpartum workout imagery still left new mothers feeling worse about their bodies (PMID 41724692); health behaviors get moralized specifically when they look harmful to other people, which is exactly how campaigns are framed (PMID 41185114); fitness Instagram correlates with activity only among people who don't already see themselves as exercisers (PMID 42803216) — and at the clinical end, eating disorder clinicians say they routinely assess compulsive exercise and then have nothing to do about it, with only 42% having access to any protocol (PMID 41982074).

**Why pitch this now:** Social comparison is now a default design feature of fitness apps, gym classes and workplace wellness programs, and public health messaging increasingly leans on the burden-to-others frame — both at the moment when the first direct evidence arrives that the motivational gain and the body-image cost are the same event, and when the clinicians who treat the downstream casualties say they have no agreed method.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the unmeasured side-effects of exercise motivation — tracing one design decision (the leaderboard, the "realistic" fitness photo, the "don't burden the NHS" campaign line) from the lab experiment through the app to the eating disorder clinic that has no protocol for what arrives. For general readers who use a fitness app, plus the product and public-health people who build these features.
