### Study 1: Insomnia on top of sleep apnea is linked to a sharply higher risk of dementia
**PMID:** 42640940 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Core facts check out: TriNetX US network, first OSA diagnosis 2016–2019, insomnia within 90 days, 51,043 vs 51,043 after propensity-score matching, HR 1.34 (1.24–1.46), subtype elevations (vascular, DLB, AD), and risk separation within the first year.

**Issue A. CPAP framing in the pitch** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Millions of people lie awake despite their CPAP machine, and this is the first large study to ask what that combination means for the brain."
- **Abstract says:** COMISA was defined by diagnostic codes only ("COMISA was defined as an insomnia diagnosis within 90 days of the OSA index date"); no treatment or CPAP data are mentioned.
- **Problem:** The hook builds the scene around CPAP-treated apnea, but the cohort is defined by newly diagnosed OSA with no information on whether anyone was treated. The digest's own caveat notes "no data on treatment adherence," so the pitch runs slightly ahead of the evidence.
- **Suggested fix:** "Millions of people diagnosed with sleep apnea also can't sleep, and this is the first large study to ask what that combination means for the brain — though the records contain no information on who was actually treated."

**Issue B. Named contact** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call Chung-Yao Hsu's group at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital"
- **Abstract says:** "Hsu CY(6)(7). … Department of Neurology, Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Chang Gung University College of Medicine, Chiayi, Taiwan."
- **Problem:** Institution and initials match the corresponding author, but the full first name is unverifiable from the abstract alone (and a differently affiliated Chung-Yao Hsu publishes in Taiwanese epilepsy research). Worth confirming before the pitch goes out.
- **Suggested fix:** "Call the corresponding author, C.-Y. Hsu, in the neurology department at Chiayi Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (confirm full name)."

### Study 2: People with severe mental illness are diagnosed with dementia far younger than everyone else
**PMID:** 42013686 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Ns (176,870 SMI; 694,086 total; 517,216 controls by subtraction), prevalence figures (3.49% vs 0.36%), the 30–39 OR of 23.21 (13.74–39.19) and the stroke peak at 40–49 (OR 2.99) all match. Cross-sectional design and pharma COIs are correctly flagged in Caveats.

**Issue A. Pitch hook rests on relative odds with no absolute risk** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Lead with the number that shouldn't be there — a 23-fold odds ratio for dementia in people in their thirties."
- **Abstract says:** "Dementia prevalence was 3.49% (range, 0.33% at ages 20-29 to 39.51% at 90-99 years) in SMIs and 0.36% (0.02%-17.7%) in controls."
- **Problem:** At young ages the absolute prevalence in the SMI group is a fraction of 1%, so a 23-fold odds ratio describes a large multiple of a very small number. The digest never gives the young-age absolute figures, and a reader could reasonably infer that dementia is common in thirty-somethings with severe mental illness.
- **Suggested fix:** "Lead with the number that shouldn't be there — a 23-fold odds ratio for dementia at ages 30–39 — while being explicit that the absolute prevalence at those ages is well under 1%, so this is a large multiple of a small number."

**Issue B. Headline implies age at diagnosis was measured** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "People with severe mental illness are diagnosed with dementia far younger than everyone else"
- **Abstract says:** "Weighted age-stratified prevalence was calculated and logistic regression models estimated age-specific odds ratios"; "the strongest association observed at younger ages".
- **Problem:** This is a cross-sectional prevalence study; it reports age-stratified odds ratios, not age at diagnosis. The headline asserts an outcome (younger age at diagnosis) the study did not measure.
- **Suggested fix:** "The dementia gap between people with severe mental illness and everyone else is widest in the young"

### Study 3: The politics of the state you grew up in is linked to your dementia risk 50 years later
**PMID:** 41109848 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=6,410, ages 65–80 in the 2000 HRS wave, the definition of state policy liberalism, discrete-time hazard models, and the 2000–2016 window are all accurate, as is the direction of the association.

**Issue A. Birth-cohort arithmetic in the pitch** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Open on a cohort member who was 15 in the 1950s and whose state legislature was, without knowing it, setting something in motion."
- **Abstract says:** "a nationally representative sample of 6,410 U.S. adults ages 65 to 80 in the 2000 wave of the Health and Retirement Study"
- **Problem:** People aged 65–80 in 2000 were born roughly 1920–1935, so they turned 15 between about 1935 and 1950 — the Depression and wartime years, not the 1950s.
- **Suggested fix:** "Open on a cohort member who was 15 in the late 1930s or 1940s and whose state legislature was, without knowing it, setting something in motion."

**Issue B. Coverage window vs actual publication** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Published:** September 2026 … **Coverage window:** Last 30 days"
- **Abstract says:** "J Health Soc Behav. 2026 Sep;67(3):393-413. doi: 10.1177/00221465251371065. Epub 2025 Oct 18."
- **Problem:** The print issue date is correct, but the paper went online in October 2025 — nearly a year before this run. Prior coverage is therefore more likely than the "no recent NS.com coverage" check suggests, and an editor should know the finding isn't new.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Published:** September 2026 print issue (online 18 October 2025 — check for earlier coverage)"

### Study 4: The claim that caring for someone with dementia shortens your life may rest on one study
**PMID:** 42721295 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The characterization of the paper's argument — single-study origin, opposing population-based findings, and the helping/witnessing distinction — matches the abstract closely, and Caveats correctly identify it as a non-empirical reframing.

**Issue A. Date attached to the original study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "may be built on a single 1999 paper that later work did not replicate"
- **Abstract says:** "this claim originated from a single study and multiple other population-based studies have found the opposite"
- **Problem:** The abstract never dates or names the originating study. Unverifiable from abstract only; the 1999 attribution needs the full text before it appears in a pitch.
- **Suggested fix:** "may be built on a single study that later work did not replicate (confirm the specific citation in the full text)"

**Issue B. "Live longer" overstates "reduced mortality"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "multiple subsequent population-based studies have found the opposite — that caregivers live longer than non-caregivers"
- **Abstract says:** "multiple other population-based studies have found the opposite: that caregiving is associated with reduced mortality"
- **Problem:** "Live longer" reads as an established fact about caregiving; the abstract reports an association with reduced mortality, which the digest's own caveat attributes partly to selection effects.
- **Suggested fix:** "multiple subsequent population-based studies have found the opposite — that caregiving is associated with lower mortality"

**Issue C. NS fit score for a no-new-data argument paper** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — a widely believed "fact" challenged head-on, though this is an argument paper rather than new data."
- **Abstract says:** "We argue that the disconnect between what is widely accepted and what the data actually show arises because…"; "we present an integrated framework and call for systematic investigation".
- **Problem:** A 7 implies evidentiary weight the paper does not carry: no new data, no systematic review, no meta-analysis. The self-flagged limitation should pull the score down rather than sit alongside it.
- **Suggested fix:** "**NS fit:** 6/10 — a widely believed 'fact' challenged head-on, but a position paper with no new data or systematic synthesis."

### Study 5: A leak of spinal fluid physically distorts the brain's memory and behavior regions
**PMID:** 42665818 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=96 SIH patients pre/post surgical closure, 61 healthy adults scanned twice, AI-assisted diffeomorphic registration, von Mises deviatoric strain, underpowered cognitive analyses, retrospective single-center design — all accurate.

**Issue A. Strain range rounded upward** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Temporal poles, hippocampus and basal forebrain showed roughly 9–10% deviatoric strain"
- **Abstract says:** "temporal poles (right/ left: 8.6/ 8.7%), hippocampus (9.0/ 9.8%), and basal forebrain (9.9/ 9.8%) (p < 0.001 for all)"
- **Problem:** The temporal pole values (8.6/8.7%) fall below the stated range. Small, but the digest presents a tidier number set than the paper.
- **Suggested fix:** "Temporal poles, hippocampus and basal forebrain showed roughly 9–10% deviatoric strain (8.6–9.9% across regions and hemispheres)"

### Study 6: Creativity depends on a gradient running across the front of the brain
**PMID:** 41614679 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The ECOCAPTURE attribution, the bvFTD-as-model rationale, VBM plus resting-state connectivity and gradient mapping, the rostromedial/rostrolateral split, and the gradient-range-predicts-creativity finding are all faithful to the abstract. The missing patient N is correctly flagged.

**Issue A. Dissociation stated more cleanly than the paper does** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It splits creativity into two dissociable components with distinct neural addresses" / headline: "Creativity depends on a gradient running across the front of the brain"
- **Abstract says:** "we found a critical role for the rostromedial PFC in generating remote semantic associations and for the rostrolateral PFC in combining semantic associates, while both regions were critical for creative abilities."
- **Problem:** The abstract explicitly reports that *both* regions were critical for creative ability overall, so the dissociation applies to the two sub-processes, not to creativity itself. The headline verb "depends on" also hardens a correlational finding (gradient range *predicts* creative ability).
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Creativity is predicted by a gradient running across the front of the brain." Body: "It splits two sub-processes of creativity — generating remote associations and combining them — onto adjacent regions, though both regions mattered for overall creative ability."

### Study 7: A bad mood week makes people with mild cognitive impairment test worse than they really are
**PMID:** 40859820 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

N=123 (54 MCI-AD, 69 MCI-LB), ACE-R and GDS-15, repeated annual assessment, the 2-point/1-point relationship, the absent trait-level association and the absent trajectory modification are all correct.

**Issue A. Causal headline contradicts the digest's own caveat** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "A bad mood week makes people with mild cognitive impairment test worse than they really are"
- **Abstract says:** "Within-person variations in depressive symptom severity are associated with acute cognitive performance differences." The digest's own Caveats: "the direction of the within-person link cannot be fully resolved."
- **Problem:** "Makes … test worse" asserts causation and direction from an observational, concurrent-measurement design — the exact thing the caveat says cannot be resolved. "A bad mood week" also invents a timescale; assessments were annual and depressive symptoms were measured concurrently with cognition, not over a week.
- **Suggested fix:** "When mood dips, people with mild cognitive impairment score lower on memory tests at the same visit"

**Issue B. Reversibility presented as established** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "and that the score will bounce back if the mood does" / "Two points on a depression questionnaire buys you one point off your memory test score — and it is reversible."
- **Abstract says:** "Cognitive scores derived during active depressive periods may underestimate longer-term cognitive capabilities. Treating depressive symptoms in MCI may clarify underlying cognitive performance capacity".
- **Problem:** The study measured a state-based association; it did not treat depression or demonstrate score recovery. "Will bounce back" and "it is reversible" state as fact what the authors offer as a possibility.
- **Suggested fix:** "and raises the possibility — untested here — that scores recover as mood does" / "…and the authors suggest treating the depressive symptoms may reveal a truer score."

**Issue C. Point estimate presented without its uncertainty** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a 2-point rise in depression score corresponded to roughly a 1-point fall in cognitive score at the same visit"
- **Abstract says:** "a 2-point increase in depressive score explained a 1-point decrease in cognitive score, on average (point estimate -0.56, 95% credibile interval (CrI) -1.05 to -0.08)"
- **Problem:** Accurate as a point estimate, but the credible interval runs almost to zero, so the effect could be roughly a tenth of that. Pitching the exchange rate as a crisp "two points buys one point" oversells the precision.
- **Suggested fix:** "a 2-point rise in depression score corresponded on average to about a 1-point fall in cognitive score at the same visit, though the credible interval (−1.05 to −0.08) leaves the size of that effect quite uncertain"

### Study 8: Dementia carers' hearts fall into rhythm with the person they care for
**PMID:** 42398884 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=148 dyads, ages 22–86, 27% Black, 79% female, 63% spousal, 5-day EMA with six surveys a day and 24-hour heart rate monitoring, the stability-and-influence synchrony result, and the tension-by-race pattern all match. The 40-dyad Black subgroup figure is a correct derivation of 27% of 148.

**Issue A. Temporal resolution overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the two hearts track each other minute by minute"
- **Abstract says:** "past moment heart rate of one dyad member was associated with current moment heart rate of the other dyad member"; caregivers "completed mobile phone surveys every three hours, six times per day"
- **Problem:** The abstract's "moment" is undefined and the survey cadence is three-hourly; minute-by-minute coupling is not something the abstract supports.
- **Suggested fix:** "the two hearts track each other from one moment to the next"

**Issue B. Tension finding generalized in the hook** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the two hearts track each other minute by minute — most tightly during arguments"
- **Abstract says:** "Physiological synchrony was stronger for spousal and White caregiver dyads, particularly during periods of higher interpersonal tension, whereas Black caregiver dyads exhibited physiological synchrony during periods of lower interpersonal tension."
- **Problem:** "Most tightly during arguments" was specific to spousal and White dyads; for Black dyads the pattern reversed. The digest flags the racial difference a sentence later, but the hook as written misstates the overall finding.
- **Suggested fix:** "the two hearts track each other moment to moment — most tightly during tense periods, at least in spousal and White dyads"

### Study 10: Nearly one in six adults with intellectual disability has a dementia diagnosis — and it comes early
**PMID:** 42623159 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=1,046,621 adults with ID aged ≥30, 2011–2022 Medicaid/Medicare claims, Down syndrome excluded, 184,206 (17.6%), one case per 5.7 people, mean age at onset 62.6, few differences by sex/race/ethnicity — all accurate. Boston University and Rubenstein attributions are correct.

**Issue A. Headline fraction and prevalence type** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Nearly one in six adults with intellectual disability has a dementia diagnosis"
- **Abstract says:** "184,206 (17.6%) had claims for ADRD, corresponding to an 11-year prevalence of one case per 5.7 people."
- **Problem:** 17.6% is slightly *more* than one in six (one in 5.7), so "nearly one in six" understates it; and the figure is an 11-year cumulative prevalence, not the proportion who currently have a diagnosis. The body text gets this right; the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Over 11 years, one in six adults with intellectual disability picked up a dementia diagnosis — and it comes early"

**Issue B. Trend described as a rise** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "yearly rates rose from 11.3% in 2011 to a peak of 16.7% in 2019"
- **Abstract says:** "Yearly prevalence was lowest in 2011 (11.3%) and highest in 2019 (16.7%)."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports only the lowest and highest years; it does not describe a monotonic rise or a "peak" with a subsequent decline. The shape of the trend is unverifiable from the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "yearly prevalence was lowest in 2011 (11.3%) and highest in 2019 (16.7%)"

### Study 11: The case for reviving lithium — at a fraction of the psychiatric dose — as a dementia drug
**PMID:** 42268602 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The review's content is summarized accurately: increased N-acetylaspartate on MRS, preserved gray matter on structural MRI, ~0.3 mM versus the 0.6–1.0 mM psychiatric range, preclinical hippocampal neurogenesis, epidemiological association with lower dementia risk, the call for a low-dose lithium orotate trial, the Gates Ventures/ADDI affiliations, and the kidney/thyroid caveat.

**Issue A. Novelty label** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** "The 25-Year Evolution of Lithium as a Disease-Modifying Agent in Dementia: A Narrative Review"; "These convergent data support a prospective clinical trial".
- **Problem:** The paper synthesizes and advocates; it does not overturn a prior finding. The label oversells a narrative review and sits awkwardly with the digest's own accurate NS-fit note.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** Synthesis / repurposing case"

**Issue B. Trial evidence hardened** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "early MCI trials showing cognitive stabilization and favorable tau changes"
- **Abstract says:** "early randomized clinical trials in MCI suggest cognitive stabilization and favorable tau biomarker changes at low, well-tolerated doses"
- **Problem:** "Showing" upgrades "suggest" for trials the digest itself elsewhere describes as small.
- **Suggested fix:** "early, small randomized MCI trials that suggest cognitive stabilization and favorable tau biomarker changes"

### Study 13: Brains from a mid-century asylum archive suggest seizures leave tau behind
**PMID:** 42715912 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=32, median age 61.5, 59.4% men, Danish Brain Collection (deaths 1945–1982), bilateral sections stained for pTau and beta-amyloid with a blinded assessor, associations with age at death and epilepsy duration, and the 11-patient focal-seizure hemispheric comparison (pTau p=0.02; amyloid p=1.0) are all accurate. The digest also correctly reports the authors' own hedged conclusion.

**Issue A. Cohort described as untreated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Thirty-two brains preserved from Danish psychiatric hospitals before modern anti-seizure drugs existed" / "a window on untreated epilepsy that can never be reopened"
- **Abstract says:** "Post-mortem brain tissue was obtained from the Danish Brain Collection comprising autopsies from psychiatric patients that died between 1945 and 1982."
- **Problem:** The abstract says nothing about treatment status, and phenobarbital, phenytoin, carbamazepine and valproate were all in use during part of that window. "Untreated epilepsy" is unverifiable from abstract only and may be wrong.
- **Suggested fix:** "Thirty-two brains preserved from Danish psychiatric hospitals in an era of limited seizure treatment — check the paper for what medication data, if any, survive."

**Issue B. "Mostly vascular" overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "81% had a competing dementia diagnosis, mostly vascular"
- **Abstract says:** "81.3% of all patients (n = 26) had a competing dementia diagnosis: three patients fulfilled the pathological criteria of Alzheimer's dementia, 13 patients had clinical and/or autoptic diagnosis of vascular dementia."
- **Problem:** Vascular dementia accounted for 13 of the 26 competing diagnoses — half, not "most," with the remainder unspecified in the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "81% had a competing dementia diagnosis, half of them vascular"

### Study 14: Depression and anxiety in older people with epilepsy may not be caused by the epilepsy
**PMID:** 42155275 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=38,173 community-dwelling men aged 65–85, >20 years of linked administrative follow-up, 505 with epilepsy at baseline, attenuation after adjustment for neurological comorbidity, loss of significance under competing-risk analysis, and the persistence of alcohol use disorder in age-matched analyses are all accurate.

**Issue A. Clinical implication goes beyond the abstract** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "a reframing that shifts the clinical implication from "screen epilepsy patients for depression" to "treat the underlying neurology as one problem""
- **Abstract says:** "these findings suggest that the psychiatric burden observed in older male adults with epilepsy most likely reflects shared pathology rather than causality, highlighting the importance of integrated neurological and mental health care."
- **Problem:** The authors call for *integrated* neurological and mental health care; the digest converts a null incidence finding into an argument for de-emphasizing psychiatric screening. Mental disorders were still more *prevalent* in men with epilepsy, so screening remains clinically relevant. This is a clinical recommendation the data do not support.
- **Suggested fix:** "a reframing that points toward integrated neurological and mental health care rather than treating the psychiatric burden as a downstream consequence of epilepsy — note that prevalence of mental disorders was still higher in men with epilepsy"

**Issue B. Headline generalizes past the study population** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Depression and anxiety in older people with epilepsy may not be caused by the epilepsy"
- **Abstract says:** "a population-based cohort study of 38,173 older men"; "In this cohort of older men…"
- **Problem:** The cohort is men only, which Caveats correctly flag; the headline nonetheless says "older people."
- **Suggested fix:** "Depression and anxiety in older men with epilepsy may not be caused by the epilepsy"

### Study 15: Moving a relative with dementia into a care home does not end the caregiving
**PMID:** 42720084 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=9, 60–120 minute semi-structured interviews, Colaizzi's method, six themes, all participants above the CES-D-10 clinical threshold, and the shift from physical tasks to monitoring/communication/crisis management all match. Aaron's Case Western affiliation is correct.

**Issue A. "Afterwards" implies a change the design can't show** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The day you move your mother into a care home is supposed to be the day the weight lifts. All nine caregivers here screened positive for depression afterwards."
- **Abstract says:** "Brief quantitative measures, including the CES-D-10 and the Caregiver Self-Assessment Questionnaire, were collected to contextualize participants' emotional burden"; "all participants scoring above the CES-D-10 clinical threshold".
- **Problem:** There is no pre-placement measurement, so the post-placement scores cannot be read as showing that distress persisted rather than changed. Caveats note "no control group," but the pitch's juxtaposition still implies a before/after comparison.
- **Suggested fix:** "All nine caregivers screened positive for depression when interviewed after placement — with no pre-placement scores for comparison."

**Issue B. Health-economics claim not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Placement is routinely modeled in health economics and in family expectations as the point at which caregiver burden drops."
- **Abstract says:** "Placement of a relative with Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) in residential care is often assumed to reduce caregiver burden."
- **Problem:** The specific claim about health-economics modeling is unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Placement is often assumed — by families and in the literature — to be the point at which caregiver burden drops."

### Study 16: A genetic small-vessel disease disrupts the hippocampus's rhythms long before dementia
**PMID:** 41622715 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The model system description (R182C-TgN3 humanized mouse, post-mortem tissue with four NOTCH3 variants, primary human VSMCs with p.R133C), impaired hippocampal gamma oscillations, reduced fibre length and aberrant morphology also seen in human tissue, reduced mitochondrial respiratory complexes, reduced ATP with increased pro-inflammatory gene expression, and iDISCO+ findings of VSMC loss and increased microglial vessel attachment at 9 months are all accurate.

**Issue A. Suggested contacts misrepresent the author list** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call Bengt Winblad's neurogeriatrics division at Karolinska — Wei Shao and Helena Karlström are the leads — and Raj Kalaria at Newcastle for the human pathology perspective."
- **Abstract says:** Authors include "Shao W(1) … Kalaria R(5) … Karlström H(1)", Division of Neurogeriatrics, Karolinska Institutet; Winblad is not listed.
- **Problem:** Winblad is not an author on this paper (unverifiable from abstract whether he heads the division), and Kalaria is a co-author — so he cannot supply an outside read on the human pathology. As written, an editor might commission him as the independent voice.
- **Suggested fix:** "Call Wei Shao and Helena Karlström in the Division of Neurogeriatrics at Karolinska; note that Raj Kalaria at Newcastle is a co-author, so a genuinely independent small-vessel-disease pathologist is still needed."

**Issue B. Caveat implies mouse behavioral data exist** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "no behavioral cognitive outcomes in humans"
- **Abstract says:** "we performed ex vivo electrophysiology, immunohistochemistry … western blotting, Seahorse assay, quantitative PCR and single-cell RNA sequencing."
- **Problem:** No behavioral or cognitive testing is reported in the mice either, so specifying "in humans" implies the mouse work established a cognitive phenotype. The gamma-oscillation-to-memory link in "Why it matters" is inferential.
- **Suggested fix:** "no cognitive or behavioral outcomes reported in either mice or humans — the link from gamma oscillations to memory is inferred"

### Study 17: In Down syndrome, frailty tracks cognitive decline whatever its cause
**PMID:** 42609160 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Cross-sectional design, 148 consecutive patients at an LMU Munich DSAD outpatient clinic, ID-FISF frailty index, CAMCOG-DS/SABS/SPES-SCOPA/DSQIID-G measures, exploratory blood biomarkers with bioimpedance and indirect calorimetry, the aetiology-independent link to (pre)frailty, and the open question about ID-adapted thresholds are all accurate. Levin and Nübling attributions are correct.

**Issue A. "Predicted" in a cross-sectional design** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Both baseline intellectual disability severity and current cognitive performance independently predicted frailty."
- **Abstract says:** "Both baseline ID severity (β = 0.093, p = 0.014) and cognitive performance (CAMCOG-DS, β = -0.0033, p < 0.001) were independently associated with ID-FISF scores."
- **Problem:** "Predicted" implies temporal ordering the cross-sectional design cannot establish — the same limitation the digest flags in Caveats.
- **Suggested fix:** "Both baseline intellectual disability severity and current cognitive performance were independently associated with frailty scores."

### Study 19: "Digital dementia" may explain how escaping into your phone dents your memory
**PMID:** 42141732 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=394, mean age 21.17, 59.4% female, 6.24 hours of daily internet use, the three scales, digital fugue predicting both other constructs, and partial mediation with a surviving direct effect via bootstrap analysis are all accurate. Caveats are unusually candid and the NS fit of 4/10 is well judged.

**Issue A. Headline implies objective memory impairment** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** ""Digital dementia" may explain how escaping into your phone dents your memory"
- **Abstract says:** Data came from "'OPIZA Digital Fugue Scale', 'Digital Dementia Scale-Adult Form' and 'Digital Amnesia Scale-Adult Form'"; the outcome is self-reported digital amnesia, and daily *internet* use was the exposure measured.
- **Problem:** The headline asserts a memory effect ("dents your memory") from three self-report questionnaires with no behavioral or cognitive testing — which the body text and Caveats correctly acknowledge, so the headline contradicts the rest of the entry. "Your phone" also narrows an internet-use measure.
- **Suggested fix:** ""Digital dementia": three questionnaires try to pin down what escaping into screens does to memory"
