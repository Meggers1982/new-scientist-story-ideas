### Study 1: Postpartum depression is strongly heritable, Danish twin study finds
**PMID:** 42648551 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

All core numbers check out: 10,418 female twins (5209 pairs), born January 1949–December 2000, MZ probandwise concordance 0.22 vs DZ 0.11, heritability 0.60 (95% CI 0.46–0.75), unique environment 0.40, and the "15 concordant pairs" figure (10 MZ + 5 DZ).

**Issue A. Comparison to major depression** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a genetic contribution on a par with that of major depression"
- **Abstract says:** Nothing about the heritability of major depression; it reports only that heritability of PPD was 0.60 and that "its underlying causes and degree of heritability remain unclear."
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only, and the direction is questionable — published twin heritability for major depression is usually put around 0.35–0.40, i.e. lower than 0.60, so "on a par" may understate the contrast the study actually implies.
- **Suggested fix:** "a substantial genetic contribution — around 60 per cent of the variation in liability — reframes it as a condition women are predisposed to"

**Issue B. Incomplete competing-interest disclosure** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "author funding from the Novo Nordisk and Lundbeck Foundations, with Lundbeck lecture fees declared"
- **Abstract says:** "Carolyn E Cesta reports a relationship with Merck AB that includes: speaking and lecture fees."
- **Problem:** The digest captures the main funders and the Lundbeck relationship but omits a second industry relationship (Merck AB) declared in the same statement.
- **Suggested fix:** "...with Lundbeck lecture fees declared by the senior author and Merck AB lecture fees by a co-author."

### Study 2: Insomnia travels with PTSD and depression but does not seem to cause them
**PMID:** 42750113 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. N = 846, the six timepoints, the RI-CLPM design, the null insomnia→PTSD/depression cross-lagged paths, the significant within-time within-person covariances at all timepoints, and the small reciprocal PTSD–depression effects all match. The hedged headline ("does not seem to cause them") is consistent with the authors' own wording ("co-fluctuates with, but does not causally drive"). Framing is consistent with the abstract.

### Study 3: Twelve minutes of vigorous exercise a week matches 150 minutes of moderate activity
**PMID:** 42745313 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 147,414, ages 40–74, NAKO + UK Biobank, mean follow-up 7.9 years, 5,545 deaths, HR 0.70 for 150 min/week moderate, ~510 min light / 12 min vigorous equivalence, and the 18–30% reallocation figures are all correct. The Bayer/Siemens Healthineers disclosure is correctly carried into Caveats.

**Issue A. Headline omits the outcome and implies equivalence of effect** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Twelve minutes of vigorous exercise a week matches 150 minutes of moderate activity"
- **Abstract says:** "Compared with no activity, 150 min per week of moderate-intensity activity was associated with lower mortality (HR: 0.70…). Similar associations were observed for approximately 510 min per week of light-intensity activity or 12 min per week of vigorous-intensity activity."
- **Problem:** The equivalence is specifically for the *association with all-cause mortality* in an observational pooled cohort. Unqualified, "matches" reads as a general health-benefit equivalence and as a demonstrated effect.
- **Suggested fix:** "Twelve minutes of vigorous activity a week is linked to the same lower death risk as 150 minutes of moderate activity"

**Issue B. Device and wear-period details not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "pooled wrist accelerometer data" / "baseline activity measured once, over about a week"
- **Abstract says:** "individually pooled baseline device-measured physical activity"; "the largest pooled accelerometer dataset to date." No placement or wear-duration specified.
- **Problem:** Wrist placement and the ~7-day wear window are plausible for both cohorts but are unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "pooled device-measured (accelerometer) activity data" and, in Caveats, "baseline activity measured once, over a short wear period."

**Issue C. NS fit score and section fit** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10"
- **Problem:** The evidence quality supports a high score (very large, device-measured, prospective), so 7 is defensible on rigour. The mismatch is placement: this is an all-cause mortality paper with no mind or brain outcome, filed under New Scientist Mind. Either the section assignment or the score rationale needs a line acknowledging that.
- **Suggested fix:** Keep 7/10 but flag for a Health rather than Mind slot, or note "no cognitive or mental-health outcome measured."

### Study 4: "Food noise" is quieter in people six months into GLP-1 drugs — and sweet tastes register less
**PMID:** 42641845 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Group sizes (27/25/27, total 79), the sweet-threshold result in both treated groups, and the lower cue responsivity, craving, food reward and energy intake in 6-month users are accurately reported.

**Issue A. Causal mechanism language from a cross-sectional design** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It suggests these drugs work mainly on the wanting and the mental chatter around food rather than by making food taste different"
- **Abstract says:** "In this cross-sectional study… Overall, GLP-1RA use was associated with lower food salience, cravings, cue responsiveness, food reward, and energy intake, while chemosensory differences were limited mainly to sweet taste detection."
- **Problem:** Three separate groups compared at one timepoint cannot establish how the drugs work; the authors keep to "associated with." The Caveats flag the design, but the body text asserts a mechanism anyway.
- **Suggested fix:** "It hints that the difference between treated and untreated people lies in wanting and the mental chatter around food rather than in how food tastes — with one exception for sweetness — though a cross-sectional comparison cannot show what the drugs are doing."

**Issue B. "Markedly" overstates the food-noise result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Six-month users reported markedly less food noise, craving, cue responsivity and food reward, and ate less."
- **Abstract says:** "Food Noise Questionnaire scores were lower in 6-month users than in pre-use controls (P = .026)."
- **Problem:** p = .026 on a questionnaire score in a ~27-per-group comparison does not support "markedly"; no effect size is given.
- **Suggested fix:** "Six-month users reported significantly lower food noise, along with lower craving, cue responsivity, food reward and energy intake."

**Issue C. Fat-taste exception dropped** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "while most other chemosensory measures were essentially unchanged"
- **Abstract says:** "Suprathreshold taste intensity, taste hedonics, olfactory function, and chemesthesis were largely unchanged, except for a limited fat-intensity difference at medium linoleic acid concentration."
- **Problem:** There was a second, smaller chemosensory difference; the digest's "one specific exception for sweetness" framing in Why it matters therefore isn't quite right.
- **Suggested fix:** "…while other chemosensory measures were largely unchanged, apart from a limited fat-intensity difference at one linoleic acid concentration."

**Issue D. NS fit score too high for the design** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — puts laboratory numbers on the much-discussed 'food noise' effect, but cross-sectional with fewer than 30 people per group."
- **Problem:** The headline finding (less food noise) rests on a self-report questionnaire in a cross-sectional comparison of ~27 people per group, with no one followed through treatment. That is exactly the profile the 7+ threshold is meant to catch.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 5/10, with the rationale "first laboratory chemosensory numbers on food noise, but cross-sectional, tiny groups, self-reported primary outcome."

### Study 5: Boosting a language region of the brain makes metaphors harder to understand
**PMID:** 42667874 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 40 (20 anodal, 20 sham), HD-tDCS over LIFG, the three sentence types with the exact example items, and the selective reduction in metaphor comprehensibility are all accurate. "Causal role" is the authors' own language, so the Why it matters framing is fair.

**Issue A. Right-hemisphere claim not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "why the right hemisphere's traditional starring role in metaphor now looks overstated"
- **Abstract says:** "the left inferior frontal gyrus (LIFG), which is traditionally considered a literal language semantic control region"
- **Problem:** The abstract frames the novelty as LIFG involvement in metaphor, not as a rebuttal of right-hemisphere accounts. Unverifiable from abstract only, and the study included no right-hemisphere condition or control site.
- **Suggested fix:** "ask what selective excitation of a region traditionally tied to literal semantic control tells us about how figurative meaning is retrieved."

### Study 6: Survivors of the Nova festival attack are being studied from weeks after the trauma to two years on
**PMID:** 42735067 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Correctly identified as a protocol paper with no results; the assessment battery, PCL-5/K6 primary outcomes, ~24-month span, sociocultural comparison group, and the authors' own statement that findings will be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects are all accurately conveyed.

**Issue A. Named drugs and "psychedelics" go beyond the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "whether being on MDMA or LSD during a trauma alters the outcome" / "many of them on psychedelics at the time"
- **Abstract says:** "Many survivors also reported being under the acute influence of psychoactive substances during the attack and the following hours."
- **Problem:** No specific substances are named. Unverifiable from abstract only, and naming MDMA and LSD sharpens the hook beyond the stated exposure.
- **Suggested fix:** "whether being under the acute influence of psychoactive substances during a trauma alters the outcome" / "many of them under the influence of psychoactive drugs at the time."

**Issue B. Cohort size and assessment terminology** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "hundreds of people exposed to the same catastrophic event within hours of each other" / "ecological momentary assessment"
- **Abstract says:** No survivor sample size is given (the 370+ figure refers to attendees killed); the method is listed as "ecological assessments."
- **Problem:** The cohort size is unverifiable, and "ecological momentary assessment" is a specific method the abstract does not name.
- **Suggested fix:** "a cohort of survivors of one catastrophic event, all exposed within a narrow time window" and "ecological assessments."

### Study 7: Anxious people don't get the usual relief of bad imagined futures fading
**PMID:** 42750110 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The four-experiment structure, the one-week/one-month follow-ups, and the key interaction (greater emotional fading for negative future events in the low-anxiety group, absent in the high-anxiety group) are accurate. Caveats correctly note that sample sizes are absent from the abstract.

**Issue A. "More vivid" is not among the reported differences** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Sentence completion and open association produced more vivid, more plausible, more emotionally charged future events than the standard recombination paradigm."
- **Abstract says:** "the sentence completion method elicited future events perceived as more likely to occur and with greater perceived impact, inducing stronger emotions"; open association "evoked stronger emotions and had a larger perceived impact on participants, with a greater probability of occurrence."
- **Problem:** Vividness was not a reported outcome; also, only sentence completion is explicitly compared with recombination in Experiment 1a.
- **Suggested fix:** "Sentence completion produced future events rated as more likely, more impactful and more emotionally charged than the standard recombination paradigm, and open association did the same."

**Issue B. Attribution of the null one-month result to method** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the one-month experiment found nothing, which the authors attribute to method rather than absence of effect"
- **Abstract says:** "Experiment 1b used a sentence completion task with a one-month follow-up, but found no significant emotional fading difference between positive and negative future events."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports the null but does not state an explanation; the method interpretation is inferred from the sequence of experiments. Also, 1b differs from 2b in both method *and* follow-up length.
- **Suggested fix:** "the one-month experiment (a different method and a longer interval) found no such difference."

### Study 8: Your baseline cortisol decides whether stress wrecks your memory
**PMID:** 42749210 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 63 (pre-encoding) and 56 (pre-retrieval), the significant pre-retrieval impairment, cortisol reactivity positively predicting false alarms, baseline cortisol negatively predicting them, and the lure-discrimination interpretation are all accurate.

**Issue A. Headline asserts causation for a measured moderator** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Your baseline cortisol decides whether stress wrecks your memory"
- **Abstract says:** "baseline cortisol levels moderated the effect of stress on memory performance. When the baseline cortisol levels were lower, a greater cortisol reactivity was associated with a higher FA rate."
- **Problem:** Stress was manipulated, but baseline cortisol is an observed individual difference; "decides" is causal language for a correlational moderator. "Wrecks your memory" also overstates a shift in false-alarm rates on a word-pair associative recognition task.
- **Suggested fix:** "Your baseline cortisol may shape whether a stress response distorts your memory"

**Issue B. "Only" overstates the moderation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Baseline cortisol moderated the effect: only people with low baseline levels were hurt by a big stress response."
- **Abstract says:** "When the baseline cortisol levels were lower, a greater cortisol reactivity was associated with a higher FA rate in both experiments."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports the association at lower baseline; it does not state that there was no association at higher baseline.
- **Suggested fix:** "Baseline cortisol moderated the effect: a big stress response was linked to more false alarms mainly in people with lower baseline levels."

**Issue C. Caveat inconsistent with the measures used** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "salivary cortisol is a noisy single-sample measure"
- **Abstract says:** Reports both "baseline cortisol levels" and "cortisol reactivity," which requires at least two samples.
- **Problem:** The caveat contradicts the design implied by the abstract; the sampling schedule is otherwise unverifiable.
- **Suggested fix:** "salivary cortisol is a noisy marker and the exact sampling schedule is not given in the abstract."

### Study 9: Sending clowns into dementia wards changes how families experience visiting
**PMID:** 42749305 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 11 family members and 20 staff, four long-term care homes in Eastern Australia, the two themes, the easing of visit-related stress, and the transfer of dementia-specific communication skills are all accurately reported.

**Issue A. "Wards" misdescribes the setting** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Sending clowns into dementia wards"
- **Abstract says:** "four long-term care homes in Eastern Australia"
- **Problem:** Residential aged care homes are not hospital wards; the distinction matters for readers and for anyone following up with providers.
- **Suggested fix:** "Sending clowns into dementia care homes changes how families experience visiting"

**Issue B. Laughter specifically** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "shared moments of laughter supported reminiscence, memory-making and family connection"
- **Abstract says:** "Shared positive experiences supported socialisation, reminiscence, and memory-making, which may have supported family connections."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports shared positive experiences and perceived happiness, not laughter as such; it also hedges the family-connection link with "may have."
- **Suggested fix:** "shared positive moments supported socialisation, reminiscence and memory-making, which participants felt strengthened family connection."

**Issue C. Invented illustrative detail in the pitch** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a professional clown gets a response from a mother who has not responded to her own daughter in months"
- **Abstract says:** Only the title quotation, "Someone's Acknowledged My Mum…in that Moment she Connected."
- **Problem:** The specific "not responded in months" scenario is not in the abstract and could read as a reported finding rather than a scene to look for.
- **Suggested fix:** "Lead on the quoted moment — 'someone's acknowledged my mum' — and find a family willing to describe it."

### Study 11: Exercise gives a small but real lift to memory in people with depression
**PMID:** 42641961 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

13 RCTs, 1098 participants, Hedges' g = 0.210 (95% CI 0.10–0.32), similar immediate (0.206) and delayed (0.213) recall effects, no significant moderation, moderate certainty of evidence — all accurate.

**Issue A. Null total-learning result omitted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "with similar gains for immediate and delayed recall"
- **Abstract says:** "significant improvements were observed for delayed recall (g = 0.213) and immediate recall (g = 0.206), whereas the effect for total learning was not significant (g = 0.243)."
- **Problem:** A third memory index did not reach significance despite a numerically larger point estimate — relevant context for a story built on consistency.
- **Suggested fix:** "with similar gains for immediate and delayed recall, though the effect on total learning was not significant."

**Issue B. Moderator list is more specific than the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "No exercise characteristic — type, intensity, duration, frequency — moderated the effect"
- **Abstract says:** "No significant differences were found across memory indices or exercise characteristics (all ps > 0.05)."
- **Problem:** The named moderators are plausible but unspecified in the abstract. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "No exercise characteristic tested moderated the effect"

**Issue C. "First" claim unverifiable** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** First-in-class … first meta-analysis pooling RCTs on this specific outcome"
- **Abstract says:** Makes no priority claim; describes the approach as "a three-level meta-analysis."
- **Problem:** Priority cannot be confirmed from the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "a three-level meta-analysis pooling RCTs on this outcome (priority claim to be confirmed)."

### Study 12: Aggression, insomnia and loneliness explain much of the link between childhood trauma and suicide risk
**PMID:** 42637149 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

The body text correctly reports N = 1572, NESDA, OR 1.51 / OR 2.68, the 66% / 39% reductions, the surviving mediators, and the small IL-6 contribution. The problems are in the headline and pitch.

**Issue A. Headline names the wrong mediator** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Aggression, insomnia and loneliness explain much of the link between childhood trauma and suicide risk"
- **Abstract says:** "In multiple-mediator models, direct effects reduced by 65.6% (SI+SA-) and 39.1% (SI+SA+) and aggression, insomnia, social support (both outcomes), neuroticism, introversion, hopelessness (SI+SA-) and locus of control (SI+SA+) remained significant."
- **Problem:** Loneliness was significant only in *single*-mediator models; in the multiple-mediator models the surviving variable was low social support, which the digest body correctly reports. The headline swaps in a variable that dropped out. "Explain" also imports causal meaning the authors avoid ("statistically account for").
- **Suggested fix:** "Aggression, insomnia and low social support account for much of the statistical link between childhood trauma and suicide risk"

**Issue B. Pitch attaches the 66% figure to the attempt outcome** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Childhood trauma nearly triples the odds of a suicide attempt in people with depression or anxiety — and two-thirds of that statistical link runs through things that can, in principle, be treated now"
- **Abstract says:** "direct effects reduced by 65.6% (SI+SA-) and 39.1% (SI+SA+)"
- **Problem:** The two-thirds reduction applies to ideation without attempt. For ideation *with* attempt — the outcome the sentence is about — the reduction was 39%. As written the pitch's central number is attached to the wrong outcome.
- **Suggested fix:** "…and around 40 per cent of that statistical link runs through factors that can, in principle, be treated now; for ideation without an attempt, the figure is closer to two-thirds."

**Issue C. "Nearly triples" assumes a comparison the abstract does not specify** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Childhood trauma nearly triples the odds of a suicide attempt"
- **Abstract says:** "The NEMESIS childhood trauma interview (0-8 score) measured CT severity. Higher CT scores were associated with … SI+SA+ (OR = 2.68, 95% CI: 2.21-3.27)."
- **Problem:** The exposure is a 0–8 severity score, so OR 2.68 is most likely per unit (or per standard deviation) of severity, not trauma versus no trauma. Unverifiable from abstract only — and if it is per unit, "nearly triples the odds of a suicide attempt" is badly wrong.
- **Suggested fix:** "Each step up the childhood-trauma severity scale was associated with sharply higher odds of a suicide attempt (OR 2.68) — confirm the scaling with the authors before using any multiplier."

**Issue D. Cohort naming** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call Jasper Wiebenga or Brenda Penninx (Amsterdam UMC / NESARC-scale NESDA cohort)"
- **Abstract says:** "Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety"; authors listed as Wiebenga JXM and Penninx BWJH.
- **Problem:** "NESARC-scale NESDA" conflates two unrelated cohorts (NESARC is the US survey used in Study 13) and will confuse a commissioning editor. First names are also unverifiable from the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Call the authors at Amsterdam UMC / GGZ inGeest (Wiebenga, Penninx), who run the NESDA cohort."

### Study 13: Cannabis use disorder tracks with suicide attempts — except in people with bipolar disorder, where it barely moves quality of life
**PMID:** 42664567 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

Sample sizes (753 / 35,556), the 33.71% vs 16.65% attempt rates, the mental-health QoL gradient in the non-bipolar group (51.49 → 45.28) and its absence in the bipolar group, and the null physical-health QoL result are all accurately reported in the body.

**Issue A. Pitch misattributes the doubled attempt rate to cannabis severity** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "in bipolar disorder, escalating cannabis problems go with double the rate of suicide attempts but no measurable drop in reported quality of life"
- **Abstract says:** "the rate of lifetime suicide attempts was 16.65% … in people without BD and with severe CUD compared to 33.71% … in people with BD and severe CUD."
- **Problem:** The doubling is a *bipolar versus non-bipolar* comparison at the same (severe) CUD level — it is attributable to bipolar status, not to escalating cannabis use. The abstract does not report an attempt-rate gradient across CUD severity within the bipolar group.
- **Suggested fix:** "among people with severe cannabis use disorder, those who also have bipolar I report roughly double the rate of lifetime suicide attempts — while their reported mental-health quality of life does not decline with cannabis severity at all."

**Issue B. Headline is ambiguous in a way that can reverse the finding** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Cannabis use disorder tracks with suicide attempts — except in people with bipolar disorder, where it barely moves quality of life"
- **Abstract says:** Attempt rates are *higher*, not lower, in the bipolar + severe CUD group; the exception applies only to the quality-of-life measure.
- **Problem:** "except in people with bipolar disorder" reads as attaching to the suicide-attempt clause, inviting readers to conclude the attempt association disappears in bipolar disorder — the opposite of the data.
- **Suggested fix:** "In bipolar disorder, worsening cannabis use disorder tracks with suicide attempts but not with reported quality of life"

### Study 14: Detained teenagers reach psychiatric emergency rooms with insomnia and suicide attempts, not aggression
**PMID:** 42748415 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

1206 adolescents, 160 (13%) detained, insomnia 43% vs 19%, suicide attempts 26% vs 18%, depression 26% vs 35%, and the higher rates of stress-related/adjustment (30:10), conduct (27:10) and substance use disorders (26:20) are all correct, as is the relatively lower frequency of behavioural presentations.

**Issue A. "Not aggression" is stronger than the data, and sits awkwardly with the conduct-disorder result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Detained teenagers reach psychiatric emergency rooms with insomnia and suicide attempts, not aggression"
- **Abstract says:** "behavioural presentations such as agitation, aggressiveness, irritability, disobedience and attention difficulties were comparatively less frequent" — while "conduct disorder (27%:10%)" was more frequent among detained adolescents.
- **Problem:** Aggression was *comparatively* less frequent, not absent, and conduct disorder diagnoses were nearly three times as common in the detained group. "Not aggression" overreaches.
- **Suggested fix:** "Detained teenagers reach psychiatric emergency rooms with insomnia and suicide attempts more often than with aggression"

**Issue B. Presentations versus individuals** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Researchers reviewed all 1206 adolescent presentations to a public child and adolescent psychiatric emergency service in Brazil over one year, of which 160 (13 percent) involved adolescents in custodial or semi-custodial detention."
- **Abstract says:** "Of 1206 adolescents, 160 (13%) were detained."
- **Problem:** The abstract counts adolescents (patients), not presentations; a single adolescent could present more than once. The digest also uses "consecutive presentations" in the NS fit line.
- **Suggested fix:** "Researchers reviewed the records of all 1206 adolescents presenting to a public child and adolescent psychiatric emergency service in Brazil over one year, of whom 160 (13 per cent) were in custodial or semi-custodial detention."

**Issue C. Detention-environment framing in the wider angle** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "what a 43 percent insomnia rate inside detention actually reflects — the environment, withdrawal, or trauma" / "sleep as a neglected instrument of harm in youth custody"
- **Abstract says:** Insomnia was a presenting complaint in 43% of emergency consultations involving detained adolescents — a rate among those reaching a psychiatric emergency service, not a prevalence inside detention facilities.
- **Problem:** The 43% figure describes a highly selected group (detained adolescents acute enough to reach emergency psychiatry). Reading it as an insomnia rate "inside detention" overgeneralizes. "Instrument of harm" also imports intent the data say nothing about.
- **Suggested fix:** "what a 43 per cent insomnia rate among detained adolescents who reach emergency psychiatry reflects" / "sleep as a neglected problem in youth custody."

### Study 15: Boys and girls with autism turn out to look developmentally alike — with one exception
**PMID:** 42748595 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

1050 children (834 boys, 216 girls), aged 2–10, Beijing Autism Association, 2021–2024, the single surviving domain difference (Cognitive Performance at ages 2–3, Cohen's d = −0.36, p_FDR = 0.011), the null results at 4–6 and 7–10, and the authors' own two-way interpretation are all accurate. The Caveats correctly note the analytic sample of 215 girls and the unverified gender-sensitivity of the instrument.

**Issue A. Significant overall multivariate effect omitted** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "After controlling for age and correcting for multiple comparisons, only one of seven developmental domains differed by sex"
- **Abstract says:** "MANCOVA revealed a significant overall multivariate gender effect (Wilks' λ = 0.974, F(7, 1032) = 3.89, p < .001)."
- **Problem:** The digest presents the study as essentially null ("the story is the null"; "essentially nothing") but omits that the omnibus multivariate test of sex differences was significant. That is a meaningful qualification to a null-result pitch.
- **Suggested fix:** "The overall multivariate test of sex differences was significant, but after controlling for age and correcting for multiple comparisons only one of seven domains differed in any age band."

**Issue B. "Overturns prior research" overstates what a single-instrument null shows** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research" / "**Why it matters:** … in a large Chinese clinical sample they are almost entirely absent on this standard developmental profile"
- **Abstract says:** "Previous studies using simple domain comparisons have yielded inconsistent findings" and the null pattern "may reflect genuinely similar developmental profiles … and/or the possibility that the C-PEP-3's domain-general structure limits its capacity to detect gender-specific patterns."
- **Problem:** The prior literature is described as inconsistent rather than as establishing a finding to overturn, and the authors explicitly leave the instrument explanation open. The Why it matters line does flag the instrument caveat, so this is mainly about the novelty label.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** Adds a large null to an inconsistent literature"

### Study 16: People with psychosis are least likely to get the medication that treats opioid addiction
**PMID:** 42743214 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

TEDS-D 2016–2022, 12,219 adult outpatient discharges with comorbid OUD and mental disorder, multilevel logistic models with state random intercepts, AOR 1.48 (depressive) and 1.43 (anxiety) relative to psychotic disorders — all accurate, as is the "planned MOUD" distinction in Caveats.

**Issue A. Headline generalizes from discharge records to people** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "People with psychosis are least likely to get the medication that treats opioid addiction"
- **Abstract says:** "discharges with depressive disorders … and anxiety disorders … had higher odds of MOUD than discharges with psychotic disorders"; the outcome is *planned* MOUD.
- **Problem:** The unit is treatment discharges, not people, and the outcome is a treatment plan rather than medication received — a distinction the digest acknowledges in Caveats but contradicts in the headline's "get."
- **Suggested fix:** "Treatment episodes involving psychosis are least likely to include planned medication for opioid addiction"

**Issue B. "Substantially more likely" and "12,000 treatment episodes" framing** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "were substantially more likely to include planned medication for opioid use disorder" / "a clear, actionable disparity across 12,000 treatment episodes"
- **Abstract says:** AOR 1.48 (95% CI 1.19–1.85) and 1.43 (95% CI 1.14–1.80); the authors conclude only that disparities "warrant further investigation."
- **Problem:** Adjusted odds ratios under 1.5 with lower confidence bounds near 1.14 are modest; "substantially" and "clear, actionable" overstate them, and the authors make no actionability claim.
- **Suggested fix:** "were around 1.4 to 1.5 times as likely to include planned medication for opioid use disorder" / "a consistent disparity across more than 12,000 treatment discharges."

**Issue C. Unsupported causal attribution in the pitch** — Severity: Minor

### Study 18: Patients who avoid infection by staying home have far worse depression and anxiety scores
**PMID:** 42745244 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 143 (88 women, 55 men, mean age 45.4), Hannover Medical School, PHQ-9 and GAD-7, the visual-analogue measures of fatigue, pain and quality of life, median scores of 5 on both instruments, ρ = 0.52 and ρ = 0.48, and the 26% undiagnosed/untreated figure are all accurate.

**Issue A. Correlations reported as a group difference** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "those who reported self-isolating because of infection concerns scored substantially higher on both depression (ρ = 0.52) and anxiety (ρ = 0.48)"
- **Abstract says:** "Patients who self-isolated because of infection concerns had significantly higher PHQ-9 (Spearman's ρ = 0.52, p < 0.001) and GAD-7 scores (Spearman's ρ = 0.48, p < 0.001)."
- **Problem:** The abstract's own wording is ambiguous, but a Spearman's rho indicates a rank correlation — most plausibly between degree of self-isolation and symptom scores — not a two-group mean difference. Presenting a correlation coefficient as evidence that one group "scored substantially higher" risks misdescribing the analysis. Flag as partly unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "self-isolation because of infection concerns correlated moderately with both depression (Spearman's ρ = 0.52) and anxiety scores (ρ = 0.48) — check with the authors exactly how self-isolation was scored."

**Issue B. "Far worse" against modest median scores** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Patients who avoid infection by staying home have far worse depression and anxiety scores"
- **Abstract says:** "The median PHQ-9 score was 5 (IQR 8) and the median GAD-7 score was 5 (IQR 7)" — both in the mild range.
- **Problem:** The digest body notes "median scores were modest," but the headline's "far worse" implies clinically severe distress in the isolating group, which a moderate correlation in a 143-person sample does not establish.
- **Suggested fix:** "Patients who avoid infection by staying home report more depression and anxiety"

**Issue C. Undiagnosed proportion described imprecisely** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Around a quarter of patients with clinically relevant symptom levels had never been diagnosed or treated."
- **Abstract says:** "Eleven of 140 patients with PHQ-9 scores of at least 10 had no psychiatric diagnosis. Overall, 26% of patients with relevant symptoms had not received a diagnosis or treatment."
- **Problem:** The 26% figure is accurate, but "never been diagnosed or treated" is stronger than "had not received a diagnosis or treatment," and the abstract's own two sentences are hard to reconcile (11 of 140 is 8%). Worth checking the denominator before publication.
- **Suggested fix:** "Around a quarter of patients with relevant symptom levels had not received a diagnosis or treatment — the abstract's figures need reconciling with the authors."

**Issue D. Named contacts** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call Thea Thiele or Luca Wolff (Hannover Medical School)"
- **Abstract says:** "Wolff LJ… Thiele T"; first names not given in full.
- **Problem:** First names are unverifiable from abstract only (Wolff's initials are LJ, so "Luca" is plausible from the email address but not confirmed).
- **Suggested fix:** "Call T. Thiele or L. J. Wolff (Hannover Medical School)"

### Study 20: A self-help app for trauma in Arabic reduced sadness, but did not raise happiness
**PMID:** 42747789 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 87 Egyptian adults with elevated PTSS, randomized to PTSD Coach Online-Arabic (n = 41) or waitlist (n = 46), 8 weekly timepoints, quadratic model best fitting sadness, significantly lower sadness in the final intervention week (b = −.35, p = .020) and at endline (b = −.42, p = .043), and the failure of both linear and quadratic models to fit happiness — all accurate. Caveats correctly note the single-item emotion outcomes and the waitlist comparator.

**Issue A. Headline implies a tested-and-failed happiness effect** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "A self-help app for trauma in Arabic reduced sadness, but did not raise happiness"
- **Abstract says:** "Neither the linear nor quadratic growth model fit well for the happy models."
- **Problem:** The happiness analysis failed at the model-fitting stage — no growth trajectory could be modelled, so no treatment effect on happiness was estimated at all. That is a null *model fit*, not a demonstrated absence of effect. The digest's body text gets this right ("Neither linear nor quadratic models fit the happiness ratings at all"), but the headline, Why it matters ("shifted one emotion and not its supposed opposite"), and pitch ("left happiness untouched") all read it as a tested null.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "A self-help app for trauma in Arabic reduced sadness — happiness ratings followed no clear trajectory at all". Why it matters: "A minimal-contact digital tool shifted sadness, while happiness ratings could not be modelled over time — a caution against treating 'feeling better' as a single dial."

**Issue B. Two-systems interpretation is the digest's, not the study's** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "consistent with the idea that negative and positive affect run on partly separate systems"
- **Abstract says:** "These results suggest enrollment in the intervention predicted improvements in sadness among adults affected by PTSS."
- **Problem:** The authors draw no conclusion about positive/negative affect systems; given that the happiness models did not fit, the data cannot bear on that question.
- **Suggested fix:** Drop the affect-systems inference, or attribute it explicitly as the writer's framing to be put to the authors.

### Cross-cutting notes for the section editor

1. **Headline-versus-body drift is the dominant failure mode.** In studies 8, 12, 13, 14, 16, 17, 18 and 20 the body text is accurate and appropriately hedged while the headline or pitch line asserts something stronger. Studies 12, 13 and 20 are the three where the headline could leave a reader with the wrong finding, and those need rewriting before pitching, not just softening.

2. **Two entries have score problems.** Study 4 at 7/10 rests on a cross-sectional comparison of ~27 people per group with a self-reported primary outcome; recommended 5/10. Study 3 at 7/10 is defensible on evidence quality but has no mind or brain outcome and looks misfiled under New Scientist Mind.

3. **Twelve claims are unverifiable from the abstract alone** and should be marked as such in the digest rather than stated flat: the major-depression heritability comparison (1), accelerometer placement and wear period (3), the right-hemisphere framing (5), named substances and cohort size (6), the one-month null explanation (7), moderator list and priority claim (11), the OR scaling (12), the self-isolation scoring (18), and the toilet-privacy mechanism (21). Study 12's OR scaling is the one that most needs resolving with the authors, because the pitch's central multiplier depends on it.

4. **Disclosure handling was good overall** — studies 3 and 4 both carried industry funding into Caveats. Study 1 missed one of two declared industry relationships.
