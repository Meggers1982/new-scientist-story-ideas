### Study 1: Land acknowledgements raise awareness of Indigenous groups but can dampen interest in their culture
**PMID:** 42791336 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (two Moderate)

**Issue A. "First solid causal evidence" overstates novelty** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "This is the first solid causal evidence on a ritual that has become near-ubiquitous at conferences, sporting events and university lectures"
- **Abstract says:** "Proponents argue they raise awareness of Indigenous histories and build support for reparations, but causal evidence is limited."
- **Problem:** "Limited" is not "absent." The abstract makes no priority claim, so "first" is the digest's inference.
- **Suggested fix:** "This is among the first causal evidence on a ritual that has become near-ubiquitous at conferences, sporting events and university lectures."

**Issue B. Sample attribution in the pitch** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "across nearly 7,000 people, the statement changed what the audience knew but not what they wanted"
- **Abstract says:** Awareness and attitude outcomes come from the Australia (N = 1,724) and USA (N = 1,773) experiments; the separate "Additional experiments (N = 3,638)" tested institutional reputation.
- **Problem:** The awareness/interest/attitude finding rests on ~3,500 participants, not ~7,000. The larger figure also under-counts the true total (7,135).
- **Suggested fix:** "across some 3,500 people in two countries, the statement changed what the audience knew but not what they wanted — with a further 3,638 participants tested on how institutions benefit"

**Issue C. "Expensive commitments were what shifted attitudes"** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "participants rewarded institutions for the cheap gesture, but the expensive commitments were what shifted attitudes"
- **Abstract says:** "the adoption of land acknowledgements provides small reputational benefits to institutions facing public scrutiny. Costlier practices—such as financial compensation and Indigenous participation in decision-making—have significantly larger effects."
- **Problem:** In context, the "significantly larger effects" are on institutional reputation, the same outcome as the acknowledgement condition — not on the political attitudes (collective guilt, reparations support) measured in the first pair of experiments. The pitch implies costly commitments moved attitudes the acknowledgements failed to move, which the abstract does not report.
- **Suggested fix:** "participants gave institutions only a small reputational credit for the cheap gesture, and a substantially larger one for costly commitments"

**Issue D. "Pre-registered-style" is unverifiable** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "large pre-registered-style randomized experiments across two countries"
- **Abstract says:** No mention of preregistration.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only; "pre-registered-style" is a vague construction that implies rigor not documented here.
- **Suggested fix:** "large randomized experiments across two countries" (check registration status in the full text before using).

NS fit 8/10 is otherwise defensible: randomized, two countries, >7,000 participants.

### Study 2: Angry voters pick dominant-looking leaders, frightened voters pick trustworthy-looking ones
**PMID:** 42858097 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Most angry and … most frightened" vs. abstract's "or"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "named the real political issue that made them most angry and the one that most frightened them"
- **Abstract says:** "identified the political issue that made them most angry or most afraid"
- **Problem:** The abstract's "or" leaves open whether this was within-subject (both nominated) or between-subject (one or the other). The digest asserts within-subject. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "named a real political issue that made them angry or afraid" — or confirm the design in the full text.

**Issue B. NS fit of 7 on a 3-point difference in a hypothetical face-choice task** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — clean emotional mechanism and an obvious election hook; limited by modest effect sizes and computer-generated faces."
- **Abstract says:** "chose a more dominant and less trustworthy-looking leader more often when angry (49.5 percent) than when fearful (46.5 percent)"; sample size not reported.
- **Problem:** The headline behavioral effect is a 3-percentage-point shift on a hypothetical choice with synthetic stimuli, and N is not reported in the abstract. The score sits a notch above what the evidence carries.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10, with the same rationale text.

### Study 3: Offered good and bad stories about a drug, people deliberately choose the bad ones — and then expect side effects
**PMID:** 42843138 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Temporal/causal phrasing for a correlational relationship** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Those who selected more negative anecdotes went on to report stronger expectations of side effects, more worry about them"
- **Abstract says:** "People who selected more negative anecdotes also had greater expectancies for side effects and worry about side effects."
- **Problem:** "Went on to" implies measured sequence and directionality; the abstract describes an association between selection and expectancies. Selection bias could run either way.
- **Suggested fix:** "Those who selected more negative anecdotes also reported stronger expectations of side effects and more worry about them"

**Issue B. NS fit of 7 on online, hypothetical, self-report studies** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — three studies, a genuinely new cognitive mechanism ('valenced interrogation bias')"
- **Abstract says:** "Across three online studies, participants were presented with an information leaflet describing a medication"; outcomes are expectancies, worry and willingness; evidence for the willingness effect is "moderate."
- **Problem:** All outcomes are self-reported, no drug or symptoms involved, and the mechanism label is the authors' own proposal. The digest's own Caveats acknowledge this; the score doesn't.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10.

### Study 4: Two heads don't average — they pick a winner
**PMID:** 42822293 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Usually clustered" overstates "often"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "but they were usually clustered near one member's estimate rather than between the two"
- **Abstract says:** "Joint judgments were more accurate than individual judgments, but were often in the neighborhood of one of the individual judgments."
- **Problem:** "Usually" implies a majority; the abstract says "often," and no proportion is given.
- **Suggested fix:** "but they often fell close to one member's estimate rather than between the two"

**Issue B. "Reputation … determines who wins"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The researchers also show that reputation built over past trials determines who wins"
- **Abstract says:** "In both models, relative accuracy on the current trial, as well as historical accuracy, were both predictors of whose individual estimate was given more weight in the joint judgment."
- **Problem:** Two issues: (i) historical accuracy was one of two predictors (current-trial accuracy was the other), so it doesn't "determine" the outcome; (ii) "reputation" is an interpretive gloss — the models used objective historical accuracy, not measured perceptions of a partner.
- **Suggested fix:** "The researchers also find that accuracy on previous trials, not just the current one, predicts whose number carries the day"

*Note:* Dyad sample size is not reported in the abstract — unverifiable from abstract only. NS fit 7/10 is otherwise defensible for a controlled lab study with formal model comparison.

### Study 5: People are better at judging a food's nutritional quality than its calories — but far more confident about the calories
**PMID:** 42863397 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Drove" and "systematically judged" for associations** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Familiar foods were systematically judged more nutritious and less calorific; liking, rather than familiarity, drove trial-level confidence."
- **Abstract says:** "Familiarity with food items was associated with choice behaviour in opposing directions across conditions, consistent with a tendency to perceive familiar foods as more nutritious and less calorie-dense. Liking rather than familiarity was associated with higher trial-level confidence."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports associations and a pattern "consistent with" a tendency; "systematically" and "drove" both harden this into a demonstrated, directional effect — in an N=32 correlational analysis.
- **Suggested fix:** "Familiar foods tended to be judged more nutritious and less calorific; liking, rather than familiarity, was associated with higher trial-level confidence."

**Issue B. Omits that metacognitive efficiency did not differ** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The notorious gap between knowing about nutrition and eating accordingly may partly be a metacognitive problem"
- **Abstract says:** "Metacognitive efficiency did not differ between conditions."
- **Problem:** The dissociation is in confidence *level*, not in how well confidence tracked accuracy. Leaving out the null on metacognitive efficiency makes the "metacognitive problem" framing sound broader than the data.
- **Suggested fix:** Add to the study description: "Metacognitive efficiency — how well confidence tracked accuracy — did not differ between the two conditions."

NS fit 6/10 is appropriately cautious.

### Study 6: We work hardest when we believe our effort can change the outcome — and depressive traits sharpen that calculus
**PMID:** 42675261 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Exerted more mental effort" vs. willingness to exert effort** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "People exerted more mental effort when the environment was controllable"
- **Abstract says:** "participants were more willing to exert effort in controllable environments than in uncontrollable ones and adjusted their decisions to exert effort dynamically"
- **Problem:** The measured outcome is the *decision* to accept effort, not effort actually expended; the drift-diffusion modeling is of "decisions to exert effort." The digest's headline ("we work hardest") compounds this.
- **Suggested fix:** "People were more willing to take on effortful work when the environment was controllable"

NS fit 6/10 is defensible for n=61 with a novel task.

### Study 8: Teenagers' thinking gets faster and sharper with age — and puberty barely explains it
**PMID:** 42826555 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Hormonal maturation" contradicts the digest's own caveat** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "chronological age and processing efficiency do most of the work, with hormonal maturation contributing little extra"
- **Abstract says:** "Pubertal maturation can be characterized by timing … and tempo"; conclusion refers to "weak links to individual differences in pubertal timing and tempo."
- **Problem:** No hormonal measure is reported — the exposures are pubertal timing and tempo (questionnaire-derived, as the digest's Caveats note). The body text introduces a biological measure the study did not use.
- **Suggested fix:** "with pubertal timing and tempo contributing little extra"

**Issue B. Risk-taking is not an outcome in this study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Much popular writing attributes adolescent cognition and risk-taking to puberty; here, chronological age and processing efficiency do most of the work"
- **Abstract says:** Outcomes are "accuracy, response times, and drift diffusion model parameters from an n-back task."
- **Problem:** Juxtaposing risk-taking with "here" implies the study bears on risk-taking; it tested working memory and decision parameters only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Much popular writing attributes adolescent cognitive change to puberty; for working-memory decision efficiency at least, age and processing efficiency do most of the work"

### Study 9: The EU's new energy labels are easier to read — and change what you can remember about them
**PMID:** 42832023 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Easier to read" is not a tested outcome** — Severity: Minor

- **As written (headline):** "The EU's new energy labels are easier to read — and change what you can remember about them"
- **Abstract says:** "Ratings confirmed that the labels of the old scale were psychologically skewed in mental space whereas the new scale had a more linear organization."
- **Problem:** The study tested the psychological spacing of the scale and subsequent recall, not readability or comprehension.
- **Suggested fix:** "The EU's new energy labels are more evenly spaced in the mind — and change what you can remember about them"

**Issue B. "Consumers end up remembering" overreaches the lab task** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a label redesign decided by policymakers quietly changes what consumers end up remembering"
- **Abstract says:** Preregistered experiment in which participants studied kWh values for seven refrigerators and recalled them after "a brief filled delay."
- **Problem:** Participants were not shopping and the values were experimenter-supplied; the Caveats flag this, but the body text asserts a consumer-behavior conclusion anyway.
- **Suggested fix:** "a label redesign decided by policymakers can quietly change what people later recall about the numbers behind it — at least in the lab"

### Study 10: Brain wiring differs by drug of choice — and in cannabis dependence it tracks impulsive choice
**PMID:** 42855476 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Underplays the "shared" half of the finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It argues against a single, generic 'addicted brain' signature"
- **Abstract says:** "Our findings reveal both shared and distinct WM alterations across SUDs"
- **Problem:** Opioid and cannabis dependence showed *convergent* widespread abnormalities; the authors frame the result as shared-and-distinct, not as a refutation of common signatures.
- **Suggested fix:** "It argues against a single, uniform 'addicted brain' signature, showing both shared and substance-specific white matter patterns"

**Issue B. "Dose-dependent signal"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a dose-dependent signal in an era of rising potency"
- **Abstract says:** "Moderated mediation analyses indicated that this association was indirectly driven by cannabis consumption, with asignificant conditional indirect effects observed only among daily users."
- **Problem:** A conditional indirect effect present only in daily users is consistent with, but not the same as, a demonstrated dose–response gradient; and nothing in the abstract concerns potency.
- **Suggested fix:** "a signal that appeared only in daily users, in an era of rising potency"

Caveats correctly note n=16 for opioids and the cross-sectional design. NS fit 6/10 is defensible.

### Study 11: Scanning doctors' brains as they decide who qualifies for assisted dying
**PMID:** 42091837 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. "Doctors" / "a clinician's brain" — participants were medical students** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written (headline and Why it matters):** "Scanning doctors' brains as they decide who qualifies for assisted dying" … "this is the first attempt to characterize what happens in a clinician's brain at the moment of decision"
- **Abstract says:** "This functional MRI study examines how social norms and empathy influence MAiD decisions in 59 Australian medical students while evaluating hypothetical assisted-dying scenarios."
- **Problem:** The headline and "Why it matters" both assert practising clinicians, directly contradicting the digest's own caveat ("medical students, not practicing clinicians") and the abstract. A headline is the most-repeated element of a pitch.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Scanning trainee doctors' brains as they decide who qualifies for assisted dying." Why it matters: "…what happens in a future clinician's brain at the moment of decision."

**Issue B. "Affectively easier" contradicts the amygdala result** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The surprise is the asymmetry — saying yes within the law looked affectively easier and more compassion-like"
- **Abstract says:** "Normative scenarios were associated with increased amygdala activity, particularly among less religious participants, suggesting a role for negative affective salience."
- **Problem:** The qualifying (normative) cases carried *both* a compassion-related vmPFC/ACC signature and increased amygdala activity interpreted as negative affective salience. The digest reports the amygdala finding in the body but then builds the pitch on an "affectively easier" reading that the authors' own interpretation cuts against.
- **Suggested fix:** "The surprise is the asymmetry — saying no was cognitively effortful and mentalizing-heavy, while saying yes engaged compassion-related circuitry alongside heightened amygdala activity the authors read as negative affective salience."

Caveats correctly flag reverse inference, N=59 and the Australian legal context. NS fit 6/10 is defensible.

### Study 12: Playing for points instead of cash makes people try less hard — even in psychosis research
**PMID:** 42475772 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one Moderate)

**Issue A. Author conflicts of interest omitted from Caveats** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written (Caveats):** "Secondary/exploratory analyses for the income effect (p=0.046); single behavioral task; clinical high-risk status is not a diagnosis; US-based multi-site sample."
- **Abstract says:** "PRC is a cofounder of Tetricus Labs, a computational psychiatry company. SWW received speaking fees from the American Psychiatric Association and Medscape Features, and was granted U.S. Patent 8,492,418 B2 for a glycine agonist treatment."
- **Problem:** Declared commercial interests — including a co-founder of a computational psychiatry company, in a paper about the scalability of computerized behavioral tasks — are not mentioned in the digest.
- **Suggested fix:** Add: "Two co-authors declare commercial interests, including co-founding a computational psychiatry company; the authors state these did not influence the work."

**Issue B. "$4 bonus" is not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the same $4 bonus is a different experiment depending on who is sitting at the keyboard"
- **Abstract says:** No reward amounts are reported.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only; a concrete dollar figure in a pitch invites an editor to quote it.
- **Suggested fix:** "the same cash bonus is a different experiment depending on who is sitting at the keyboard" (verify the amount in the full text before using a figure).

**Issue C. Framing runs against the authors' stated conclusion** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this shows the currency changes the answer"
- **Abstract says:** "Present findings support further study of point-based rewards in tasks indexing amotivation."
- **Problem:** The authors found a main effect of reward type but no interaction with clinical status or symptoms, and read the result as *encouraging* for point-based tasks. The digest's framing — "an awkward methodological implication for an entire task literature" — is one reading, but the contrary authorial conclusion should be on the page.

### Study 13: People really do compute the average of a set of numbers — they're not just eyeballing the biggest one
**PMID:** 42870117 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Under time pressure" vs. "while timed"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "participants saw a 2×2 grid of Arabic digits and judged under time pressure whether the arithmetic mean was above or below 5"
- **Abstract says:** "participants viewed a 2 × 2 array of four Arabic digits and decided, while timed, whether the arithmetic mean of the set was smaller or larger than 5"
- **Problem:** "While timed" means response time was recorded; it does not establish a deadline or speed instruction. A speed–accuracy manipulation would be a different design claim.
- **Suggested fix:** "judged, with response times recorded, whether the arithmetic mean was above or below 5"

Everything else — N=40 per experiment, the ensemble-distance effect, the two heuristic competitors, the Experiment 2 conflict manipulation, the non-preregistration caveat — matches. NS fit 5/10 is well judged.

### Study 15: A night in a sleep lab already contains a decade-long warning about your heart
**PMID:** 42852996 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Roughly doubled … in both validation sets"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A one-standard-deviation rise in the score roughly doubled atrial fibrillation hazard in both validation sets."
- **Abstract says:** "AF 2.03 (1.82-2.27) (EUH) and 2.72 (2.31-3.21) (BIDMC)"
- **Problem:** 2.72 is nearly a tripling, with a confidence interval excluding 2.3; "roughly doubled in both" flattens a meaningfully larger effect in one cohort.
- **Suggested fix:** "A one-standard-deviation rise in the score was associated with roughly a doubling of atrial fibrillation hazard at one validation site (HR 2.03) and nearly a tripling at the other (HR 2.72)."

**Issue B. Not all five outcomes replicated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "External validation in two independent cohorts (n=9,810 and n=12,576) showed the network's output remained strongly associated with outcomes after adjusting for…"
- **Abstract says:** "MI 1.38 (1.22-1.55) and 1.10 (0.86-1.40)"
- **Problem:** The myocardial infarction hazard ratio at BIDMC has a confidence interval crossing 1 — i.e. not significant in that cohort. "Outcomes" as an undifferentiated set implies uniform replication.
- **Suggested fix:** "…remained strongly associated with most outcomes — atrial fibrillation, heart failure and death most robustly, with myocardial infarction not reaching significance in one of the two cohorts."

NS fit 5/10 and the Caveats (retrospective, ICD-coded outcomes, sleep-clinic populations) are sound.

### Study 16: Pilots describe what actually saves them when something happens that no checklist covers
**PMID:** 42095443 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one Moderate)

**Issue A. "The most-cited strategy"** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "why 'stay aware' is both the most-cited strategy and the least trainable"
- **Abstract says:** "Pilots relied on procedures and checklists, basic flight skills, and Crew Resource Management principles to manage these events. Cognitive strategies, such as sustained situational awareness, were also identified as essential."
- **Problem:** The abstract ranks only *event types* by frequency ("most frequently reported"), not strategies — and situational awareness is listed after procedures, checklists, flight skills and CRM. "Most-cited" is unsupported, and "least trainable" is the digest's editorializing.
- **Suggested fix:** "why sustained situational awareness is repeatedly named as essential yet is among the hardest things to train"

**Issue B. "Structured" interviews** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Researchers ran 50 structured knowledge-elicitation interviews with pilots"
- **Abstract says:** "We conducted 50 knowledge elicitation interviews with pilots."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not specify structured vs. semi-structured. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Researchers ran 50 knowledge-elicitation interviews with pilots"

**Issue C. Background claim attributed to the pilots** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "said training covers known anomalies while leaving 'judgment expertise' under-taught"
- **Abstract says:** Background: "While current training focuses on known anomalies…"; Results: "Pilots emphasized the need for additional simulator-based training and training on judgment expertise concepts."
- **Problem:** "Training covers known anomalies" is the authors' framing in the Background; what the pilots asked for was more simulator-based training plus judgment-expertise training. The digest also drops the simulator request.

### Study 17: People rarely check whether an AI advisor is making things up — and generic warnings don't help
**PMID:** 42759086 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Headline converts a measurement gap into a behavioral finding** — Severity: Major

- **As written (headline):** "People rarely check whether an AI advisor is making things up — and generic warnings don't help"
- **Abstract says:** "Nearly all studies measure reliance, while variables such as user skepticism and verification of the information are more often targeted by an intervention than measured directly."
- **Problem:** The review's point is that verification is rarely *measured*, not that people rarely verify. The headline states as an established behavioral fact precisely the thing the authors say the literature has failed to establish — and the digest's own body text gets this right ("Nearly all studies measure reliance alone"), so the headline contradicts the entry. Any pitch built on this hook would be built on a result the paper does not report.
- **Suggested fix:** "We don't actually know whether people check AI advisors for hallucinations — and generic warnings don't seem to help"

**Issue B. "Don't help" / "largely useless" hardens a mixed-evidence claim** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "finds the standard disclaimer largely useless" / "generic warnings don't help"
- **Abstract says:** "Whereas general warnings about the risk of hallucinations are among the most deployable interventions for organizations, they show the weakest and most mixed effects in the studies reviewed."
- **Problem:** "Weakest and most mixed" across a literature the authors describe as limited is not the same as "useless" or "don't help." A review of thin, heterogeneous evidence cannot support a null verdict.
- **Suggested fix:** "finds the standard disclaimer has the weakest and least consistent track record of the interventions reviewed"

Credit where due: the Caveats correctly disclose the Analysis Group writing assistance. NS fit 5/10 is appropriate for a narrative review.

### Study 19: In shared-motherhood IVF, who carries the baby comes down to who wants to be pregnant

**Issue A. Headline is more deterministic than the finding** — Severity: Minor

### Study 20: Doctors running ketamine clinics have written their own rulebook
**PMID:** 42144140 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one Moderate)

**Issue A. Rupert McShane is an author, not an independent voice** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Worth calling David S. Mathai at Baylor College of Medicine, and an independent voice such as Rupert McShane at the University of Oxford for the UK contrast."
- **Abstract says:** Author list includes "McShane R" (Department of Psychiatry, University of Oxford); COI statement: "RMcS runs a ketamine clinic on behalf of his employer Oxford Health NHS Foundation Trust" and he is listed among those who "serve as volunteer faculty with ASKP3."
- **Problem:** The digest offers a co-author with a declared ketamine-clinic interest as the independent counterweight. A writer following this instruction would quote a guideline author as an outside critic — exactly the error the Caveats warn about.
- **Suggested fix:** "Worth calling David S. Mathai at Baylor College of Medicine and co-author Rupert McShane at the University of Oxford for the UK perspective — then sourcing a genuinely independent critic with no ASKP3 affiliation or clinic ownership."

**Issue B. "First attempt at a standard of care"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this is the first attempt at a standard of care — written by the field itself"
- **Abstract says:** "There is an urgent need for current, evidence-informed treatment guidelines to facilitate patient safety, clinical decision-making, and care quality in real-world contexts."
- **Problem:** The authors claim a need for *current* guidelines, not an absence of all prior consensus documents. "First" is unverifiable from the abstract and likely overstated given earlier ketamine consensus statements.
- **Suggested fix:** "this is the sector's most comprehensive attempt yet at a standard of care — written by the field itself"

Caveats on industry/ownership conflicts and the 28/39 response are accurate and well placed. NS fit 4/10 is correct.
