### 1. Your earliest memory dates from the same age whatever culture you grew up in

**Journal:** *Memory* | **Published:** 18 September 2026
**PMID:** 42757868 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09658211.2026.2727406
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a clean cross-cultural null that contradicts a textbook claim, though it rests on self-reported dating of memories.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers asked 268 New Zealanders aged 12 to 21 — identifying as Māori (n=91), New Zealand European (n=90) or New Zealand Chinese (n=88) — to narrate their earliest memory, date it, and report what they knew of their family history, plus measures of identity and well-being. The long-standing claim that people from East Asian backgrounds have later first memories than Westerners did not hold: there were no cultural, gender or age differences in the age of the earliest memory. What did differ by culture was the *content* — how emotional the memory was and whether it centered on the self or on others — and, for Māori adolescents, family history knowledge uniquely predicted strength of ethnic identity.

**Why it matters:** The "offset of childhood amnesia varies by culture" finding has been repeated in psychology teaching for two decades; this suggests the cultural signature lies in what people remember rather than how far back they can reach.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the standard classroom demonstration — ask a room of people for their earliest memory and the received wisdom that culture shifts the answer by a year or more. Then the twist: in a carefully matched three-culture adolescent sample, it didn't. Worth calling Elaine Reese at the University of Otago, who has spent a career on how families talk about the past, and asking her what the cultural difference actually is if it isn't the age.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a general-interest or arts outlet on family history knowledge as psychological infrastructure — the finding that knowing your whakapapa predicts ethnic identity in Māori teenagers speaks to a broader story about intergenerational storytelling, genealogy services and adolescent identity formation.

**Caveats:** Observational and cross-sectional; earliest-memory age is self-dated and unverifiable; single-country sample with modest per-group numbers; well-being outcomes self-reported.

### 2. Intrusive memories may not be the simple conditioned reflexes we assumed

**Journal:** *Behaviour Research and Therapy* | **Published:** 15 September 2026
**PMID:** 42759257 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.brat.2026.105156
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a deliberate, well-designed attempt to break a dominant clinical theory, and it partly succeeded; limited by an analogue trauma design in students.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Ninety-one undergraduates watched films depicting accidents and injury, violence and threat, or neutral scenes, each paired with a distinct neutral odor as a conditioned cue. Participants then logged intrusive memories in daily life for eight days, spraying one of the odors on their clothes either immediately or three days later. The odor cue did trigger more intrusions than a control scent — but the intrusions were not specific to the film that odor had been paired with, and cue-specificity in the lab was only partial.

**Why it matters:** Trauma therapies are built on the idea that intrusive memories are conditioned responses to specific trauma-linked cues; if the cue-memory link is looser and more context-dependent than that, the targets for treatment may need rethinking.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the smell test — a scent worn on your own clothes can pull unwanted images into your head, but not the images it was trained to pull. Open on the participant spraying an odor on a jumper three days after leaving the lab. Renée Visser and Merel Kindt's group at the University of Amsterdam are the people to call; Kindt has form in challenging consensus on reconsolidation-based treatment.
- **Wider angle:** A specialist piece for a clinical psychology or psychiatry outlet on how far analogue "trauma film" paradigms can carry theories of PTSD, and what happens to a field when its best laboratory model keeps producing equivocal answers.

**Caveats:** Analogue trauma in a healthy student sample, not patients; intrusions self-reported via diary; no clinical outcomes; short follow-up (8 days).

### 3. A third of sexual abuse survivors with PTSD report genital arousal when the memory returns

**Journal:** *European Journal of Psychotraumatology* | **Published:** 7 September 2026 (issue dated December 2026)
**PMID:** 42704048 | **DOI:** 10.1080/20008066.2026.2716440
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a large clinical sample on a genuinely under-researched and stigmatized phenomenon; entirely self-reported and cross-sectional.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers assessed 1057 patients with PTSD and a history of sexual abuse (84.7% female, mean age 38.8) referred for trauma-focused treatment in the Netherlands, combining clinician-administered PTSD severity ratings with self-report measures of genital responses tied to abuse memories. Some 34.4% reported such responses; among them, 91.8% reported shame, 87.6% disgust and 70.6% self-hatred, and 38.2% said the responses strongly affected daily life. Disgust and self-hatred — but not shame — tracked with greater PTSD severity, and those reporting genital responses had been abused younger and more repeatedly.

**Why it matters:** Involuntary genital response during sexual assault is well documented, but its recurrence during *memory recall* has barely been quantified; this puts a number on an experience many survivors never disclose and gives clinicians a reason to ask.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the body doing something the mind experiences as betrayal — years later, on recall alone. Open with the clinical silence: a symptom reported by a third of a 1000-patient cohort that almost nobody asks about. Iva Bicanic at the National Psychotraumacenter in Utrecht or Ad de Jongh (PSYTREC) can speak to why assessment practice has avoided this, and what the appraisal — "my body wanted it" — does to recovery.
- **Wider angle:** A longer piece for a health or women's-interest outlet on arousal non-concordance — the well-replicated mismatch between genital response and subjective desire — and how ignorance of it shapes courtrooms, juries and survivors' self-blame. Sexologist Stephanie Both is a co-author and a natural voice.

**Caveats:** Genital responses and their emotional valence entirely self-reported, no physiological measurement; treatment-seeking sample may not represent all survivors; cross-sectional, so severity associations cannot establish direction; single clinical network.

### 4. Typing the first few digits helps you hold more in working memory

**Journal:** *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* | **Published:** 17 September 2026
**PMID:** 42754727 | **DOI:** 10.3758/s13423-026-03014-2
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a tidy three-experiment demonstration that the classic "span" limit is partly an artifact of how we test it; laboratory-bound and modest in scale.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Participants tried to remember lists of seven or nine digits. In the key condition they cumulatively typed the first four on a numeric keypad while the rest of the list appeared, holding the remaining digits by attention alone. Across three experiments, this "typing-based maxispan" beat a standard span task — and the benefit appeared not only for the typed digits (near ceiling) but also for the unrehearsed ones at the end of the list, and it survived articulatory suppression.

**Why it matters:** It suggests a substantial part of what we call working memory capacity is a coordination problem rather than a hard storage limit, and that the motor system — not just inner speech — can be recruited as a scratchpad.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Sell it as the phone-number trick: the reason you drop the last digits may be that you're using one strategy where two would work better. Open on someone thumbing a number into a keypad while still hearing the rest of it. Jean Saint-Aubin at the Université de Moncton can explain why previous maxispan work assumed the rehearsal had to be vocal, and whether the trick survives outside the lab.
- **Wider angle:** For a technology or design outlet: if offloading items to a low-attention motor routine expands what people can hold, what does that imply for interface design in high-load settings — cockpit callouts, pharmacy dispensing, air traffic control — where touchscreens have quietly removed the motor component of tasks?

**Caveats:** Small laboratory samples across three experiments; none preregistered; single center; digits only, so generalization to meaningful material is untested.

### 5. People who recall their lives in more detail tell their stories more often

**Journal:** *Memory* | **Published:** 15 September 2026
**PMID:** 42742196 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09658211.2026.2733323
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — first real-time test of a link previously known only from questionnaires, and highly relatable; still correlational.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (1 hits)

**The study:** Using experience sampling, 116 participants were pinged eight times a day for seven days and asked whether they were with other people and, if so, whether they were sharing an autobiographical memory. People whose autobiographical recall was more detailed shared memories more frequently — and, notably, they modulated sharing by closeness, telling more to closer partners, whereas people with less detailed recall shared at the same rate regardless of who they were with.

**Why it matters:** It moves the "memory is social" claim from retrospective questionnaires into real time, and hints that vivid recall may be part of how some people calibrate intimacy.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is the closeness effect, not the sharing effect: people with sketchy autobiographical recall don't just tell fewer stories, they don't seem to save them for the people who matter. Open on the phone buzzing mid-conversation. Jun Hashimoto's group at Hiroshima University can speak to what this means for people at the low end of autobiographical memory — including those with severely deficient autobiographical memory (SDAM).
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a general-interest magazine on aphantasia and SDAM as social phenomena rather than perceptual quirks — what relationships look like when you cannot furnish them with detailed shared past.

**Caveats:** Small-to-moderate N and observational; memory detail and sharing both self-reported; single cultural sample; direction of the association untestable.

### 6. Choosing to act makes you remember better — and the brain signal shows it

**Journal:** *International Journal of Psychophysiology* | **Published:** 17 September 2026
**PMID:** 42753975 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2026.113475
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — four experiments with converging behavioral and ERP evidence, and a satisfying dissociation; effects are modest and laboratory-specific.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Across four experiments, the researchers manipulated participants' sense of agency over to-be-remembered items in two ways: by varying whether an action reliably produced its expected outcome, and by varying whether the action was freely chosen or instructed. Both boosted simple recognition, but only voluntary action improved recollection specifically — with matching increases in the FN400 and late positive ERP components — while consistency between action and outcome did not.

**Why it matters:** It identifies *volition* rather than control per se as the ingredient that folds an item into self-referential processing, which may explain why actively chosen learning material sticks.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the practical asymmetry — being in control of an outcome isn't enough; you have to have chosen. Open on a student deciding which flashcard to turn over versus being handed one. Ke Ma at Southwest University, with Bernhard Hommel as senior author, can situate this against decades of work on the self-reference effect and say whether it predicts anything for self-directed learning.
- **Wider angle:** For an education or edtech outlet: adaptive learning software increasingly chooses the next item for the learner in the name of efficiency. This is a line of evidence that the act of choosing is itself doing memory work — a genuine tension worth reporting on.

**Caveats:** Laboratory recognition tasks with artificial stimuli; sample sizes per experiment likely modest; not preregistered as reported; one of four experiments failed to show the effect, and the authors' explanation is post hoc.

### 7. Years on the football field damage memory directly; military service does it through PTSD

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychiatric Research* | **Published:** 11 September 2026
**PMID:** 42753419 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2026.09.008
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a big sample and a clean dissociation between two exposures usually lumped together; self-selected online cohort and mostly self-reported outcomes.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Among 3055 male former American football players in the online Head Impact & Trauma Surveillance Study, 375 of whom had served in the military, simultaneous equation models tested how years of football and military service related to cognitive and neuropsychiatric outcomes, with PTSD as an intermediate factor. More years of football were directly associated with worse self-reported cognition, worse mood and worse performance on a computerized paired-associates memory test. Military service, by contrast, was associated with worse outcomes only indirectly, through PTSD.

**Why it matters:** Repetitive head impacts and military service are routinely bundled together as "brain trauma exposures"; this suggests they reach later-life cognitive complaints by different routes, with implications for who gets screened for what.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is that two populations assumed to share a risk pathway don't. Open on a former lineman who also served, and the question of which exposure his memory complaints belong to. Michael Alosco at the Boston University CTE Center is the contact; press him on why the objective memory measure tracked football years but not service.
- **Wider angle:** For a sports or policy outlet: a piece on the rise of large self-enrolled online cohorts as the main evidence base for chronic traumatic encephalopathy research, their selection biases, and what they can and cannot settle.

**Caveats:** Observational — no causal inference possible; volunteer online cohort of players concerned about their brains, so strongly self-selected; most outcomes self-reported; male-only; extensive industry and consulting disclosures among senior authors (see conflict statement).

### 8. Talking yourself down after good news makes you read social situations more rigidly

**Journal:** *Behaviour Research and Therapy* | **Published:** 8 September 2026
**PMID:** 42727265 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.brat.2026.105149
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — preregistered, randomized and reasonably powered, with a causal manipulation rather than another correlation; lab-based and short-term.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** In a preregistered experiment, 268 Dutch adults recalled a positive autobiographical memory and were randomly assigned to dampen it ("this won't last"), amplify it, or a control condition, then completed a task measuring how readily they revised negative interpretations of ambiguous social scenarios as new evidence arrived. Dampening increased rigidity: those participants clung to negative interpretations even when disconfirming evidence appeared, relative to both other groups.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the habit of deflating your own good feelings is not merely a symptom of low mood but a causal step that stiffens negative beliefs — a specific, trainable target for therapy.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the internal move everyone recognizes: the promotion arrives and you immediately think "they'll realize their mistake." The surprise is the downstream cost — not a worse mood, but a demonstrably worse ability to update beliefs about other people. Jonas Everaert at Tilburg University is the person to call, and the manipulation is vivid enough to describe in full.
- **Wider angle:** A therapy-focused piece for a health or psychology outlet on the growing interest in positive-affect regulation — why several cognitive treatments now spend as much time on how patients handle good feelings as on how they handle bad ones.

**Caveats:** Single-session laboratory manipulation with no follow-up; non-clinical Dutch adult sample; outcome is a computerized belief-updating task, not real-world interpretation; effect on positive interpretation bias was null.

### 9. Event boundaries matter less to your memory than what happens next

**Journal:** *Memory & Cognition* | **Published:** 18 September 2026
**PMID:** 42760449 | **DOI:** 10.3758/s13421-026-01947-6
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — preregistered reanalysis with a genuinely unexpected asymmetry; limited by being a reanalysis of existing narrative-memory data.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** In a preregistered reanalysis of their own previously published narrative studies, the authors separated the causal connections surrounding each story event into antecedents (what caused it) and consequences (what it went on to cause). Memory was far more strongly predicted by the number of consequences an event had than by its causes or its position in the story — consistent with each new consequence reactivating, and thereby strengthening, the earlier event's trace.

**Why it matters:** It reframes narrative memory as forward-driven: an event becomes memorable because of what it goes on to explain, which is the opposite of the intuition that we remember the causes of things.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is a reversal of intuition — the parts of a story you remember are the ones the story keeps coming back to use, not the ones that set things up. Open on why you can recall the seemingly minor detail that later turned out to matter. Gabriel Radvansky at Notre Dame has spent a career on event models and can explain what this does to the Event Horizon Model.
- **Wider angle:** For a books or screenwriting-adjacent outlet: what a cognitive account of reactivation says about foreshadowing, Chekhov's gun and why certain plot structures are easier to hold in mind — a craft piece with real experimental grounding.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of previously collected data (though the reanalysis was preregistered); narrative-memory laboratory paradigm; original samples likely undergraduate; no new data collection.

### 10. Interrupting a film scrambles your memory of it but not how you carve it into events

**Journal:** *Memory & Cognition* | **Published:** 18 September 2026
**PMID:** 42758465 | **DOI:** 10.3758/s13421-026-01946-7
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a clean dissociation between two processes usually treated as one; modest sample and not preregistered.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Participants watched video excerpts either uninterrupted, interrupted by a white-noise screen every minute, or interrupted *and* jumbled so the one-minute clips played out of order, marking where they felt one event ended and another began, then recalling what they had seen. Segmentation was remarkably robust — overall rates, patterns and hierarchical structure were unchanged, with only a brief spike in boundary-marking after a jump that settled within about 5 seconds. Recall, by contrast, suffered badly in the jumbled condition, both in how much was remembered and how well it was organized.

**Why it matters:** Event segmentation and episodic encoding are usually assumed to be two faces of the same event-model machinery; this suggests segmentation works on a few seconds of information while memory needs coherence sustained over minutes.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Frame it around fragmented viewing — the way people watch anything now, in clips, out of order, interrupted. The brain still cuts the world into scenes just fine; it's the remembering that falls apart. Khena Swallow at Cornell is the person to call, and Karen Sasmita is now at the National University of Singapore.
- **Wider angle:** For a media or culture outlet: a reported piece on whether short-form, algorithmically shuffled video changes what viewers retain, grounded in this experiment rather than in the usual attention-span panic — and honest about how far a lab study can be pushed.

**Caveats:** Laboratory video task with a likely modest sample; not preregistered; single center; artificial interruption regime (every 60 seconds) does not mirror real viewing.

### 11. Even fluent bilinguals falter when instructions come in their second language

**Journal:** *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology* | **Published:** 9 September 2026
**PMID:** 42717310 | **DOI:** 10.1177/17470218261490089
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — everyday and relatable, with a specific and surprising dissociation; small sample and one language pair.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (1 hits)

**The study:** Thirty-six Persian–English bilinguals followed sequences of five spoken instructions in each of their languages, recalling them either by speaking them back or by carrying them out on a computer screen. The second-language penalty appeared only when participants had to *act* on the instructions, not when they simply repeated them — and phonological short-term memory scores, which were uniformly high, did not predict performance.

**Why it matters:** It locates the hidden cost of operating in a second language not in remembering the words but in doing something with them while holding the context in mind — relevant anywhere safety-critical instructions are given across a language barrier.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the mismatch between fluency and function: these participants could repeat the instruction perfectly and still get it wrong when they had to enact it. Open in a hospital or a workshop where instructions are issued in a language that isn't the listener's first. Elisabet Service at McMaster's ARiEAL center can explain why enactment is the point of failure.
- **Wider angle:** For an occupational health or aviation/medicine trade outlet: a story on multilingual workplaces and instruction protocols, and why verbal read-back — the standard safety check — may be testing the wrong thing.

**Caveats:** Small N (36); single language pair and a highly proficient sample; laboratory screen-based enactment rather than real tasks; predictor analyses underpowered.

### 12. Raised gamma brainwaves in autistic children track memory for faces over four years

**Journal:** *European Neuropsychopharmacology* | **Published:** 10 September 2026
**PMID:** 42721928 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.euroneuro.2026.113864
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — rare longitudinal biomarker data at scale, but the finding is an association with cognition rather than a usable clinical marker.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Drawing on 1597 EEG recordings from 399 autistic and typically developing children in the Autism Biomarkers Consortium for Clinical Trials, assessed at baseline, 6 weeks, 6 months and 4 years, the researchers analyzed resting-state gamma-band power as an index of excitation–inhibition balance. Gamma power was elevated in autistic children, and that elevation was longitudinally associated with lower non-verbal IQ and poorer memory for faces. Gamma power was higher in males than females and declined with age in both groups.

**Why it matters:** It is one of the few large, repeatedly measured tests of the excitation–inhibition hypothesis of autism in humans, and it ties a neural measure to a specific, socially consequential memory ability.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the four-year window — a single resting EEG measure that keeps tracking how well a child remembers faces. Open with what the measurement actually involves: a child sitting quietly in a cap of electrodes. Sara Jane Webb at Seattle Children's Research Institute leads the work and should be pressed on the gap between "associated with" and "biomarker."
- **Wider angle:** For a science-policy or specialist outlet: the biomarker consortium model itself — hundreds of millions spent hunting objective markers for clinical trials in autism, and what a decade of results has and hasn't delivered.

**Caveats:** Observational; group-level associations with wide individual variation, so no diagnostic or prognostic use is supported; multiple senior authors report pharmaceutical consulting and advisory relationships (see conflict statement); attrition over the 4-year timepoint likely.

### 13. Nighttime seizure activity may be stealing the memory benefit of sleep

**Journal:** *Epilepsy & Behavior* | **Published:** 16 September 2026
**PMID:** 42748517 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.yebeh.2026.111269
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a plausible explanation for a long-standing muddle in the epilepsy literature, but it is a synthesis of 13 heterogeneous studies.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** A systematic review of studies published 2000–2025 identified 13 eligible papers covering 581 adults with epilepsy, examining links between sleep architecture or sleep quality and cognition. Nine found significant sleep–cognition correlations and four showed that the overnight memory consolidation benefit is retained in people with epilepsy. Six studies converged on nocturnal interictal epileptic discharges — the brief electrical events that occur between seizures — harming cognition, which the authors argue may disrupt consolidation and explain why sleep's memory benefit sometimes fails to appear in this population.

**Why it matters:** If subclinical nighttime discharges are eroding the consolidation window, that reframes memory complaints in epilepsy as partly a sleep problem rather than an inevitable consequence of the condition — but the evidence is not yet strong enough to change care.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the everyday complaint of people with epilepsy: memory that feels unreliable despite controlled seizures. The candidate culprit is activity happening while they sleep, invisible to them. Ioannis Stavropoulos at King's College London can say what a properly powered study would need to look like — be explicit that this is a hypothesis, not a finding.
- **Wider angle:** A broader feature for a health outlet on the overnight consolidation window as a shared point of vulnerability across conditions — epilepsy, sleep apnea, insomnia, aging — and the emerging interest in protecting it rather than treating the daytime symptom.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of a small, heterogeneous literature (13 studies, 581 patients total); no causal evidence; the authors themselves call for larger and more homogeneous cohorts; risk of bias variable across included studies.

### 14. A depressive bias doesn't stop your brain getting the usual memory boost from a sudden target

**Journal:** *BMC Psychology* | **Published:** 7 September 2026
**PMID:** 42706587 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s40359-026-05313-8
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a Bayesian-supported null on an intuitive prediction, which is worth reporting, but it is a small student study with a preliminary read on mechanism.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Eighty-one university students — 42 with elevated depressive symptoms (BDI-II ≥ 14, confirmed as not meeting clinical criteria) and 39 controls — completed the attentional boost paradigm, in which images shown at the moment a target is detected are remembered better than those shown with a non-target. Both groups showed the effect equally, with Bayesian analysis giving moderate evidence for no group difference. Adding a warning cue before the target improved detection and shifted the memory advantage toward positive images in both groups.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the negative bias associated with low mood does not corrupt the basic arousal-driven mechanism that makes moments of detection memorable — the bias is more context-dependent than blanket.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The angle is the intact machinery: something about depressive cognition that appears untouched, in a literature dominated by deficits. Open on the paradigm itself, which is oddly like scrolling while waiting for a notification. Fangjun Huang's group at Fujian Medical University can be asked what an arousal-based intervention would even look like.
- **Wider angle:** For a psychology-focused outlet: a piece on the value of well-powered null results in depression research, using Bayesian evidence for the null as the organizing idea — a field that has spent decades cataloging differences and is now learning where there aren't any.

**Caveats:** Small N (81); analogue, sub-clinical sample of university students rather than diagnosed patients; single-session laboratory task; mechanism claims are the authors' interpretation and explicitly described as preliminary.

### 15. Decades after epilepsy surgery, most people say life is good — even those still having seizures

**Journal:** *Epilepsy & Behavior* | **Published:** 11 September 2026
**PMID:** 42727324 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.yebeh.2026.111272
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — an unusually long follow-up window (up to 44 years) on a question normally answered at two years, but small and survivorship-prone.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Eighty-six people who had undergone temporal lobe epilepsy surgery 10 to 44 years earlier completed the QOLIE-89 quality-of-life measure and an anxiety inventory. Scores were generally high across all subscales. Seizure freedom remained the strongest predictor, but 55–72% of those still having seizures also reported good quality of life, and predictors included absence of anxiety, ability to work and — for patient-rated sentiment — figural memory performance.

**Why it matters:** Surgical success in epilepsy is scored almost entirely on seizure freedom; over decades, cognitive compensation, working life and shifting expectations appear to matter more than that framing allows.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the long view: we judge brain surgery on outcomes measured two years out, and here is what people say four decades later. Open on a patient operated on in the 1980s. Eli Kyte and Morten Lossius at Oslo's National Centre for Epilepsy can talk about what changes in the twenty years after the usual follow-up stops.
- **Wider angle:** For a medical or health-services outlet: response shift — the well-documented way people recalibrate what "good health" means after a chronic illness — and the awkward question it poses for every patient-reported outcome measure used to judge whether treatments work.

**Caveats:** Small N (86); single center; cross-sectional with no pre-surgical quality-of-life comparison; survivorship bias — those doing worst are least likely to respond decades on; outcomes self-reported.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- **Trauma memory theory keeps failing its own tests.** Last month's "Eye-movement therapy works even when you are not consciously thinking about the memory" (PMID 42531952) and "Eye-movement therapy dampens shame, anger and sadness memories, not just fear" (PMID 42622123) chipped away at EMDR's stated mechanism; this month "Intrusive memories may not be the simple conditioned reflexes we assumed" (PMID 42759257) goes after the conditioning account that sits underneath exposure-based treatment. Both digests come out of *Behaviour Research and Therapy* and both point the same way: the clinical models are more assumption than evidence.
- **PTSD as something other than a fear disorder.** The emotion-general dual-task result (PMID 42622123) and the personalized network "shapes" of PTSD (PMID 42607016) are now joined by "A third of sexual abuse survivors with PTSD report genital arousal when the memory returns" (PMID 42704048), where disgust and self-hatred — not fear, and notably not shame — tracked symptom severity.
- **Positive memories as a clinical lever.** "Daily diaries show mood and PTSD symptoms move together within a day" (PMID 42623486) covered a therapy built on retrieving detailed positive memories; "Talking yourself down after good news makes you read social situations more rigidly" (PMID 42727265) supplies the causal counterpart, showing that deflating a positive memory stiffens negative beliefs about other people.
- **The overnight consolidation window as a shared vulnerability.** Following the insomnia tDCS trial (PMID 42537590) and the sleep-and-source-memory work (PMID 42492165), "Nighttime seizure activity may be stealing the memory benefit of sleep" (PMID 42748517) makes the same argument for epilepsy — treat the night, not the daytime complaint.
- **Well-powered nulls are doing real work.** "To remember to do something later, physically rehearse it" (PMID 42627881) reported a flat null for episodic future thinking; this run adds a Bayesian null in "A depressive bias doesn't stop your brain getting the usual memory boost from a sudden target" (PMID 42706587) and a cross-cultural null in "Your earliest memory dates from the same age whatever culture you grew up in" (PMID 42757868).

**Advances**
- **Memory as social infrastructure moves from questionnaire to real time.** The sleep/conversation study (PMID 42492165) and the cheater-recognition work (PMID 42625545) argued that memory serves social bookkeeping; "People who recall their lives in more detail tell their stories more often" (PMID 42742196) tests it with experience sampling and adds the closeness-calibration twist.
- **The gap between a neural measure and a clinical one.** Last month's single-case "Targeted brain stimulation moves a network — but not the memory it was aimed at" (PMID 42610835) has a large-sample sibling in "Raised gamma brainwaves in autistic children track memory for faces over four years" (PMID 42721928): 1597 EEG recordings, a robust group-level association, and still nothing usable per child.

**Counterpoints**
- **Offloading to the hands: cost or capacity?** "Taking notes you expect to keep makes you encode less deeply than a deliberately shallow task" (PMID 42622798) framed externalizing as corrosive; "Typing the first few digits helps you hold more in working memory" (PMID 42754727) finds that shunting items into a motor routine expands what people can hold, including for the digits they never typed. The reconciliation is probably *expected access* versus *motor rehearsal*, and it is worth a sentence in any future offloading piece.
- **Enactment helps you remember — and is also where you fail.** "Acting out an instruction helps you remember it — even if you have never seen anything" (PMID 42622513) made enactment the reliable route to instruction memory; "Even fluent bilinguals falter when instructions come in their second language" (PMID 42717310) finds the second-language penalty appears *only* when instructions must be acted on, not repeated.

**New themes**
- Narrative and event structure arrives as a cluster: "Event boundaries matter less to your memory than what happens next" (PMID 42760449) and "Interrupting a film scrambles your memory of it but not how you carve it into events" (PMID 42758465) — nothing comparable in the previous digest.
- Long-horizon clinical outcomes and exposure attribution: "Years on the football field damage memory directly; military service does it through PTSD" (PMID 42753419) and "Decades after epilepsy surgery, most people say life is good" (PMID 42727324) both interrogate how brain-injury and neurosurgery outcomes are conventionally scored.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** An unusual share of this batch consists of memory researchers dismantling claims their own field teaches. "Your earliest memory dates from the same age whatever culture you grew up in" (PMID 42757868) finds no cultural offset in childhood amnesia; "Intrusive memories may not be the simple conditioned reflexes we assumed" (PMID 42759257) shows odor cues trigger intrusions that are not the ones they were paired with; "Event boundaries matter less to your memory than what happens next" (PMID 42760449) is a preregistered reanalysis in which the authors reverse the direction of their own model; "Interrupting a film scrambles your memory of it but not how you carve it into events" (PMID 42758465) splits apart two processes routinely treated as one machine; and "A depressive bias doesn't stop your brain getting the usual memory boost from a sudden target" (PMID 42706587) reports Bayesian evidence for no deficit where the literature expects one.

**Why pitch this now:** Individually these read as small corrections, but together they show a field that has started spending its effort on tearing down rather than adding — and the claims coming down (cultural childhood amnesia, cue-conditioned intrusions, event boundaries as the unit of memory) are exactly the ones that have made it into teaching, therapy manuals and popular writing.

**Angle:** A reported piece on memory science's demolition phase: what it takes to kill a well-loved finding, who funds and publishes negative results, and which clinical and classroom practices are quietly still resting on the old versions. For readers who have absorbed pop-psychology memory lore and for the clinicians and teachers who act on it.

**Potential headlines:**
- Memory researchers are spending this year taking apart their own textbooks
