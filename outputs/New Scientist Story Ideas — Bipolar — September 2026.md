### 1. Genes for addiction — not for drinking or smoking as such — explain why bipolar disorder and substance problems travel together

**Journal:** *Biological Psychiatry* | **Published:** 24 August 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42636965 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.biopsych.2026.08.008
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a clean genetic dissociation between "using" and "becoming dependent", built on very large samples; limited by the abstraction of polygenic methods.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** An Oslo-led team used GWAS summary statistics to separate the genetics of substance *use* (drinks per week, ever using cannabis, smoking initiation, prescription opioid use) from the genetics of substance use *disorders* (alcohol, cannabis, opioid, tobacco), then tested how each related to bipolar disorder. Polygenic scores derived from these latent factors were validated in the Norwegian Mother, Father and Child Cohort Study. Bipolar disorder overlapped strongly with the dependence factor and with a shared use/dependence factor, while the "pure use" factor correlated *negatively* with psychiatric disorders and predicted only self-reported lifetime use. Pathway analysis pointed to dopamine signaling and interneuron function in the shared bipolar–dependence architecture.

**Why it matters:** It undercuts the intuitive story that people with bipolar disorder drink and smoke more because of the illness, and suggests instead an inherited liability specific to dependence — a distinction that matters for how comorbidity is conceptualized and studied.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is that the genetic variants nudging people toward *having a drink* are not the ones that link to bipolar disorder — only the ones tied to losing control of it. Open on the long-standing clinical assumption that mania drives excess drinking, then show how the genetics reverses the arrow. Call Kevin O'Connell or Ole Andreassen at the Centre for Precision Psychiatry, University of Oslo.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a genomics or general-science outlet on GWAS-by-subtraction as a technique — researchers now routinely "subtract" one trait from another to isolate hidden causal signatures, with implications well beyond psychiatry. Frame it as statistical dissection of behavior.

**Caveats:** Observational and genetic-correlation based (cannot establish causation); polygenic scores explain small proportions of variance; GWAS samples are overwhelmingly of European ancestry, so findings may not generalize; MoBa outcomes partly self-reported.

### 2. Serotonin abnormality blamed on borderline personality disorder turns out to belong to depression

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychiatric Research* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42173043 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2026.05.028
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a neat reattribution of a well-known brain finding using rare PET data, though group sizes are modest.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Columbia researchers used [11C]WAY-100635 PET to measure serotonin 1A receptor binding across 13 predefined brain regions in people with mood disorders (major depression or bipolar I/II) plus comorbid borderline personality disorder (n=21), mood disorders alone (n=63), and healthy volunteers (n=27). Binding was elevated in the mood disorder groups relative to controls — but post-hoc analysis showed the elevation tracked the mood disorder, not borderline personality disorder. Impulsivity, aggression, depression severity, and suicidal ideation showed no relationship to binding.

**Why it matters:** Borderline personality disorder has long been described as a "serotonin" condition, partly on the strength of findings like this; if the signal actually belongs to comorbid depression, that helps explain why SSRIs, which act on the 5-HT1A autoreceptor, work for mood disorders but disappoint in borderline personality disorder.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the reversal — the serotonin signature attributed to borderline personality disorder for years appears to be depression hiding inside the comorbidity. Open on the clinical reality that most people diagnosed with borderline personality disorder are prescribed antidepressants that barely help. Call M. Elizabeth Sublette or J. John Mann at the New York State Psychiatric Institute.
- **Wider angle:** For a psychiatry-focused or long-read outlet: the comorbidity confound problem across biological psychiatry — how many "biomarkers" for one diagnosis are really markers of the second diagnosis in the same patients? Use this as the worked example.

**Caveats:** Small comorbid subgroup (n=21), cross-sectional, single-center, secondary post-hoc analysis of pooled PET cohorts; several authors report royalties and industry ties (see disclosure) though unrelated to this work.

### 3. More data beats smarter algorithms when predicting bipolar disorder in children

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 1 December 2026 (Epub 14 August 2026)
**PMID:** 42600789 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122378
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a well-designed head-to-head test that punctures AI hype in psychiatry; the topic is technical but the message is sharp.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers compared a simple clinical nomogram, standard statistics (logistic regression, LASSO), four machine-learning models, and a deep-learning neural network for predicting pediatric bipolar disorder, using two independent samples: an academic clinic (N=550) and a community clinic (N=511). All models performed well within the dataset they were trained on and dropped substantially when applied to the other setting. Adding complexity did not help; training on pooled, heterogeneous data did. A single parent-report screening measure, PGBI-10M, was consistently the strongest predictor across every model.

**Why it matters:** It is direct evidence that the bottleneck in psychiatric prediction is the diversity of data, not the sophistication of the model — a corrective to the assumption that deep learning will crack diagnosis.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Sell the deflation: a neural network was no better at spotting bipolar disorder in children than a questionnaire a parent fills in, and every model got worse the moment it left the clinic it was trained in. Open on the mismatch between academic and community clinics, where the same child could be scored differently. Call Eric Youngstrom at Nationwide Children's Hospital.
- **Wider angle:** For a technology or health-policy outlet: "dataset shift" as the quiet failure mode of clinical AI, with the case for open, pooled psychiatric datasets — and the governance and privacy fights that would entail.

**Caveats:** Two US clinical samples only, so population may not generalize; retrospective secondary analysis of existing cohorts; posted earlier as a medRxiv preprint; several authors declare extensive industry and commercial interests (see disclosure), including consultancy on psychological assessment.

### 4. Most people with bipolar depression would not qualify for the trials that produced their treatment guidelines

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 1 December 2026 (Epub 10 August 2026)
**PMID:** 42575197 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122363
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a striking, readable number with a strong dose-response, undercut by modest sample size and self-rated outcomes.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Clinicians in Naples applied a Trial Eligibility Index to 122 outpatients being treated for acute bipolar depression, asking how many would have been enrolled in the average randomized controlled trial behind current guidelines. Only 43.4% would have been eligible. Those who would have qualified had nearly ten times the odds of reporting that their medication had helped (OR=9.83, 95% CI 4.17–23.18), a result that held across different eligibility thresholds.

**Why it matters:** It quantifies something clinicians suspect but rarely measure — the patients seen in real clinics are messier than trial participants, and the guideline evidence may simply not describe them.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the arithmetic: fewer than half of real bipolar depression patients resemble the people the drug evidence was built on, and the other half report far less benefit. Open on a composite patient with the comorbidities — substance use, medical illness, suicidality — that get you excluded from trials. Call Michele Fornaro at University of Naples Federico II.
- **Wider angle:** For a health-policy or medical outlet: the generalizability crisis across medicine, from oncology to cardiology, and the growing push for pragmatic trials with deliberately broad eligibility. This study is a psychiatric datapoint in a much bigger argument.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational (cannot establish causation); N=122, single center; primary outcome is patient-perceived improvement, i.e. self-reported and unblinded; senior author reports extensive pharmaceutical advisory and honorarium relationships (Janssen, Lundbeck, Otsuka and others).

### 5. Stimulating the balance organ changes how quickly you recognize your own face

**Journal:** *Biological Psychology* | **Published:** September 2026 (Epub 11 June 2026)
**PMID:** 42276516 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.biopsycho.2026.109326
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — genuinely novel causal link between the inner ear and self-recognition, but N=50 and effects are reaction-time-level.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Fifty healthy volunteers did explicit and implicit face-recognition tasks while receiving sinusoidal galvanic vestibular stimulation — weak current across the mastoids that fools the balance system — or sham. Everyone showed the usual "self advantage", recognizing their own face fastest. But stimulation polarity mattered: right-anodal current slowed self-face recognition, while left-anodal current slowed recognition of strangers and sped up recognition of familiar others, erasing the self advantage and converting it into a general familiarity effect.

**Why it matters:** It provides causal evidence that the vestibular system — usually filed under balance and spatial orientation — contributes to the sense of self at the level of face identity, extending vestibular contributions to bodily self-representation into the domain of who you recognize as "me".

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is bodily: nudge the balance organ with a mild current and the privileged status of your own face in your brain quietly dissolves into ordinary familiarity. Open with the volunteer sitting in front of a screen, electrodes behind the ears, unable to say why their own face suddenly feels no more special than a friend's. Call Roberto Gammeri or Raffaella Ricci at the SAN Lab, University of Turin.
- **Wider angle:** For a features desk or a neurology-focused outlet: what vestibular disorders do to the sense of self — patients with chronic dizziness frequently report depersonalization, and this line of work offers a mechanism. A patient-led piece on the underexplored psychiatry of the inner ear.

**Caveats:** Small N (50) split across two polarity groups; healthy volunteers only; reaction-time effects appeared in the explicit task only; short-lived laboratory manipulation with no follow-up.

### 6. Two stages of treatment-resistant depression turn out to be two different conditions

**Journal:** *International Clinical Psychopharmacology* | **Published:** 1 September 2026 (Epub 26 March 2026)
**PMID:** 41904070 | **DOI:** 10.1097/YIC.0000000000000625
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — large prospective inpatient cohort with a clear split, but the finding is a refinement of an existing staging idea rather than a new one.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers in Milan prospectively followed 538 depressed inpatients, classifying them as non-resistant, TRD1 (two or more failed antidepressants at minimum licensed dose) or TRD2 (two or more failures across different drug classes at maximum tolerated dose), and tracked symptoms at admission and at 2, 4 and 8 weeks. TRD1 patients (24%) had earlier illness onset and far more personality disorders, anxiety, smoking and substance use — yet improved at a normal rate. Only TRD2 patients (29%) showed genuinely blunted improvement over eight weeks.

**Why it matters:** "Treatment-resistant" is currently a single label attached to roughly a third of depressed patients; this suggests it lumps together a complicated-but-responsive group with a biologically stubborn one, which matters for who gets offered ketamine, ECT or neuromodulation.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the split: half of what psychiatry calls treatment resistance may be complexity, not resistance — patients whose depression sits alongside anxiety, addiction and personality difficulties, and who improve fine once treated properly. Open on the label itself and what it does to a patient's expectations. Call Raffaella Zanardi at IRCCS San Raffaele Turro, Milan.
- **Wider angle:** For a specialist medical or drug-development outlet: how the definition of treatment-resistant depression is being rewritten just as billion-dollar drugs (esketamine, psilocybin, neuromodulation devices) are being licensed against it — and what a shifting denominator does to efficacy claims.

**Caveats:** Observational, single-center inpatient cohort (cannot establish causation); naturalistic treatment, so groups received different interventions by design; eight-week follow-up only; results may not generalize to outpatients.

### 7. Veterans with PTSD improve faster in borderline personality disorder therapy, not slower

**Journal:** *Cognitive Behaviour Therapy* | **Published:** September 2026 (Epub 14 October 2025)
**PMID:** 41084836 | **DOI:** 10.1080/16506073.2025.2572480
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a useful contradiction of the "too complex to treat" assumption, but small and uncontrolled.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Sixty-two US veterans entering full-model dialectical behavior therapy at a VA medical center were tracked; 31 had probable or confirmed PTSD alongside borderline personality disorder symptoms. Comorbid PTSD did not predict worse baseline severity, worse engagement, or worse outcomes. In fact, veterans with both conditions showed steeper reductions in borderline symptoms over the course of treatment than those without PTSD.

**Why it matters:** Much of the evidence for DBT comes from trials with deliberately clean diagnostic profiles — often excluding bipolar disorder and other comorbidities — and clinicians frequently assume complex patients will do worse. Here, the complex patients did at least as well.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The counterintuitive line is that the "hardest" patients were not the hardest: veterans carrying both trauma and borderline symptoms dropped their symptoms faster in a therapy designed for only one of them. Open on the routine gatekeeping that excludes complex patients from trials — and sometimes from treatment. Call Allison Schreiber at the University of Kentucky College of Medicine.
- **Wider angle:** For a health or veterans-affairs outlet: the diagnostic-purity problem in psychotherapy research, and what happens when treatments developed on tidy samples meet the people who actually turn up at the clinic. Could run with data on trial exclusion rates across psychotherapy RCTs.

**Caveats:** Small N (62); no control or comparison arm — everyone received DBT, so improvement cannot be attributed to therapy with confidence; single VA center; outcomes self-reported; PTSD diagnosis was "probable" in most cases; regression to the mean is a plausible partial explanation for steeper decline in the more symptomatic group.

### 8. Bipolar disorder and schizophrenia look identical in blood tests at the first psychotic episode

**Journal:** *International Journal of Psychiatry in Clinical Practice* | **Published:** September 2026 (Epub 11 May 2026)
**PMID:** 42112712 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13651501.2026.2669132
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — the null result between diagnoses is the interesting part, but the design is cross-sectional and the markers are crude.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Turkish researchers analyzed routine blood counts taken within 48 hours of admission from 68 medication-naive patients hospitalized for a first psychotic episode during mania in bipolar disorder, 55 with schizophrenia spectrum disorders, and 61 matched healthy controls. Both patient groups showed raised white cells, neutrophils, monocytes and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratios versus controls — but were indistinguishable from each other. Only two small differences separated them from controls in a condition-specific way: lower red cell counts in bipolar disorder, lower eosinophils in schizophrenia.

**Why it matters:** It adds to evidence that low-grade immune activation accompanies psychosis regardless of which diagnosis is eventually given, which cuts against hopes that simple inflammatory markers could triage bipolar disorder from schizophrenia at first presentation.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the failure: routine blood work picks up inflammation in first-episode psychosis but cannot tell bipolar disorder from schizophrenia — the diagnostic distinction clinicians agonize over for months. Open on the emergency admission where the diagnosis is a coin flip. Call Esra Uzun Uysal at Bakırköy Training and Research Hospital, Istanbul.
- **Wider angle:** For a science or medical outlet: the immuno-psychiatry story at mid-life — a decade after inflammation became the hot hypothesis in mood disorders, what has it actually delivered? A stock-take feature, and a natural pairing with the ongoing childhood-inflammation cohort work.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational; single-center; retrospective use of routine hemogram records; acute hospitalization, stress and physical illness all affect white cell counts and are hard to exclude; small differences of uncertain clinical meaning; population may not generalize.

### 9. Shame about a bipolar diagnosis damages daily functioning through a specific route

**Journal:** *Psychiatric Rehabilitation Journal* | **Published:** September 2026 (Epub 11 May 2026)
**PMID:** 42113129 | **DOI:** 10.1037/prj0000688
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a clear mediation result on an underserved population, but cross-sectional and largely confirmatory of stigma research.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers assessed 110 Tunisian adults with bipolar disorder in remission on internalized stigma, coping style, functioning, self-esteem, cognitive insight and perceived social support. A quarter had clinically significant internalized stigma, with alienation and social withdrawal the strongest components. Mediation analysis showed the link between internalized stigma and poorer functioning ran specifically through perceived devaluation and discrimination — not through coping style, insight, self-esteem or social support, none of which mediated anything.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that the functional cost of stigma in bipolar disorder is carried by the expectation of being devalued by others, rather than by how people cope or how much support they have — a pointer to which target interventions should aim at, though only a pointer.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The finding worth leading on is what *didn't* matter: self-esteem, social support and coping strategies all failed to buffer the effect of internalized stigma on functioning, while the belief that other people look down on you did the damage. Open on a person in full remission who still won't tell an employer. Call Bochra Nourhène Saguem at the University of Sousse.
- **Wider angle:** For a global-health or social-science outlet: mental illness stigma outside the Anglophone research bubble. Almost all stigma instruments were developed in North America and Europe; this study asks what they measure in a Tunisian clinic, and whether anti-stigma campaigns designed elsewhere would transfer.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional (mediation here is statistical, not causal); N=110, single site; all measures self-reported; remitted outpatients in one Tunisian region may not generalize.

### 10. Loading the dose of a long-acting antipsychotic upfront shortens hospital stays for women

**Journal:** *Journal of Clinical Psychopharmacology* | **Published:** September–October 2026 (Epub 2 April 2026)
**PMID:** 41924987 | **DOI:** 10.1097/JCP.0000000000002183
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — clinically relevant and sex-specific, but retrospective, small and non-randomized.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** A retrospective cohort of 87 female inpatients with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder in Istanbul compared two ways of starting long-acting injectable aripiprazole: the standard single injection plus 14 days of oral tablets, versus a newer two-injection start with a single 20 mg oral dose. The two-injection group left hospital sooner (8.1 vs 11.8 days), were less likely to be readmitted within a year (17.0% vs 37.5%), and took longer to relapse when they did.

**Why it matters:** Long-acting injections exist precisely because taking daily tablets is hard; a regimen that removes the two-week oral overlap could remove a real point of failure — but this is early, non-randomized evidence in women only.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The angle is the two-week gap nobody talks about: patients are discharged on an injection *plus* a fortnight of pills, and that fortnight is where adherence breaks. Frame around the discharge day and the tablet box that goes unopened. Call Ahmet Ucok at Moodist Psychiatry Hospital, Istanbul. Do not present this as grounds for changing prescribing.
- **Wider angle:** For a pharmacy or medicines-focused outlet: the near-total absence of sex-stratified data in antipsychotic dosing, despite well-documented differences in metabolism, body composition and prolactin effects. This study is unusual simply for being all-female.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, non-randomized cohort (cannot establish causation); small N (87); single-center; female inpatients only, so results may not generalize to men or outpatients; clinicians chose the regimen, so allocation was likely confounded by patient characteristics; adverse events poorly documented.

### 11. Memory circuits go wrong in different ways in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychiatric Research* | **Published:** 27 August 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42679550 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2026.08.027
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a careful direct comparison of two disorders rarely scanned side by side, but the effects are subtle and the story is technical.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Montreal researchers compared functional connectivity of six hippocampal subregions — anterior, mid and posterior on each side — in people with schizophrenia, people with bipolar disorder, and healthy controls, both at rest and during memory tasks. Some abnormalities were shared: reduced connectivity between the right mid-hippocampus and temporal cortex appeared in both disorders. Others were specific: hyperconnectivity between the right posterior hippocampus and inferior temporal gyrus occurred only in schizophrenia. One anterior hippocampal pattern was schizophrenia-specific at rest but shared once participants were actually doing a memory task.

**Why it matters:** Treating the hippocampus as a single structure has blurred the differences between these two diagnoses; splitting it along its long axis, and scanning during a task rather than at rest, reveals overlap and divergence that resting-state work alone misses.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The nice detail is context-dependence — one brain difference looked specific to schizophrenia when participants lay still, and stopped being specific the moment they were asked to remember something. Use that to open on the limits of resting-state brain imaging. Call Stéphane Potvin at the Institut Universitaire en Santé Mentale de Montréal.
- **Wider angle:** For a neuroscience-literate outlet: the long-axis hippocampus as an idea — the growing evidence that its front and back ends do quite different jobs (emotion and context versus fine spatial and episodic detail), and what that reorganization is doing to memory research generally.

**Caveats:** Sample sizes not stated in the abstract and likely modest for imaging; cross-sectional and observational; participants almost certainly medicated, a persistent confound in psychiatric neuroimaging; multiple seeds and two contexts raise multiple-comparison concerns; single-center.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Five studies in this batch converge on the same problem from different directions — psychiatric evidence is built on unusually tidy patients, and it does not survive contact with the messy ones. "Most people with bipolar depression would not qualify for the trials that produced their treatment guidelines" (PMID 42575197) puts a number on the exclusion (43.4% eligible, with the eligible group nearly ten times more likely to say their medication helped); "Serotonin abnormality blamed on borderline personality disorder turns out to belong to depression" (PMID 42173043) shows a signature biomarker was really a second diagnosis hiding in the comorbidity; "Veterans with PTSD improve faster in borderline personality disorder therapy, not slower" (PMID 41084836) and "Two stages of treatment-resistant depression turn out to be two different conditions" (PMID 41904070) both find that the "complicated" patients routinely screened out of research do fine, or fine for reasons the label obscures; and "More data beats smarter algorithms when predicting bipolar disorder in children" (PMID 42600789) shows prediction models trained in an academic clinic degrade the moment they meet a community one.

**Why pitch this now:** Guidelines, diagnostic labels and a new generation of prediction tools are all being written and licensed on samples that describe a minority of the people who actually turn up in clinics — and this batch is the first time the size of that gap has been quantified across drugs, therapy, biomarkers and algorithms at once.

**Angle:** A reported feature following one composite, entirely ordinary patient — bipolar depression plus a drinking problem, some trauma history, a personality disorder diagnosis — through every stage where the evidence base quietly stops applying to them: the trial they can't enroll in, the biomarker that belongs to someone else's diagnosis, the algorithm trained down the road, the label that predicts their prognosis wrongly. For a general science-literate audience interested in how medical evidence gets made, not just what it says.

**Potential headlines:**
- Fewer than half of bipolar depression patients match the trials behind their prescriptions
- The patients psychiatry calls too complicated to study keep doing better than expected
- Your diagnosis has a research double, and the evidence was written for them

**Potential outlets:**
- **New Scientist Features** — has the room for a multi-study, mechanism-plus-policy piece and an audience already primed by coverage of replication and generalizability problems in medicine.
- **STAT** — the trial-eligibility arithmetic and the "shifting denominator" for treatment-resistant depression land directly in their drug-development and regulatory beat, especially with esketamine and psilocybin being licensed against that very label.
- **Undark** — well suited to the critical, methods-forward framing of how exclusion criteria and comorbidity confounds shape what psychiatry believes it knows.
- **Wired** — would take the dataset-shift strand (PMID 42600789) as the spine, with the trial-eligibility work as evidence that clinical AI inherits an older problem rather than solving it.
