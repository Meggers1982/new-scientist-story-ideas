### 1. Moderates aren't seen as neutral — partisans treat them as the enemy

**Journal:** *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 27
**PMID:** 42507376 | **DOI:** 10.1037/xge0001957
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 9/10 — Highly counterintuitive finding with immediate societal resonance around political polarisation, strong preregistered multi-study design, large combined N, and a clear mechanism — a near-ideal NS Mind pitch.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Across five preregistered experiments with 3,272 participants, researchers found that people who hold a partisan position on a sociopolitical issue consistently perceive moderates — those expressing genuinely centrist views — as belonging to the opposing ideological out-group rather than as neutral. The 'moderate as out-group' effect was strongest when the issue was morally charged and when the opposing side was perceived as a threat. The mechanism appears to be that partisans read a moderate's failure to condemn the other side as tacit endorsement of an immoral enemy, rather than as independent thinking.

**Why it matters:** The findings suggest that in polarised societies, attempting to take a balanced position may paradoxically alienate both sides rather than bridge them — a finding with implications for political communication, conflict resolution, and media framing.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Trying to be the reasonable one in an argument makes both sides think you're their enemy" Expressing a moderate, balanced view on a contested political issue doesn't win people over — it makes partisans on both sides more likely to see you as an opponent, according to five experiments with over 3,000 participants. Political common sense says that taking a middle-ground position should be the safest, most broadly appealing move. New research turns that assumption on its head: partisans don't see moderates as neutral arbiters — they see them as enemies. The key finding is that the effect is driven not by moderates failing to love the in-group, but by their failure to hate the out-group, which partisans interpret as covert sympathy with a threatening, immoral ideology. Crucially, this only happened when issues were both moralised and the other side was perceived as a genuine threat — conditions that describe most major political debates today. Why it fits: At a moment of intense political polarisation, NS Mind readers will immediately recognise this dynamic from their own lives — and the psychological mechanism behind it is genuinely surprising and counterintuitive.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** self-reported outcomes, observational design (experimental vignette-based). Studies used hypothetical vignettes rather than real political interactions; participants were likely drawn from US online panels (generalisability to other political cultures uncertain); self-reported attitudes may not predict actual behaviour toward moderates in real life *Editor's fact-check note: Abstract confirms five preregistered studies, N=3,272 total. Effect described accurately. The studies are experimental (vignette-based), so 'observational design' is a mild flag — the design is experimental but uses self-report attitude measures; noted accordingly.*

### 2. Early risers are not the healthiest — intermediate chronotypes have the lowest heart attack risk

**Journal:** *Sleep Health* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 24
**PMID:** 42498583 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.sleh.2026.05.010
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 9/10 — Strongly counterintuitive finding (morning types are not the healthiest) with a large longitudinal cohort, clean design, clear lifestyle hook, and societal relevance to the 'early bird' cultural myth.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers analysed data from 4,578 adults aged 40 and over enrolled in the Sleep Heart Health Study, following them for a mean of 10.6 years to track incident myocardial infarction. Using the Munich Chronotype Questionnaire, they categorised participants as morning, intermediate, or evening types. Both morning (HR=1.50) and evening (HR=1.40) chronotypes were associated with significantly higher heart attack risk compared with intermediate types, with the associations strongest among those with obstructive sleep apnea. The conventional wisdom that being a 'morning person' is best for cardiovascular health was not supported.

**Why it matters:** The finding challenges the widespread assumption that early rising is inherently healthier, suggesting that extremes of sleep timing — in either direction — may be associated with greater cardiovascular risk, particularly for people with sleep apnea.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Being a morning person doesn't protect your heart — it may actually raise your risk" The cultural myth that early risers are the healthiest people has taken a hit: a decade-long study finds that morning chronotypes face a 50% higher risk of heart attack than those with intermediate sleep timing. Among nearly 4,600 adults tracked for over a decade, both night owls and early birds had significantly higher rates of myocardial infarction than people with intermediate sleep preferences — and the effect was even stronger in those with sleep apnea. The research, a secondary analysis of the large Sleep Heart Health Study, challenges the 'early to bed, early to rise' health narrative that dominates wellness culture. It raises questions about whether it is the extremity of sleep timing itself, rather than its direction, that matters for the heart. Why it fits: Sleep chronotype is a topic NS Mind readers are actively curious about, and this finding directly challenges a deeply held popular belief promoted in productivity culture.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** observational design, self-reported outcomes, secondary analysis. Secondary analysis with observational design means causation cannot be established; chronotype was self-reported; it is unclear whether the findings fully account for all confounders such as shift work; the sample was adults aged 40+ so may not apply to younger people *Editor's fact-check note: The abstract clearly states this is a secondary analysis of the Sleep Heart Health Study (NCT00005275). ### 11. Your brain responds to someone else's eyes differently from arrows — and scientists still don't know why

**Journal:** *Consciousness and Cognition* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 25
**PMID:** 42501672 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.concog.2026.104101
**Novelty:** Included for relevance
**NS fit:** 6/10 — Interesting social-cognition puzzle with clear relevance to neurodiversity and social attention, but incremental rather than paradigm-shifting, and the finding is primarily a null result (joint attention does not explain the effect) rather than a counterintuitive positive finding.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers ran four experiments in which participants identified the direction of a peripheral target cued by either a gaze stimulus or an arrow, varying the fixation stimulus and adding eye-movement requirements to test whether joint attention explains a known brain-response anomaly. While arrows reliably produced faster responses when the cue and target matched (standard congruency effect), averted gaze stimuli consistently produced the opposite pattern — faster responses when cue and target direction conflicted (reversed congruency effect). Crucially, manipulations designed to invoke joint attention did not eliminate this gaze-specific effect, suggesting joint attention alone does not explain why eyes are processed so differently from arrows. Sample size is not reported in the abstract.

**Why it matters:** Understanding why the human brain processes social cues like gaze in a fundamentally different way from non-social directional signals could shed light on the neural underpinnings of social attention and conditions like autism where gaze processing is atypical.
