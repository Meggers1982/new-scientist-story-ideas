### 1. Autistic children keep more regular bedtimes than their peers — but get up at wildly different times

**Journal:** *Autism* | **Published:** 13 August 2026
**PMID:** 42593049 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13623613261477806
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a nationally representative dataset splits "sleep regularity" into two halves that point in opposite directions, which is both surprising and easy to explain.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Using parent-reported data on 11,462 US children aged 5–17 from the 2022 and 2024 National Health Interview Survey, researchers separated bedtime regularity from wake-time regularity. After adjusting for sociodemographics, family context, daytime fatigue and mental health, an autism diagnosis was associated with *lower* odds of an irregular bedtime (aOR 0.64) but *higher* odds of an irregular wake-up time (aOR 1.60). The wake-time effect was concentrated in younger children (aOR 2.27) and absent in adolescents.

**Why it matters:** Sleep regularity is usually treated as one number; this suggests the two ends of the night behave differently and that morning timing, not bedtime, may be where autistic children's schedules come apart.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a family who have nailed the bedtime routine — bath, book, lights out at 7:30 every night — and still can't predict what time their child will surface. The hook is that the most-recommended sleep intervention (a fixed bedtime) appears to be the part autistic families already have covered, while the neglected half of the equation is waking. Worth calling Chia-Shuan Chang at the University of Maryland; also worth a comment from a paediatric sleep researcher on why regularity metrics collapse both ends into one score.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a parenting or health outlet on the rise of "sleep regularity" as a health metric — wearables now sell a Sleep Regularity Index, and epidemiology increasingly finds timing consistency predicts mortality better than duration. Ask whether a single composite score hides clinically meaningful asymmetries.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational; parent-reported sleep timing rather than actigraphy; ASD diagnosis is parent-reported; wide confidence intervals sit close to 1 for both main estimates; no information on medication or comorbid conditions that affect morning waking.

### 2. Three nights of poor sleep predict next-day mood — and how long you slept doesn't

**Journal:** *Behaviour Research and Therapy* | **Published:** 14 August 2026
**PMID:** 42617377 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.brat.2026.105143
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — intensive real-world sampling in a genuinely high-risk group, with a clean and quotable result; limited by self-report and a clinical sample.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 136 psychiatric inpatients discharged after a recent suicidal crisis completed ecological momentary assessments of sleep quality, total sleep time and mood in daily life. Multilevel models compared different ways of summarising sleep: single-night scores, cumulative "pile-up" indices and variability measures. A three-night cumulative measure of sleep *quality* consistently outperformed everything else in predicting higher positive and lower negative affect the next day, both within and between people — while total sleep time showed no robust association with mood at all.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the mood cost of bad sleep accumulates across nights rather than resetting each morning, and that hours in bed — the number most people and most sleep trackers fixate on — may be the wrong variable for predicting how you'll feel.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the mismatch between what we measure and what matters: your tracker gives you 7 h 12 m and a sleep score, but the thing that predicts tomorrow's mood is how the last three nights felt, added together. Open on the clinical scenario the study was built for — the fragile weeks after discharge from a psychiatric ward, when clinicians want an early-warning signal. Call Lena Spangenberg at Leipzig University. Be explicit that this is prediction of *affect*, not of suicidal behaviour — the authors say that link remains to be tested.
- **Wider angle:** A digital-health piece for a technology or business outlet on "just-in-time" mental health interventions: if a rolling three-night quality index is the best short-term marker of affective vulnerability, what would a phone app that acts on it actually do, and who is building one? Include the regulatory and false-alarm problems.

**Caveats:** N = 136 but a very specific clinical population that may not generalise to healthy sleepers; sleep and affect both self-reported via EMA (no actigraphy or PSG); observational within-person design cannot establish causation; funded by the German Research Foundation (non-commercial); outcome is affect, not suicidal behaviour.

### 3. Half of people in a depressive episode also meet criteria for nightmare disorder

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychiatric Research* | **Published:** 14 August 2026
**PMID:** 42617369 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2026.08.021
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a strikingly high prevalence figure plus objective actigraphy backing, though the case-control design can't tell us direction.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** In a prospective case-control study of adults with a DSM-5 major depressive episode, researchers established a diagnostic cut-off for the Nightmare Severity Index (≥10, sensitivity 96.7%, specificity 93.9%) and used it to split patients into those with and without nightmare disorder. Nearly half (49.6%) met criteria. Those patients had more severe depression, anxiety and insomnia, higher current and lifetime suicidality, and — on actigraphy — longer sleep latency, poorer sleep continuity and lower sleep efficiency. Anxiety severity statistically mediated the nightmare–suicidality link.

**Why it matters:** Nightmares are rarely asked about in a routine depression consultation, yet this positions them as a marker of a distinct and more severe depressive phenotype — and nightmares, unlike depression itself, have specific, well-tested behavioural treatments.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the question nobody asks: patients are routinely asked whether they're sleeping, almost never what they're dreaming. Sell the number — one in two people in a depressive episode has a diagnosable nightmare disorder — plus the objective actigraphy showing their nights really are more broken. Call Pierre A. Geoffroy or Julia Maruani at Hôpital Bichat / Centre ChronoS in Paris. Avoid implying nightmares cause suicidality: this is cross-sectional and the mediation is statistical.
- **Wider angle:** A longer feature for a general-interest magazine on the clinical rehabilitation of the nightmare — from Freudian curiosity to a treatable target, via imagery rehearsal therapy and the PTSD literature. Includes the emerging question of whether treating the dream changes the daytime illness.

**Caveats:** Sample size not stated in the abstract; single-centre, routine-care recruitment; case-control and cross-sectional, so causal direction is unknown; the NSI cut-off was derived and evaluated in the same sample, so it needs independent validation; several outcomes self-reported.

### 4. It's long sleep, not short sleep, that tracks with inflammation in 30,000 US adults

**Journal:** *Psychiatry Research* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 29 July 2026)
**PMID:** 42546640 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117347
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — large, well-known dataset and a result that cuts against the "short sleep inflames you" narrative, but cross-sectional with crude sleep measures.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Researchers analysed 29,605 adults from NHANES 2005–2020, relating self-reported sleep trouble and sleep duration to six composite blood inflammatory indices (NLR, PLR, LMR, SII, SIRI, PIV). In fully adjusted models, self-reported sleep trouble showed no robust association with systemic inflammation overall. Sleeping more than nine hours was consistently linked to elevated NLR, SII, SIRI and PIV; sleeping under seven hours showed no robust association after full adjustment. Associations with sleep trouble emerged only in men, people with BMI ≥25 and adults over 40.

**Why it matters:** The popular story is that skimping on sleep drives inflammation; here the inflammatory signal sits at the long end of the distribution instead — most plausibly because long sleep is a symptom of underlying illness rather than a cause of it.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the reversal: the nine-hour sleeper, not the six-hour sleeper, showed the raised inflammatory markers. The intellectually honest hook is reverse causation — is long sleep a warning light rather than a fault? Frame it as a test case for how epidemiology handles U-shaped curves. Worth calling Huawei Wang at Kunming Medical University, plus an independent sleep epidemiologist on why the long-sleep signal keeps reappearing across outcomes.
- **Wider angle:** For a specialist health or clinical outlet: composite blood-count ratios (SII, SIRI, PIV) are being applied to almost every exposure in NHANES-style papers. A critical piece on whether these cheap derived indices are meaningful biomarkers or a publication engine.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional; cannot establish causation; sleep duration and sleep trouble both self-reported and coarsely categorised; heavy multiple testing across six indices and three stratification variables; residual confounding by chronic illness is the obvious explanation for the long-sleep finding and is acknowledged by the authors.

### 5. Sleep changes forecast later depression — but depression doesn't forecast sleep

**Journal:** *Psychiatry Research* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 28 July 2026)
**PMID:** 42541934 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117352
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — challenges the standard "bidirectional" line with a decade of national panel data, though the effect is small and sleep is self-reported.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** A secondary analysis of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (2011–2020) applied a panel vector autoregression model to 12,747 middle-aged and older adults with at least three repeated measures of sleep duration and CES-D depressive symptoms. Increases in sleep duration were followed by reductions in depressive symptoms at the next wave (β = −0.064), while changes in depressive symptoms showed no significant lagged association with subsequent sleep duration (β = −0.001).

**Why it matters:** Sleep problems and depression are almost always described as a two-way street; in this large longitudinal sample the traffic ran one way, which would make sleep duration a more attractive intervention target than the reverse framing implies.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Sell the asymmetry. The received wisdom — depression wrecks your sleep, bad sleep deepens depression, round and round — is not what a decade of data from nearly 13,000 older Chinese adults shows. Open on the methodological point: with waves years apart, you can ask which change comes first. Call Yongli Yang at Zhengzhou University. Note the effect size is modest and the sleep measure is a single self-reported number.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-flavoured feature for a science or statistics-literate outlet on how "bidirectional" became psychiatry's default answer, and how cross-lagged panel models are now being used — and criticised — to break ties between co-travelling symptoms.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of an existing cohort; observational, so causal claims remain inferential despite the lagged design; sleep duration self-reported at wide intervals (roughly two years) and reduced to hours per night; findings in middle-aged and older Chinese adults may not generalise; small coefficient.

### 6. Genetic analysis suggests PTSD raises the risk of sleep apnoea — but not the other way round

**Journal:** *European Journal of Psychotraumatology* | **Published:** 18 August 2026
**PMID:** 42611531 | **DOI:** 10.1080/20008066.2026.2709174
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a directional genetic result on a link usually explained by weight and lifestyle; limited by the well-known fragilities of Mendelian randomisation.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Using genome-wide association summary statistics, researchers found a substantial genetic correlation between PTSD and obstructive sleep apnoea (rg ≈ 0.30–0.34), identified 12 novel shared variants, and pinpointed two colocalised causal loci — TRAF3 and TMEM106B — with posterior probabilities above 95%. Heritability was enriched in cerebellum and cortex rather than in metabolic tissue. Two-sample Mendelian randomisation indicated genetically predicted PTSD raised OSA risk (OR 3.24) with no significant effect in the reverse direction.

**Why it matters:** Sleep apnoea in trauma survivors is usually attributed downstream — to weight gain, alcohol, medication. Brain-tissue enrichment and a one-way genetic signal point instead to a shared neural substrate, which would be a new way to think about why the two conditions cluster.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead on the direction of the arrow: trauma appears to push people towards a breathing disorder, not the reverse. The counterintuitive detail is the tissue map — heritability concentrated in the cerebellum, an area rarely invoked in either condition. Call the corresponding group at Soochow University's Brain and Nerve Research Laboratory, and pair with a sceptical MR methodologist, because an OR of 3.24 from IVW is large enough to invite questions about instrument validity and sample overlap.
- **Wider angle:** For a specialist medical or veterans-affairs outlet: CPAP adherence is notoriously poor in PTSD patients, and apnoea worsens nightmares. A service-focused piece on the two-way clinical tangle of trauma and breathing, regardless of what the genetics say.

**Caveats:** No new participants — entirely secondary analysis of GWAS summary statistics; MR assumptions (no pleiotropy, valid instruments) are strong and only partly testable; GWAS samples are overwhelmingly European-ancestry; OSA is under-diagnosed, which biases case definition; effect sizes from MR are not directly interpretable as clinical risk.

### 7. Heart-and-metabolic illness makes depression harder to recover from — and poor sleep makes it worse still

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 21 August 2026
**PMID:** 42628578 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122408
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — the focus on *reversion* (getting better) rather than onset is the fresh part, and it replicates across two national cohorts on two continents.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)

**The study:** Multi-state Markov and Cox models were applied to 14,468 adults in China (CHARLS) and 8,551 in England (ELSA), tracking transitions between depressive and non-depressive states. Cardiometabolic multimorbidity raised the hazard of developing depressive symptoms (HR 1.29 in China, 1.21 in England) and, more strikingly, lowered the hazard of recovering from them (HR 0.79 and 0.74). Unhealthy sleep duration (<6 or ≥9 hours) showed significant additive interaction with multimorbidity in both cohorts.

**Why it matters:** Most cardiometabolic–depression research asks who gets depressed; this asks who gets out again, and finds physical multimorbidity acts as a brake on recovery — with sleep duration amplifying the effect.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Frame it around exit, not entry: two big ageing cohorts show that having diabetes plus heart disease doesn't just make depression more likely, it makes escaping it less likely — and the combination with short or long sleep is worse than either alone. Open on the clinical reality of an older patient managing three conditions at once. Contact the corresponding author at Shandong Second Medical University; pair with a UK ELSA investigator for the English side.
- **Wider angle:** A policy-facing piece for a health-service or ageing outlet on multimorbidity as the defining challenge of ageing populations, and why single-disease clinics and single-disease trials systematically miss the interactions that determine who recovers.

**Caveats:** Observational cohorts; depressive symptoms measured by CES-D screening thresholds, not diagnostic interview, so "reversion" may partly reflect measurement noise around a cut-off; sleep duration self-reported at baseline only; residual confounding by medication, disability and socioeconomic position likely.

### 8. "You can sleep when you die": how hustle culture rations sleep in women of colour

**Journal:** *Sleep Health* | **Published:** 21 August 2026
**PMID:** 42624738 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.sleh.2026.07.011
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — qualitative rather than quantitative, but the framing is vivid and it names a mechanism behind a much-cited disparity.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Eight focus groups with 77 female community health workers from African immigrant/refugee, Black/African American, Hispanic/Latinx and Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander communities in Utah were analysed using grounded theory. Participants consistently described trading sleep for caregiving, work and community obligations, and identified "hustle culture" — the confluence of work, family and community demands compounded by racial and acculturation stress — as the organising theme. Sleep-related stigma (sleep as laziness) and culturally rooted practices emerged as key context for designing interventions.

**Why it matters:** Sleep disparities by race and gender are well documented statistically but thinly explained; this puts language and mechanism to the trade-offs, which is what intervention designers actually need.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the title quote and the stigma finding — that in several of these communities, sleeping enough is read as laziness, so people conceal it. The hook for a Mind readership is that "sleep hygiene" advice presumes a discretionary evening; for these women there isn't one. Call Sharon Talboys or Kelly Glazer Baron at the University of Utah. Frame as a study of why generic sleep advice fails, not as a claim about health outcomes.
- **Wider angle:** A reported feature for a general-interest or social-affairs outlet on sleep as a distributed resource — night-shift work, caregiving, housing noise, second jobs — and the emerging "sleep security" concept now being argued for in the same journal. Pairs naturally with interviews outside academia.

**Caveats:** Qualitative, no quantitative sleep measurement and no health outcomes; participants are community health workers, an unusually health-literate group, so views may not represent their communities; single US state with an atypical religious and demographic context; no control or comparison group by design.

### 9. Night and weekend shifts predict which toddlers get a bedtime routine — unless the mother has a strong support network

**Journal:** *Journal of Family Psychology* | **Published:** 3 August 2026
**PMID:** 42545383 | **DOI:** 10.1037/fam0001526
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a clean moderation finding with an actionable implication, in a large, under-studied sample; observational.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Drawing on 1,364 three-year-olds and their mothers from the Future of Families and Child Wellbeing Study (50% non-Hispanic Black, 22% Hispanic), researchers tested maternal nonstandard employment (night, weekend or variable shifts), child externalizing behaviour and maternal social capital as predictors of bedtime routines and their component activities. Nonstandard shifts predicted a lower probability of having any bedtime routine and of bedtime communication activities. Higher social capital predicted more routines — and crucially, high social capital eliminated the gap between standard and nonstandard shift workers. Mothers of children with more externalizing behaviour were *more* likely to run routines.

**Why it matters:** Bedtime routines are among the most widely recommended child-sleep interventions, but the recommendation assumes a parent is home at bedtime; this identifies social support as the thing that closes the gap for those who aren't.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a mother clocking off at 11pm and what "just do bath, book, bed" means to her. The surprise for readers is the moderation: whether a shift worker manages a bedtime routine depends less on the shift than on whether there is another trusted adult in the picture. Also flag the counterintuitive externalizing result — the most difficult children were the *most* likely to have routines, suggesting routines are partly a response to behaviour, not just a cause of good behaviour. Call Olivia Martín-Piñón at Arizona State University.
- **Wider angle:** For a policy or labour-focused outlet: the 24-hour economy and children's sleep. Scheduling algorithms, on-call shifts and predictable-scheduling legislation in US cities, with child sleep as the downstream metric nobody measures.

**Caveats:** Observational; cannot establish causation, and the externalizing finding is plausibly reverse-causal; bedtime routines and social capital maternally reported; data from an older US birth cohort in specific urban areas; no direct measure of child sleep quality or duration.

### 10. Sleep quality doesn't predict firefighters' long-term resilience — a sense of meaning does

**Journal:** *European Journal of Psychotraumatology* | **Published:** 24 July 2026
**PMID:** 42495965 | **DOI:** 10.1080/20008066.2026.2696100
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a well-designed two-year prospective study delivering a clean null on sleep, which is genuinely useful; weakened by heavy attrition.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)

**The study:** 528 Dutch firefighters from 11 regions were assessed at baseline on sense of coherence, meaning in life, sleep quality (PSQI) and social support from colleagues, then followed at 12 months (N = 237) and 24 months (N = 203). Controlling for baseline resilience, sense of coherence predicted resilience at 12 months and meaning in life at 24 months. Sleep quality and colleague support predicted resilience at neither time point — nor did age, sex or number of stressful events experienced.

**Why it matters:** Sleep is routinely listed as a pillar of resilience in occupational wellbeing programmes; here, in a high-exposure profession, it carried no predictive weight once existing resilience was accounted for, while existential beliefs did.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Sell the null. Every corporate resilience programme starts with sleep, exercise and peer support; over two years in people who attend fatal fires for a living, the thing that predicted holding up was believing life is comprehensible and manageable. Open on a firefighter describing what they tell themselves after a bad call. Call Bert Lenaert at Maastricht University / Open Universiteit. Be careful: a null in a self-report study is evidence of no *predictive* association, not proof sleep is irrelevant.
- **Wider angle:** For a work or management outlet: the resilience industry's evidence problem. Employers spend heavily on sleep apps and mindfulness while the strongest longitudinal predictors here are meaning-based and arguably not something HR can install.

**Caveats:** Substantial attrition (528 → 203, 62% lost by 24 months) raises selection bias; all measures self-reported; controlling for baseline resilience is conservative and may mask real sleep effects; single occupation in one country; observational.

### 11. A night's sleep doesn't help you forget a made-up word

**Journal:** *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* | **Published:** 13 August 2026
**PMID:** 42593994 | **DOI:** 10.1037/xlm0001660
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a neat, well-controlled four-experiment set with a sleep-versus-wake comparison; the finding is subtle and needs careful writing to land.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Four experiments tested whether hearing a word or a pseudoword once, incidentally, changed later lexical decision performance. Intervals were either 10–20 minutes or 12 hours, with the 12-hour version run both across a night of sleep and across a waking day. Prior exposure to clearly spoken real words had no measurable effect. Prior exposure to pseudowords consistently made people slower and worse at rejecting them as non-words — and that effect survived the 12-hour delay, with sleep making no apparent difference.

**Why it matters:** It refines the popular idea that sleep consolidates newly heard word-forms: here, the lingering trace of a nonsense word was equally durable across sleep and wake, implying an episodic familiarity signal rather than a sleep-dependent lexical integration.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the everyday version — a brand name, a fantasy character, a nonsense word from an advert that starts to feel like a real word after one hearing. The hook is that real words leave no trace but fake ones do, and a night's sleep doesn't scrub them. Call Arthur G. Samuel at the Basque Center on Cognition, Brain and Language. Position as a gentle corrective to the sleep-and-language-learning literature rather than a refutation of it.
- **Wider angle:** For a marketing, media or linguistics-interested outlet: why invented brand names lodge in the mind. One incidental exposure is enough to make a non-word feel plausible, which is essentially the product-naming industry's business model.

**Caveats:** Sample sizes for the individual experiments not given in the abstract; laboratory task with limited real-world validity; the sleep/wake comparison is a between-groups design with the usual time-of-day confounds; a null effect for sleep needs replication and adequate power before being read as absence of a sleep effect.

### 12. A blood marker rises in people who respond to sleep deprivation therapy for depression

**Journal:** *International Clinical Psychopharmacology* | **Published:** 1 September 2026 (online 29 July 2026)
**PMID:** 41903946 | **DOI:** 10.1097/YIC.0000000000000622
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — the treatment itself (deliberate sleep deprivation as a fast antidepressant) is under-told for general readers, but the biomarker result is preliminary and the sample small.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Researchers measured circulating extracellular NAMPT — the enzyme controlling cellular NAD+ availability — before and after treatment in 46 patients: those with major depression given a month of monoaminergic antidepressants, and those with bipolar disorder given a week of chronotherapeutics (repeated total sleep deprivation plus light therapy). Larger increases in eNAMPT tracked greater clinical benefit, regardless of diagnosis or treatment type. The rise was not necessary for response, but the biggest responders showed the biggest increases.

**Why it matters:** Not much yet clinically, but it hints that two very different antidepressant routes — a drug taken over weeks and a night without sleep — converge on the same energy-metabolism marker, which is a line of enquiry worth following.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The real hook for New Scientist Mind is the treatment most readers have never heard of: keeping severely depressed patients awake all night, repeatedly, and watching mood lift within days. Use the biomarker as the news peg for a piece explaining chronotherapeutics and asking why an intervention with decades of evidence remains confined to a handful of European centres. Call Francesco Benedetti at IRCCS San Raffaele in Milan, who has run this programme for years.
- **Wider angle:** For a biomedical or metabolism-focused outlet: NAD+ has become a supplement-industry darling on the back of ageing research. A sceptical piece on the gap between NAD+ biology in psychiatry and the claims on the bottle.

**Caveats:** N = 46, split across two diagnoses and two treatments — very small for a biomarker study; open-label with no placebo or sham condition; high individual variability in eNAMPT acknowledged by the authors; association only, and the authors explicitly frame it as facilitating rather than causing response; needs replication.

### 13. A dawn-simulating lamp changes how people with intellectual disability wake up

**Journal:** *Journal of Intellectual Disabilities* | **Published:** 18 August 2026
**PMID:** 42613717 | **DOI:** 10.1177/17446295261478674
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — tiny and uncontrolled, but the intervention is cheap, the population is badly neglected, and the mechanism proposed is intuitively appealing.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** In a residential care setting, seven adults with moderate to severe intellectual disability went through a two-week care-as-usual baseline followed by two weeks of a wake-up light (gradual dawn simulation) before morning care, in a practice-based single-case AB design. Waking state was assessed with actigraphy and daily observation, affect with daily observation and the MIPQ. All seven were more often already awake when carers arrived. Consistent improvements in mood and interest appeared in two participants — the two who, at baseline, had typically slept right up until care began.

**Why it matters:** It raises a specific, testable hypothesis: that some of the distress attributed to morning care in this population is simply the experience of being woken abruptly by another person, and that a lamp could pre-empt it.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the moment a carer switches on the overhead light on someone who was fast asleep three seconds ago, and reframe "challenging behaviour at morning care" as a sleep-inertia problem. The pitchable idea is that the two people who benefited most were exactly the two who had been sleeping through until the carer arrived — a mechanistic pattern rather than a fluke. Call Nienke Siebelink at Academy Het Dorp, Arnhem. Must be written as exploratory and hypothesis-generating throughout.
- **Wider angle:** For a health-technology or social-care outlet: the case for cheap ambient interventions in long-term care. Dawn lamps, light schedules and circadian lighting have decent evidence in dementia units but almost none in intellectual disability services — a piece on where the evidence gap sits and who funds it.

**Caveats:** N = 7 completers; single site; single-case AB design with no control group, no randomisation and no blinding of observers; baseline and intervention phases were not counterbalanced, so seasonal or expectancy effects are uncontrolled; affect assessed by observation, vulnerable to carer expectancy; explicitly framed as exploratory.

### 14. Self-blame and catastrophising are the thinking habits that track both poor sleep and poor mental health

**Journal:** *Journal of Health Psychology* | **Published:** 12 August 2026
**PMID:** 42587372 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13591053261467672
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — decent sample and a neat pinpointing of two specific strategies, but cross-sectional and all self-report; the odd finding on adaptive strategies is the most interesting part.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 1,030 young adults completed the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, the Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, and the Beck depression and anxiety inventories. Structural equation modelling identified catastrophising and self-blame as the two strategies significantly associated with both worse sleep quality and worse mental health. Less adaptive strategies generally tracked poorer sleep and more symptoms — but adaptive strategies were not associated with sleep quality or depression at all, and were *positively* correlated with anxiety symptoms.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the sleep-relevant target is subtracting maladaptive thinking rather than adding positive reappraisal — and the anxiety finding hints that effortful "healthy" coping may be a signal of distress rather than a buffer against it.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the awkward result: the coping strategies people are taught to use — putting things in perspective, positive refocusing — showed no relationship with sleep quality and went *up* with anxiety. Open on the 2am spiral of self-blame that CBT-I already targets, and ask whether emotion-regulation training is aiming at the wrong half of the repertoire. Call Jitka Bušková at the National Institute of Mental Health, Klecany.
- **Wider angle:** For a psychology or wellbeing outlet: the growing evidence that adaptive coping strategies show weak or inconsistent associations with outcomes across the literature, and what that means for the therapy techniques built on them.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional; no causal direction can be established, and the likeliest reading of the adaptive-strategy/anxiety correlation is that anxious people deploy more coping effort; entirely self-reported, including sleep quality; young-adult sample from a single country; SEM is model-dependent and the fit statistics are not reported in the abstract.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Across this batch, the single number sleep science and the consumer wearable industry both lean on — hours slept — keeps failing, while less-used metrics do the work. "Three nights of poor sleep predict next-day mood — and how long you slept doesn't" (PMID 42617377) found a rolling three-night *quality* index beat total sleep time outright; "It's long sleep, not short sleep, that tracks with inflammation" (PMID 42546640) put the biological signal at the wrong end of the duration curve; "Autistic children keep more regular bedtimes than their peers" (PMID 42593049) split the composite Sleep Regularity Index into two halves that point in opposite directions; and "Sleep changes forecast later depression — but depression doesn't forecast sleep" (PMID 42541934), "Heart-and-metabolic illness makes depression harder to recover from" (PMID 42628578, where "unhealthy sleep" is a U-shaped <6/≥9 h bracket) and the clean null in "Sleep quality doesn't predict firefighters' long-term resilience" (PMID 42495965) all show how much the conclusion depends on which summary statistic is chosen.

**Why pitch this now:** Sleep scores, regularity indices and "sleep debt" tallies have moved from research instruments into tens of millions of wrists and phones in the past three years, precisely as the underlying epidemiology is fragmenting into contradictory findings depending on whether you measure duration, quality, regularity, or accumulation across nights — and no one is auditing what those consumer numbers actually predict.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the sleep measurement crisis: how the field settled on "hours" as its master variable, what the new candidates (multi-night cumulative quality, bedtime-versus-wake-time regularity, U-shaped duration risk) actually predict, and why reverse causation — long sleep as a symptom of illness rather than a cause — keeps being read as a lifestyle warning instead. Aimed at the science-literate general reader who owns a tracker and has been told to hit seven hours.

**Potential outlets:**
- **New Scientist Features** — fits the magazine's appetite for "the thing you've been told to optimise is the wrong thing," and can be built directly from these papers plus interviews with the Leipzig and Kunming groups.
- **Wired** — the consumer angle is strongest here: sleep scores as a commercial product whose validity nobody outside the companies has tested against next-day outcomes.
- **Undark** — well suited to the harder critique embedded in the batch, including the point in PMID 42546640 about composite blood-count indices and multiple testing as a publication engine.
- **STAT** — for the clinical and digital-biomarker end: if a three-night quality index is the best short-term marker of affective vulnerability after psychiatric discharge, who is validating it and under what regulatory pathway.
