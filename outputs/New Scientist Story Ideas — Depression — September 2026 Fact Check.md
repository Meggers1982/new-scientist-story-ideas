### Study 1: Sleep and body-clock measures can tell unipolar and bipolar depression apart
**PMID:** 42751051 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

All numbers check out: 159 patients (43 BDD, 116 UDD), AUC 0.926, the six retained variables, and the direction of each sleep difference all match the abstract, as do the PSG subsample figure (20 BDD) flagged in Caveats.

**Issue A. Headline states a capability the study has not demonstrated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Sleep and body-clock measures can tell unipolar and bipolar depression apart"
- **Abstract says:** "Integrating subjective, actigraphic, and polysomnographic markers provides a promising, noninvasive approach to differentiate UDD from BDD."
- **Problem:** The 0.926 AUC comes from a backward stepwise model derived and evaluated in the same 159 patients, with no validation cohort. "Can tell apart" reads as an established capability; the authors claim only promise. The body text and Caveats are appropriately hedged, so only the headline is out of step.
- **Suggested fix:** "Sleep and body-clock measures show promise for telling unipolar and bipolar depression apart"

**Issue B. NS fit score rests on an unvalidated, internally derived model** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — a genuinely hard clinical problem with an unusually clean discrimination score, though the sample is modest and internal-only."
- **Abstract says:** Model derived by backward stepwise logistic regression in the same sample; explains 49.4% of variance; no external validation reported.
- **Problem:** A 7 implies this is close to pitch-ready. Stepwise selection plus in-sample AUC in 43 cases versus 116 controls, with PSG available for fewer than half the bipolar group, is exactly the setup that produces optimistic discrimination figures. The digest names this, then scores as if it did not matter.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10 and note explicitly that the AUC is an in-sample estimate.

### Study 2: Sleeping pills taken in pregnancy may not raise a child's risk of ADHD or autism
**PMID:** 42744942 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N (75,477 pairs; 25,159 exposed), registry years (2009–2016), the restricted-cohort design, and both adjusted hazard ratios with CIs are reproduced correctly.

**Issue A. "Disappeared" overstates "attenuated"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Associations seen in crude analyses disappeared once maternal psychiatric severity and family factors were accounted for."
- **Abstract says:** "Associations observed in crude analyses were attenuated after adjusting for maternal psychiatric severity and familial factors."
- **Problem:** The adjusted ADHD estimate is 1.08 with an upper CI bound of 1.18 — compatible with an 18% relative increase. The signal shrank and lost significance; it did not vanish. "Disappeared" is the word a sceptical editor will check first.
- **Suggested fix:** "Associations seen in crude analyses were substantially attenuated once maternal psychiatric severity and family factors were accounted for, leaving estimates that no longer reached significance."

**Issue B. Affiliation attributed to the wrong institution** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call the Chung Shan Medical University team (Jing-Yang Huang, Hui-Ping Chang)"
- **Abstract says:** Huang JY — Chung Shan Medical University; Chang HP — "Department of Nursing, Asia University, Taichung. csha615@asia.edu.tw."
- **Problem:** The corresponding author, Chang, is at Asia University, not Chung Shan. Small, but it is the contact detail a writer would act on.
- **Suggested fix:** "Call the Taichung team — Jing-Yang Huang at Chung Shan Medical University and corresponding author Hui-Ping Chang at Asia University"

**Issue C. Follow-up window not supported by the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "follow-up ends by school age, so later-diagnosed cases may be missed"
- **Abstract says:** Registry data 2009–2016; no follow-up end date or mean follow-up duration is reported.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only. The abstract gives incidence in person-months but never states how long children were tracked.
- **Suggested fix:** "Follow-up duration is not stated in the abstract; later-diagnosed cases may be missed."

### Study 3: Adding exercise to school PE lessons does nothing for teenagers' mood
**PMID:** 42752288 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=131, 52.7% female, age 13.2 ± 0.90, two public schools in Brazil, 12 weeks, twice weekly, 20 minutes, heart-rate monitoring and the three null DASS-21 interaction effects are all accurate, and "all confidence intervals straddled zero" is correct for all three outcomes.

**Issue A. Headline converts an underpowered null into a definitive absence of effect** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Adding exercise to school PE lessons does nothing for teenagers' mood"
- **Abstract says:** "No significant group × time interaction effects were observed for depression (interaction effect: 0.71; 95% CI -0.98 to 2.40; p = 0.412)... The intervention was not effective in improving adolescents' mental health indicators."
- **Problem:** The depression CI runs from -0.98 to 2.40 — the trial cannot exclude a clinically meaningful benefit or harm in either direction. With 131 students in two schools, unselected for symptoms, "does nothing" is a claim the data do not support; "did not improve" is. The Caveats correctly flag floor effects, so the headline contradicts the digest's own limitation.
- **Suggested fix:** "Adding exercise to school PE lessons failed to improve teenagers' mood in a monitored trial"

**Issue B. "Failed replication" novelty label is not what the abstract describes** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Failed replication"
- **Abstract says:** Title: "School-based physical exercise intervention for adolescents' mental health: An adaptation of the 'ActTeens Program' cluster RCT."
- **Problem:** This is a locally adapted intervention tested in a new population, not an attempt to reproduce a specific prior positive result. Labelling it a failed replication implies a direct head-to-head with an earlier finding that the abstract does not describe.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** Null result that challenges a popular assumption"

### Study 4: Blood fat levels predict who responds to magnetic brain stimulation for depression
**PMID:** 42720531 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=80 (40/40), 20 sessions over four weeks, HAMD-17 primary outcome, greater reduction with active than sham, higher baseline triglycerides associated with greater improvement, and a stronger TG–response relationship in the active arm are all faithful to the abstract. The Caveat that the abstract reports no effect sizes is correct and well made.

**Issue A. "Single-center" not supported by the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Caveats: Small N (80); single-center"
- **Abstract says:** Authors are affiliated with The First Hospital of Hebei Medical University (five authors) and Xuzhou Medical University Affiliated Peace Hospital (one author, Internal Medicine).
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only. The abstract never states the number of recruiting sites.
- **Suggested fix:** "Recruitment site(s) not stated in the abstract; authorship is concentrated at a single hospital."

**Issue B. "New mechanism" label overstates what was tested** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** New mechanism"
- **Abstract says:** "could serve as a potential exploratory biomarker for individualized dTMS treatment strategies."
- **Problem:** No mechanism was investigated — this is a candidate predictive biomarker and an interaction term. The digest's own pitch angle asks a TMS researcher to speculate about plausible mechanisms, which confirms none is in the paper.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** Candidate predictive biomarker (exploratory)"

### Study 5: Depression rewires how the brain handles trust from close friends
**PMID:** 42702279 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=123, preregistration, the two tasks, the three partner types, the counterintuitive investment behavior, the striatal SRS → TPJ association and its reversal under high depression plus high closeness are all accurately reported, and the Caveats correctly flag the bioRxiv update and the non-clinical sample.

**Issue A. Causal headline for a correlational, symptom-based study** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Depression rewires how the brain handles trust from close friends"
- **Abstract says:** "depression alters how striatal SRS relates to regional activity, potentially disrupting how individuals interpret and respond to close others" — in a cross-sectional design where "Depressive symptoms and perceived closeness were assessed via self-report."
- **Problem:** "Rewires" asserts a causal, structural change produced by depression. What was observed is that self-reported symptom severity moderated a correlation between two brain measures in a community sample — no diagnosed patients, no longitudinal data, no intervention. The digest's pitch angle correctly tells the writer to be explicit about this, which the headline then undoes.
- **Suggested fix:** "Depressive symptoms go with a different brain response to trust from close friends"

### Study 6: Distrusting doctors a little makes Black adults more likely to seek mental health help — distrusting them a lot does not
**PMID:** 42749881 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=1042, the three instruments, the ordinal logistic models, ORs of 3.73 and 5.02 for quartiles 2 and 3, and the 25.7% figure are all accurate. The body sentence "the highest-mistrust quartile was less willing than the middle groups" is a correct reading of the abstract. Industry compensation is disclosed in Caveats as required.

**Issue A. Headline misstates the highest-mistrust result and adds causal language** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Distrusting doctors a little makes Black adults more likely to seek mental health help — distrusting them a lot does not"
- **Abstract says:** "Black adults at the highest level of mistrust were less likely to seek help from a mental health professional than quartiles 2 and 3 (Quartile 4 OR = 2.38; 95%CI = 1.75, 3.25; p < 0.001)"
- **Problem:** Two errors. First, the Q4 odds ratio of 2.38 is relative to the *lowest*-mistrust quartile — so the highest-mistrust group was still markedly *more* willing to seek help than the least mistrustful group, just less so than the middle quartiles. "Distrusting them a lot does not" reverses this. Second, "makes" is causal language for a cross-sectional survey of hypothetical willingness.
- **Suggested fix:** "Moderate mistrust of doctors goes with more willingness to seek mental health help among Black adults — and the very highest mistrust with less than that"

**Issue B. "Dampens help-seeking" reads a causal, behavioural effect into a model-fit statistic** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "depression itself — the very thing treatment would address — independently dampens help-seeking"
- **Abstract says:** "The inclusion of depressive symptoms in the model resulted in a 25.7% average decrease in willingness to seek help from a mental health professional. Depressive symptoms may have an impact on the effect of medical mistrust..."
- **Problem:** The 25.7% is a change in modelled willingness on adding a covariate, in cross-sectional data, with a hypothetical-willingness outcome. "Independently dampens help-seeking" implies a demonstrated causal effect on real behaviour. The pitch angle rightly says "keep the framing to willingness, not actual treatment uptake" — the Why it matters line does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "depressive symptoms are associated with lower reported willingness to seek professional help, independent of mistrust"

### Study 7: A cold, deferential social style marks out the most stubborn depression
**PMID:** 42731108 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=1800 at the NESDA nine-year follow-up, the Structural Summary Method and circumplex quadrants, the current-diagnosis group's higher distress and more submissive style, and the cold-submissive association with comorbidity and chronicity after demographic adjustment are all correct. The Caveat that interpersonal style was measured only at follow-up matches the design.

**Issue A. Headline drops the anxiety half of a transdiagnostic finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A cold, deferential social style marks out the most stubborn depression"
- **Abstract says:** "a cold-submissive style was associated with greater comorbidity and chronicity" of "anxiety and/or depression"; the paper is explicitly framed as "longitudinal, transdiagnostic data."
- **Problem:** The chronicity and comorbidity variables span anxiety and depressive disorders together — indeed, comorbidity is part of the outcome. Attaching the finding to depression alone narrows it inaccurately, and the body text ("overlapping anxiety and depression") is more faithful than the headline.
- **Suggested fix:** "A cold, deferential social style marks out the most stubborn anxiety and depression"

**Issue B. "New mechanism" label not supported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** New mechanism"
- **Abstract says:** "Interpersonal functioning has a complex, bidirectional relationship with mental disorders"; results are cross-sectional associations of IIP-derived style with diagnostic status.
- **Problem:** Nothing mechanistic was tested, and the authors flag bidirectionality. The digest's own Why it matters concedes "whether the style drives chronicity or is shaped by it remains open."
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** New association / clinical marker"

### Study 8: It is loneliness, not how often you see people, that predicts depression
**PMID:** 42623964 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=592, MIDUS Milwaukee, 2005–06 to 2016–17, six connectedness dimensions, loneliness β=1.76 and aOR=2.14, the null for social isolation, and the family-support indirect (aOR 0.538) and direct (aOR 0.301) effects on depression are all reported correctly.

**Issue A. Headline generalizes beyond the study population** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It is loneliness, not how often you see people, that predicts depression"
- **Abstract says:** "We analyzed longitudinal data... among Black Americans (N = 592) in the Midlife in the United States (MIDUS), Milwaukee, WI."
- **Problem:** The headline reads as a general statement about loneliness and depression. The finding comes from a single-city sample of 592 midlife Black Americans, a point the Why it matters line makes ("For midlife Black Americans at least") but the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Among midlife Black Americans, loneliness — not how often you see people — predicts depression"

### Study 9: Health records suggest one in six new mothers has depression-related care
**PMID:** 42745087 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The cohort (164,767 mothers, Milan, 2017–2023), 16.0%, 23.6% with EPDS ≥8 among screened mothers, OR 8.1 for prior psychological distress, and the "modest protective" reading of lower education and employment are all accurate, as is the 10–15% conventional figure.

**Issue A. Risk factors listed as if in descending order of magnitude** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "followed by age over 40, migrant background, being unmarried and first-time motherhood"
- **Abstract says:** "age > 40 years (OR 1.3; 95% CI 1.2-1.4), migrant background (OR 1.1; 95% CI 1.1-1.2) and primiparity (OR 1.4; 95% CI 1.4-1.5)"
- **Problem:** "Followed by" implies rank order, but primiparity (1.4) is the largest of the three after prior distress and is listed last, while migrant background (1.1) is the weakest and is listed second. No OR is given for unmarried status in the abstract at all.
- **Suggested fix:** "followed at much lower magnitude by first-time motherhood (OR 1.4), age over 40 (1.3) and migrant background (1.1); unmarried status was also named as a risk factor, without a reported odds ratio."

**Issue B. Algorithm validation and EPDS "cross-check" go beyond the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "cross-checked against Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale screening" / "cases defined by an unvalidated utilization algorithm"
- **Abstract says:** "Events attributable to perinatal depression were identified through an algorithm applied to Health Care Utilization (HCU) data... Among screened mothers, 23.6% met criteria for high psychological vulnerability (EPDS ≥ 8)."
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only. The abstract reports the two figures side by side; it does not describe validating the algorithm against EPDS, nor does it state that the algorithm is unvalidated. The Caveat asserts a limitation that may or may not be true.
- **Suggested fix:** Body: "and separately reported Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale results for the screened subgroup." Caveat: "The abstract does not report validation of the utilization algorithm, so the 16% mixes true incidence with care-seeking."

### Study 11: Women in ongoing depression treatment conceive less often during fertility care
**PMID:** 42735740 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The source population (601,664), analytic cohort (8734 controls vs 4367 with depression), all three subdistribution hazard ratios (1.10, 0.53, 0.31), the prescription-coverage finding and the authors' explicit warning against an adherence reading are reproduced accurately. The framing is appropriately restrained and the "do not imply any prescribing action" instruction is the right call.

**Issue A. A second reported outcome is omitted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**The study:** ...Among treated women, greater prescription coverage went with a higher hazard of pregnancy — a finding the authors explicitly say should not be read as adherence improving fertility."
- **Abstract says:** "Antidepressant-treated pregnancy cohorts had higher odds of spontaneous abortion than non-depressed controls."
- **Problem:** The digest's Why it matters line argues that "recovered depression looks unremarkable for conception." The miscarriage result is a second reproductive outcome in the same abstract that a reader — or a reporting clinician — would consider material, and leaving it out makes the picture look tidier than it is.
- **Suggested fix:** Add to The study: "Antidepressant-treated cohorts who did become pregnant also had higher odds of spontaneous abortion than non-depressed controls — subject to the same confounding caveats."

### Study 12: People whose brains respond least to reward gain most from a single mood-training session
**PMID:** 42700813 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=92 (47/45), mean age 19.44, the MID task with EEG-recorded RewP, the null group difference in RewP, the crossover moderation pattern for perceived stress and the n=66 follow-up are all accurate, and the Caveats correctly identify this as a moderation finding on a null primary effect.

**Issue A. Headline names the wrong outcome** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "People whose brains respond least to reward gain most from a single mood-training session"
- **Abstract says:** "RewP moderated the training effects on one-month follow-up perceived stress, but not symptoms of depression or social anxiety."
- **Problem:** In a depression-focused digest for a psychology section, "gain most" invites the reader to assume mood or symptom benefit. The only moderated outcome was perceived stress; depression and social anxiety symptoms showed nothing. The body text says so, but the headline is what carries into a pitch.
- **Suggested fix:** "People whose brains respond least to reward show the biggest drop in stress after a single training session"

**Issue B. "No effects on" conflates a null main effect with a null moderation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "There were no effects on depression or social anxiety symptoms."
- **Abstract says:** "RewP moderated the training effects on one-month follow-up perceived stress, but not symptoms of depression or social anxiety."
- **Problem:** What the abstract reports is that RewP did *not moderate* effects on depression or social anxiety; it does not separately report main effects of the training on those symptoms. The two are different claims.
- **Suggested fix:** "RewP did not moderate outcomes for depression or social anxiety symptoms."

### Study 13: How harshly stroke patients treat themselves in week one tracks their depression
**PMID:** 42731412 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=407, assessment within the first week post-stroke, SCS-D-KF and PHQ-9, the two-factor structure, ΔR²=0.057 for UCS versus 0.013 for CS, the hierarchical latent SEM with sociodemographic and clinical covariates, the one-third post-stroke depression figure, and the ω=0.63 reliability caveat are all correct.

**Issue A. Direction of the uncompassionate-self-responding association not directly verifiable** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "uncompassionate self-responding — self-judgment, isolation, over-identification with distress — added the most explanatory power for depressive symptoms (ΔR²=0.057)"
- **Abstract says:** "UCS demonstrated the strongest incremental validity (ΔR2 = 0.057, β = -0.316, p < .001)"
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only. All three betas are negative (total −0.290, UCS −0.316, CS −0.201), which implies UCS was scored in the self-compassionate direction. The digest's headline and pitch angle assert the substantive direction ("self-attack" → more depression), which is the likely reading but is not stated in the abstract. Worth confirming against the full text before the direction anchors a headline.
- **Suggested fix:** Confirm scoring direction with the authors; until then, "uncompassionate self-responding explained the most additional variance in depressive symptoms (ΔR²=0.057)".

**Issue B. "Single-center" not stated in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Caveats: Cross-sectional...; single-center"
- **Abstract says:** "This cross-sectional study examined 407 patients (60.4% male) with acute stroke." Author affiliations span Düsseldorf, Bielefeld and University Hospital Heidelberg (Department of Neurology).
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only; the presence of a Heidelberg neurology co-author leaves open that recruitment spanned more than one stroke unit.
- **Suggested fix:** "Number of recruiting centres not stated in the abstract."

### Study 14: Boxing and expressive writing improve mental health in survivors of childhood sexual abuse
**PMID:** 42752360 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=17, the eight weekly two-hour sessions, the peer-led survivor-designed design, and the full list of pre-post changes (PTSD, CPTSD, depression, anxiety, stress, post-traumatic cognitions, memory fragmentation, emotional wellbeing, vigorous activity) are all accurate. The Caveats are appropriately blunt and the pitch angle tells the writer to say so out loud.

**Issue A. Headline asserts effectiveness from an uncontrolled proof-of-concept study** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Boxing and expressive writing improve mental health in survivors of childhood sexual abuse"
- **Abstract says:** "This study sought to demonstrate proof of concept... Though evaluated in a small sample, these findings build on early pilot research (2020)... This preliminary evidence illustrates the promise of LWH."
- **Problem:** Seventeen self-selected participants, no control group, no follow-up, self-report throughout. "Improve" states a causal effect the design cannot support and generalizes to survivors as a class. The digest's own pitch line — "be straight that 17 people with no control group cannot show the boxing did it" — directly contradicts its headline.
- **Suggested fix:** "Boxing and expressive writing: survivors report better mental health after a survivor-led pilot"

### Study 15: Half of trafficking survivors have depression, global review finds
**PMID:** 42752993 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Eighteen studies, the 2000–2025 search window, PROSPERO preregistration, all four pooled prevalences (44.0%, 50.5%, 52.3%, 19.1%), I² 96.9–98.9%, the 7.0–89.1% PTSD prediction interval, region as a moderator for depression only, and the two-studies-with-service-use finding are all accurate. The Caveats correctly warn against reading the pooled figures as population prevalence, echoing the authors.

**Issue A. Participant total not supported by the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "18 studies, tens of thousands of survivors, and two papers that recorded whether anyone got help"
- **Abstract says:** "Eighteen studies were included." No pooled sample size is reported.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only, and plausibly an overestimate — trafficking survivor samples are typically in the dozens to low hundreds, so 18 studies may total in the low thousands. The number is load-bearing in the pitch angle's rhetorical contrast.
- **Suggested fix:** "18 studies and two papers that recorded whether anyone got help" — or obtain the pooled N from the full text before using a figure.

### Study 16: Refugees keep their therapy gains even when asylum decisions change
**PMID:** 42703887 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=90 with T2=59, two German psychosocial centres, self-report PTSD and depression measures, linear mixed models showing significant improvement, and the null moderation by asylum status change are all accurate. The Why it matters line ("treatment should not be postponed until status is settled") tracks the authors' own plain language summary, so it is not an overreach.

**Issue A. Headline is more confident than the authors about a null moderation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Refugees keep their therapy gains even when asylum decisions change"
- **Abstract says:** "The results further cautiously hint towards the robustness of therapeutic gains across challenging legal circumstances."
- **Problem:** A non-significant moderation term in 90 patients is weak evidence of no moderation, and the authors hedge twice ("cautiously hint"). The headline states the null as an established fact; the Caveats flag small N but not the underpowered-null problem specifically.
- **Suggested fix:** "Refugees' therapy gains held up even when asylum decisions changed, small study hints" — and add to Caveats: "a non-significant moderation test in 90 patients is weak evidence that asylum status change has no effect."

### Study 17: Depression's link to advanced heart, kidney and metabolic disease is stronger in women
**PMID:** 42747918 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

NHANES, N=30,244, 16% advanced CKM, 8% with depressive symptoms, the sex-stratified ORs (women 2.24, men 1.75), the significant sex interaction and the weaker association at ≥65 versus 30–49 (OR 0.42) are all correctly reported, as is the cross-sectional caveat.

**Issue A. Two separate interactions combined into a claim about one subgroup** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It suggests the mind–body link in cardiometabolic disease is concentrated in younger and middle-aged women — a group whose cardiovascular risk is routinely underestimated."
- **Abstract says:** "Interaction estimates indicated a stronger association among women (OR, 1.58; 95% CI, 1.12-2.23; P=0.010) and a weaker association at age ≥65 versus 30-49 years (OR, 0.42; 95% CI, 0.27-0.67; P<.001)."
- **Problem:** These are two independent effect-modification tests. The abstract reports no sex-by-age joint analysis, so there is no evidence that the association is specifically *concentrated* in younger women — that would require a three-way interaction or an age-stratified, sex-stratified estimate that the abstract does not contain. The pitch angle then builds its opening scene ("a 40-year-old woman") on this unsupported combination.
- **Suggested fix:** "It suggests the mind–body link in cardiometabolic disease is stronger in women, and — separately — stronger in younger and middle-aged adults than in the over-65s."

### Study 18: Chronic pelvic pain leaves a distinct mark on body image
**PMID:** 42600312 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

22 studies, N=7609, g=0.50 (95% CI 0.30–0.69), the comparison with healthy controls, the psychosocial correlates (anxiety, depression, loneliness, sexual dysfunction, distress) and the inclusion of vulvodynia alongside endometriosis are all accurate, and the Caveats correctly note the cross-sectional and theory-poor input literature.

**Issue A. "Mediator" is stronger than what the authors claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The authors identify 'body functionality' — what the body can do rather than how it looks — as a possible mediator between pain symptoms and body image, and a candidate intervention target."
- **Abstract says:** "Body functionality may be a key outcome to consider in the relationship between condition-specific symptoms (e.g., pain) and body image concerns, demonstrating potential as a target of intervention."
- **Problem:** "Mediator" is a specific statistical claim implying a tested causal pathway. The authors say only that it may be a key outcome to consider in that relationship, and describe it as an under-researched construct needing better measurement.
- **Suggested fix:** "The authors flag 'body functionality' — what the body can do rather than how it looks — as a construct worth measuring in the relationship between pain and body image, and a candidate intervention target."

**Issue B. Headline implies a causal direction the pooled studies cannot establish** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Chronic pelvic pain leaves a distinct mark on body image"
- **Abstract says:** "Compared to healthy control groups, the meta-analysis revealed those with CPP had significantly worse body image concerns... The quality of many included studies was limited by their cross-sectional design."
- **Problem:** "Leaves a mark" describes an effect produced by the condition over time; the pooled evidence is a cross-sectional between-group difference of moderate size.
- **Suggested fix:** "Chronic pelvic pain comes with distinctly worse body image"

### Study 19: Patients lost weight over a year on a new antipsychotic
**PMID:** 42696983 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

602 treated, 229 (38.0%) completing 368 days, weight decreased as the most common TEAE at 10.1%, the significant improvements in total and LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, prolactin, weight, BMI and waist circumference, no notable EPS changes, and CDSS mean change −0.6 with ES −0.21 are all accurate. Industry funding, authorship and Correll's disclosures are handled properly in both the pitch and the Caveats, and the 62% attrition is flagged repeatedly.

**Issue A. "New antipsychotic" is not supported and is misleading about the drug's status** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Patients lost weight over a year on a new antipsychotic"
- **Abstract says:** "lumateperone 42 mg (a simultaneous modulator of serotonin, dopamine, and glutamate)" — described only as an approved-dose agent in a Phase 3 long-term safety study; the abstract makes no novelty claim.
- **Problem:** Lumateperone is an established marketed compound, and a one-year open-label extension is a late-stage safety study, not a first look at a new drug. Calling it "new" adds an unearned novelty hook to an industry-run, uncontrolled study.
- **Suggested fix:** "Patients lost weight over a year on lumateperone, in an uncontrolled industry study"
