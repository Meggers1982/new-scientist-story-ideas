### 1. Treating sleep alongside PTSD leaves service members sleeping better six months later

**Journal:** *Behavior Therapy* | **Published:** September 2026 (epub April 15, 2026)
**PMID:** 42660579 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.beth.2026.04.003
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a randomized trial with objective actigraphy and long follow-up, showing sleep got *worse* over time after standard exposure therapy.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Eighty-two active-duty US service members with PTSD were randomized to Compressed Prolonged Exposure (CPE) or Trauma Management Therapy (TMT), which bolts sleep training and skills work onto exposure therapy. Sleep was measured with wrist actigraphy, not just questionnaires, at baseline, post-treatment and 3 and 6 months later. Neither group improved much immediately, but the TMT group's sleep efficiency, onset latency, quality and time awake after falling asleep improved by 6 months (g = 0.51–0.70), while sleep in the exposure-only group drifted downward.

**Why it matters:** Trauma-focused therapy is the front-line treatment for PTSD, and the assumption has been that fixing the trauma fixes the sleep. This trial suggests the opposite can happen — and that the benefit of adding sleep work only becomes visible months after treatment ends, which is exactly the window most trials never look at.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The therapy that treats your trauma may leave your insomnia behind — and the difference doesn't show up until half a year later. Open on a service member who "finished" PTSD treatment and still can't sleep. Worth calling Deborah Beidel at UCF RESTORES and first author Anthony Cifre (now at Yale Child Study Center) on why the effects were delayed rather than immediate.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a health or military-affairs outlet on the growing case that sleep is not a downstream symptom of psychiatric illness but a parallel target — spanning PTSD, depression and psychosis, and asking why so few therapy protocols include it.

**Caveats:** Small N (82), single military population that may not generalize to civilians, sleep hygiene training was bundled with other skills so the active ingredient is unclear, treatment groups differed in more than sleep content.

### 2. Trauma therapy for children works even while the bombs are still falling

**Journal:** *European Journal of Psychotraumatology* | **Published:** September 2, 2026 (issue December 2026)
**PMID:** 42635370 | **DOI:** 10.1080/20008066.2026.2713259
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — genuinely novel setting and large effects, but no control group and open-label delivery.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** European EMDR trainers trained 32 Ukrainian therapists, who then delivered Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing to 151 children and adolescents aged 1–17 in Ukraine between June 2023 and January 2025 — during active war. PTSD symptoms, rated by both children and caregivers on the CATS-2, fell substantially from before to after treatment, with pre-post effect sizes above d = 1.5 across DSM-5 PTSD, ICD-11 PTSD and complex PTSD.

**Why it matters:** Trauma-focused therapy has long been assumed to require safety first — the idea being that you cannot "process" a trauma that is still happening. If reprocessing therapy works under ongoing threat, humanitarian mental health provision could be rethought rather than deferred until after a war ends.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Standard trauma theory says wait until the danger passes. Ukrainian children treated during air-raid alerts show large symptom reductions anyway. Open on the training pipeline — Dutch, German and Italian trainers teaching Kyiv therapists over video. Call Elisa Pfeiffer at Ulm University and Olga Uralova of the EMDR Association Ukraine. Be explicit that this is an uncontrolled effectiveness study, not proof of efficacy.
- **Wider angle:** For a global health or foreign-affairs outlet: the export model of psychological treatment — how brief-training cascades are being used to build trauma services in Ukraine, Gaza and Sudan, and what the evidence base for that model actually looks like.

**Caveats:** No control group, symptom improvement over time could partly reflect regression to the mean or natural recovery, self- and caregiver-reported outcomes, therapists were not blind, selection into treatment unknown.

### 3. Stress doesn't stop you exercising — if anything you move slightly more

**Journal:** *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* | **Published:** November 2026 (epub August 18, 2026)
**PMID:** 42612762 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychsport.2026.103243
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — directly contradicts a folk belief with intensive real-world data, but small sample in a specific group.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Seventy-six adults maintaining weight loss answered hourly smartphone prompts about stress for seven days while wearing a chest sensor that recorded heart rate variability, step counts and time in light and moderate-to-vigorous activity. Researchers modeled within-person links between stress and subsequent movement at 10, 30, 60 and 120-minute lags. Most associations were null; where significant, higher momentary stress predicted a small *increase* in subsequent activity, mainly steps and mainly within the shortest time windows.

**Why it matters:** "I was too stressed to work out" is one of the most cited barriers in health psychology, and interventions are built on it. In minute-by-minute real life, at least in this group, stress and inactivity did not line up — which suggests the barrier is a story people tell after the fact rather than a moment-to-moment process.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Everyone says stress keeps them off the treadmill; hourly data from chest sensors says otherwise. Lead on the gap between how we explain our behavior and what our bodies actually do. Call Melanie Bamert and Jennifer Inauen at the University of Bern, and ask specifically about the vagally-mediated heart rate variability findings, which picked up links that self-reported stress missed.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-and-mind feature for a general-interest science outlet on how ecological momentary assessment is quietly dismantling retrospective self-report — several "well-known" psychological barriers have failed to survive contact with hourly sampling.

**Caveats:** Small N (76), participants were all in weight-loss maintenance and unusually activity-focused, seven-day window, observational within-person design cannot establish causation, many comparisons across lags and indicators.

### 4. Sailors who nap most often report less job stress — and more burnout

**Journal:** *Journal of Sleep Research* | **Published:** September 3, 2026
**PMID:** 42692480 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jsr.70420
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a neat double-edged finding in a large occupational sample, weakened by cross-sectional self-report.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers surveyed a large sample of active-duty US Navy sailors on napping frequency, occupational stress, burnout, functioning and health. More frequent napping was associated with lower odds of high job stress and impaired functioning — but also with higher odds of burnout, and sailors napping five or more days a week had elevated odds of reporting many poor mental health days. Nap frequency was unrelated to poor physical health days.

**Why it matters:** Naps are marketed as a fatigue fix for shift workers, doctors and pilots. This suggests frequent napping may work acutely while simultaneously flagging someone whose chronic sleep debt and workload are unmanageable — a useful distinction for anyone designing a workplace fatigue program.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The nap as both remedy and warning light. Open on a sailor grabbing 20 minutes between watches. The hook is that habitual napping may be a symptom worth screening on rather than a solution to celebrate. Call Dale Russell at the Uniformed Services University and Anne Germain at Pittsburgh; press them on direction of causation, which the design cannot settle.
- **Wider angle:** For a business or work-focused outlet: the corporate nap pod reconsidered — what occupational sleep researchers now think employers should measure instead, and why "we let people nap" can coexist with a burnout problem.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational (cannot establish causation), self-reported napping and outcomes, military population may not generalize, abstract does not report sample size or effect magnitudes.

### 5. Stress makes people grab the smaller, sooner reward — even when money is not tight

**Journal:** *Journal of Behavioral Medicine* | **Published:** September 3, 2026
**PMID:** 42693377 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10865-026-00711-0
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a clean attempt to separate stress from poverty in impulsive choice, with a striking magnitude effect.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** To disentangle stress from financial scarcity, researchers recruited 247 US adults on Prolific with household incomes of at least $100,000 a year. Participants completed delay discounting and probability discounting tasks at two reward magnitudes ($100 and $10,000) plus the Perceived Stress Scale. Higher perceived stress predicted steeper discounting of both delayed and uncertain $100 rewards — but showed no relationship at $10,000.

**Why it matters:** The impatience seen in stressed people has often been attributed to poverty rather than stress itself. Here the link survives in an affluent sample, but only for small stakes, suggesting stress shapes everyday micro-decisions more than life-defining ones.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Stress does not make you reckless with your pension — it makes you reckless with a hundred quid. Lead on the magnitude split, which nobody predicted. Open on the mundane stressed purchase: the taxi, the takeaway, the impulse buy. Call Jeffrey Stein at the Fralin Biomedical Research Institute.
- **Wider angle:** For an economics or behavioral-science outlet: the scarcity-mindset literature under scrutiny, and what happens when researchers deliberately design samples to strip out income effects that have confounded a decade of findings.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational, self-reported stress, hypothetical rather than real rewards, online crowdsourced sample deliberately unrepresentative (high income), no manipulation of stress.

### 6. Resilience doesn't protect young trauma survivors who also have OCD

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** September 4, 2026
**PMID:** 42697249 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122468
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a large longitudinal cohort producing a clean negative result about a concept the wellness world treats as universal armor.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using the Texas Childhood Trauma Research Network, researchers followed 1,179 trauma-exposed young people aged 8–20 with complete post-traumatic stress symptom data at baseline and 1, 6 and 12 months. Youth with both OCD and interpersonal trauma had symptoms about 2.4 times as severe as those with neither, and stayed highest at every time point. Resilience was linked to lower symptoms overall and slightly faster improvement — but did not specifically buffer the elevated symptoms associated with OCD.

**Why it matters:** Resilience is routinely framed as a general-purpose shield. This suggests it works as a broad main effect but not as a targeted protection for the highest-risk subgroup — a distinction with real consequences for who gets prioritized in child trauma services.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Resilience helps everyone a bit, and the most vulnerable kids not much at all. Lead on the OCD-plus-interpersonal-trauma profile, which nobody screens for as a combination. Open on a clinician sorting a caseload. Call Kevin Wagner at Baylor College of Medicine, and ask whether "resilience" measures capture anything OCD does not already erode.
- **Wider angle:** For an education or parenting outlet: the resilience-training industry in schools, and what the developmental evidence actually supports — main effects, yes; protection against specific psychiatric risk, much less clear.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort (cannot establish causation), self-reported symptoms and resilience, follow-up limited to 12 months, single US state network, OCD status derived within an existing cohort rather than by gold-standard diagnostic interview across the whole sample.

### 7. Most post-concussion symptoms track distress, not the blow to the head

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychosomatic Research* | **Published:** September 2026 (epub May 19, 2026)
**PMID:** 42173054 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpsychores.2026.112870
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a rare study with an injured non-head control group, though cross-sectional and acute-phase only.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers assessed 169 consecutive trauma admissions to a Level 1 emergency department in Sydney — 100 with mild traumatic brain injury, 69 with orthopedic injuries and no head trauma — a mean of 4.6 days after injury. Psychological distress variables accounted for a large share of variance in post-concussion symptom reporting. Anxiety sensitivity, particularly fear of physical sensations, added a small but independent increment (ΔR² = 0.012); the final model explained 77.7 percent of the variance.

**Why it matters:** Concussion symptom checklists are treated as measures of brain injury. If distress and the way people interpret their own bodily sensations dominate the variance within days of injury, the checklist may be measuring something closer to alarm than to damage — with implications for how return-to-play and return-to-work decisions are made.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The concussion symptom questionnaire may be an anxiety questionnaire in disguise. The hook is the orthopedic control group: people who never hit their heads report a lot of the same things. Open in the emergency department at day four. Call Travis Wearne at Macquarie University and Susanne Meares. Do not imply anyone should skip concussion assessment — this is about interpretation, not triage.
- **Wider angle:** For a sports or health outlet: the concussion-symptom industry, from sideline apps to school protocols, and the uncomfortable finding that symptom scales cannot distinguish head-injured from otherwise-injured patients in the acute phase.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional (cannot establish causation), single center, self-reported symptoms, acute phase only with no follow-up to persistent post-concussion syndrome, anxiety sensitivity increment was very small.

### 8. People with two or more diagnoses lost far more well-being in the pandemic than their conditions predict

**Journal:** *BMJ Mental Health* | **Published:** September 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42686352 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bmjment-2026-302777
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — enormous global sample and a genuinely non-obvious supra-additive result, undercut by retrospective self-report.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** The COH-FIT consortium analyzed WHO-5 well-being scores from 121,066 adults in 155 countries, rated retrospectively for the pre-pandemic period and again during the pandemic, treating covid-19 as a global natural experiment in stress vulnerability. Psychiatric diagnoses were associated with two- to three-fold worse well-being than somatic ones. Crucially, combinations of conditions produced non-linear, supra-additive declines — anorexia nervosa plus schizophrenia, or inflammatory bowel disease plus psychosis, were associated with drops far larger than the sum of their parts.

**Why it matters:** Health systems still treat multimorbidity as additive: one condition plus another. If stress vulnerability compounds multiplicatively, the people most exposed in the next crisis are not identifiable by counting diagnoses.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** When the world got stressful, having two conditions was much worse than twice as bad. Lead on the specific pairings — a gut disease plus psychosis, an eating disorder plus schizophrenia — which look arbitrary until you see the pattern. Call Christoph Correll at Charité or Marco Solmi in Ottawa. Frame as crisis preparedness, not clinical advice.
- **Wider angle:** For a health policy publication: the case for multimorbidity-based crisis planning, and why pandemic shielding lists built around single somatic diagnoses may have missed the most psychologically exposed group entirely.

**Caveats:** Retrospective pre-pandemic ratings are vulnerable to recall bias, self-reported diagnoses and outcomes, convenience sampling skews female (64%) and white (69%), observational design, extensive competing-interest declarations among senior authors (pharmaceutical consultancies, listed in full in the paper).

### 9. Babies of three very different languages all lock onto the same speech rhythm

**Journal:** *Developmental Science* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42621832 | **DOI:** 10.1111/desc.70271
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a well-powered three-country infant EEG study answering an old question about rhythm classes; the topic is niche but the result is clean.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers recorded cortical tracking of speech in 119 infants aged 4 and 8 months learning English (stress-timed), French (syllable-timed) or Japanese (mora-timed), using synthesized syllable strings in their native language, a non-native voice and a vocoded non-speech control. All three language groups at both ages tracked strongly at 5 Hz (the syllable rate) and at 10 Hz (the mora rate), inconsistently at 2.5 Hz (the foot rate), with the syllable dominant — and processed speech differently from non-speech by 4 months.

**Why it matters:** The idea that languages fall into rhythm classes that shape infant perception from the start has anchored decades of developmental work. This suggests the syllable is a universal default in the first eight months, and language-specific rhythmic tuning arrives later than assumed.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Whatever language they are learning, four-month-old brains beat to the same syllable. Lead on the failure of the expected native-language differences to appear. Open on an infant in an EEG cap in Tokyo, Paris and Sydney. Call Thierry Nazzi at CNRS in Paris or Varghese Peter at Western Sydney.
- **Wider angle:** For a language or linguistics-interested outlet: a feature on whether "rhythm classes" survive as a concept at all, and what neural entrainment research is doing to a taxonomy invented long before anyone could record a baby's cortex.

**Caveats:** Passive listening to synthesized stimuli rather than natural speech, cross-sectional age groups rather than longitudinal follow-up, null tracking at 2.5 Hz could reflect signal-to-noise limits rather than absence of foot-level processing.

### 10. Stressing people out doesn't change how cues pull them toward a habit

**Journal:** *Learning & Behavior* | **Published:** September 2026 (epub February 12, 2026)
**PMID:** 41680576 | **DOI:** 10.3758/s13420-026-00707-5
**Novelty:** Failed replication
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a clear, honestly reported double null against a popular addiction theory; limited by whether the stress inductions actually bit.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Two experiments tested whether stress amplifies Pavlovian-to-instrumental transfer — the effect where a cue previously paired with an outcome boosts a related action, widely proposed as a mechanism by which stress triggers relapse. Stress was induced online (negative images plus heavy metal music) in Experiment 1 and in person (anticipating a filmed speech) in Experiment 2, using a quasi-avoidance-based transfer task. Neither specific nor general transfer effects were modulated by elevated stress in either experiment.

**Why it matters:** The stress-drives-cue-reactivity story is a load-bearing assumption in relapse models. A pair of well-specified human nulls does not demolish it, but it does suggest the effect is not robust in laboratory humans, and that the mechanism may live elsewhere.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Two experiments, one popular theory of relapse, no effect. Lead on the value of publishing what didn't happen. The scenario to open on: a lab making participants listen to heavy metal and anticipate a filmed speech, then finding the cues pulled just as hard as before. Call Charlotte Bonardi at the University of Nottingham.
- **Wider angle:** For a specialist psychology or open-science outlet: a piece on how laboratory stress inductions are validated — whether a filmed-speech threat or a playlist really produces anything like the stress state that precedes real relapse, and what that means for a whole literature of stress-manipulation studies.

**Caveats:** Null results can reflect weak manipulations or insufficient power rather than absent effects, laboratory analogue task with non-clinical participants, one experiment run online with limited control over conditions, sample sizes not reported in the abstract.

### 11. Perfectionism and bad sleep feed each other — and stress is the go-between

**Journal:** *Behavioral Sleep Medicine* | **Published:** September–October 2026 (epub April 16, 2026)
**PMID:** 41989986 | **DOI:** 10.1080/15402002.2026.2657920
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — highly relatable and uses daily sampling rather than one-off questionnaires, but small and heavily female.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Sixty-three Australian adults (85.7 percent female, mean age 30) completed 14 days of daily diaries covering sleep, perfectionism and stress. Higher perfectionism was linked to greater variability in sleep onset. Within individuals, higher perfectionism on a given day predicted higher stress, which in turn predicted poorer sleep quality — but poorer sleep did not directly predict next-day perfectionism, though it did so between people via stress.

**Why it matters:** Perfectionism is usually treated as a stable trait; here it fluctuates day to day and does its damage to sleep indirectly, through stress. That points at stress management rather than personality change as the tractable target for perfectionists who cannot sleep.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Your perfectionism isn't keeping you awake — the stress it generates is. Open on someone lying awake replaying a day's small failures. The daily-diary design is the hook: perfectionism turns out to wobble from Tuesday to Wednesday. Call Su Yeon Oh or Lisa Phillips at the University of Melbourne. No clinical claims — this is correlational within-person data.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a psychology or lifestyle outlet on the reframing of perfectionism as a state rather than a trait, and what that does to interventions built on the assumption it is fixed.

**Caveats:** Small N (63), overwhelmingly female sample, self-reported sleep via diary rather than actigraphy or polysomnography, observational within-person design, 14-day window.

### 12. Lonely brains respond less to their own heartbeat

**Journal:** *Biological Psychology* | **Published:** September 2026 (epub July 15, 2026)
**PMID:** 42456921 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.biopsycho.2026.109347
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — an appealing brain-body result with a hypothesis stated in advance, but N=39 and correlational.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Thirty-nine healthy adults completed self-report interoception questionnaires, heartbeat counting and discrimination tasks, and resting EEG with simultaneous ECG so researchers could measure the heartbeat-evoked potential — the brain's cortical response to each beat. Loneliness was associated with lower scores on four interoception subscales (Not Worrying, Emotional Awareness, Self-Regulation, Trusting) and with attenuated late heartbeat-evoked potentials over left frontal electrodes, localized to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Behavioral accuracy at detecting heartbeats was unrelated to loneliness, and the brain result held after controlling for depressive symptoms, sex and age.

**Why it matters:** Loneliness is normally framed as a social problem with cardiovascular consequences. This points at a bodily-signal-processing route: lonely people may not be worse at detecting internal signals so much as worse at appraising and regulating them — a hypothesis worth testing properly.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Loneliness may change how your brain listens to your body. The hook is the dissociation — self-reported bodily trust and cortical response both drop, but raw heartbeat-detection accuracy does not. Open on someone counting their own pulse in a quiet lab. Call Kenta Ono at Hiroshima University. Frame as hypothesis-generating, not as a biomarker.
- **Wider angle:** For a neuroscience-interested outlet: the rise of the heartbeat-evoked potential as a window into interoception, and how a signal once dismissed as cardiac artifact became a measure of selfhood, emotion and now social isolation.

**Caveats:** Small N (39), cross-sectional and observational, healthy volunteers only, single site, source localization with sLORETA has limited spatial precision, multiple interoception subscales tested.

### 13. For homeless young adults, family support predicts less depression — friends don't

**Journal:** *American Journal of Orthopsychiatry* | **Published:** September 3, 2026
**PMID:** 42691267 | **DOI:** 10.1037/ort0000969
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a surprising null for peer support in a hard-to-reach group, but baseline cross-sectional data only.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using baseline data from a randomized rapid-rehousing trial, researchers assessed 240 young people aged 18–24 experiencing homelessness on perceived family and friend support, depressive symptoms, suicide attempts and physiological stress. Youth with no family in their support network or with low family support reported significantly higher depressive symptoms than those with high family support, and those with no family had over three times the odds of a past-year suicide attempt. Friend support showed no significant association with any outcome, and network substance use or criminal involvement did not moderate the effects.

**Why it matters:** Services for homeless youth often lean on peer networks and treat families as the source of the problem rather than a potential resource. These data suggest family ties — where they exist and are supportive — carry weight that friendships do not.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The people who left home still do better if someone at home is on their side. Lead on the friend-support null, which nobody expected. Open on a 20-year-old sofa-surfing with a strong peer group and no family contact. Call Natasha Slesnick at Ohio State. Be careful: this is association, and reconnecting young people with harmful families is not the implication.
- **Wider angle:** For a social policy or long-form outlet: family reunification as a contested strategy in youth homelessness services — where it helps, where it endangers, and why the evidence base is so thin.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional baseline data from a trial (cannot establish causation), self-reported support and psychological outcomes, single-city sample of service-engaged youth may not generalize, physiological stress measure not described in detail in the abstract.

### 14. How you chew during the day predicts how hard you grind your teeth at night

**Journal:** *Sleep and Breathing* | **Published:** September 1, 2026
**PMID:** 42681265 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11325-026-03792-y
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a concrete, testable daytime marker for a nocturnal behavior, verified with in-lab polysomnography; single-center and observational.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** One hundred and twenty adults underwent 3D jaw tracking during standardized chewing tasks, then a night of laboratory polysomnography with bilateral masseter EMG and audio-video monitoring. Compared with people who chewed by alternating sides, those with a preferred-side chewing habit had roughly 70 percent higher bruxism episode indices and higher peak muscle activity; irregular chewers had the longest episodes and the highest proportion of sustained (tonic) grinding. The associations held after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, caffeine, smoking, perceived stress and apnea-hypopnea index.

**Why it matters:** Sleep bruxism is common, damaging and awkward to diagnose without an overnight lab study. A daytime motor-control signature that survives adjustment for stress and sleep apnea would be a practical risk marker — and hints that grinding is as much about sensorimotor coordination as about anxiety.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Your dentist may be able to guess how you sleep from how you eat lunch. The surprise is that the link survived adjustment for perceived stress — the factor bruxism is usually blamed on. Open in a jaw-tracking lab watching someone chew. Call Shuchen Zhou at Sechenov University. Present this as a candidate marker, not a diagnostic test.
- **Wider angle:** For a health or dentistry-facing outlet: teeth grinding as a movement disorder rather than a stress symptom — a reframing that would change who treats it and how.

**Caveats:** Single center, observational (cannot establish causation), one night of polysomnography with well-documented first-night effects, N=120 with subgroups smaller still, no independent validation cohort.

### 15. Pressure to hold onto your heritage — not to assimilate — tracks heavier drinking

**Journal:** *Behavioral Medicine* | **Published:** September 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42682243 | **DOI:** 10.1080/08964289.2026.2717740
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — flips the usual assumption about which direction of acculturation pressure hurts, but a cross-sectional convenience sample.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** A cross-sectional online survey of 628 Hispanic college students aged 18–25 in the US split acculturation stress into two components: pressure to acculturate to the dominant culture and pressure *against* acculturation (from one's own community). Pressure to acculturate was not associated with alcohol use severity; pressure against acculturation was. Nativity status, self-esteem and resilience all moderated the relationship.

**Why it matters:** Decades of acculturation-stress research have used single composite measures and assumed the harm comes from pressure to assimilate. Splitting the construct suggests the pressure that predicts drinking runs the other way — from within the in-group.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The acculturation stress that tracks drinking is not the pressure to fit into the mainstream but the pressure not to drift from home. Lead on the reversal of expectations. Open on a first-generation student navigating both. Call Manuel Cano at UT Southwestern. Avoid framing this as advice — it is a cross-sectional association in one student sample.
- **Wider angle:** For a sociology or migration-focused outlet: how "acculturation stress" became a catch-all in health research, and the push to unbundle it into pressures that come from different directions with different effects.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional (cannot establish causation), convenience sample of college students not representative of the wider Hispanic population, self-reported alcohol use, moderation analyses generate multiple comparisons.

### 16. Who a sexual assault survivor tells — and who tells them the wrong thing

**Journal:** *American Journal of Orthopsychiatry* | **Published:** September 3, 2026
**PMID:** 42691265 | **DOI:** 10.1037/ort0000956
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — an unusually granular mapping of survivors' actual social networks; cross-sectional and campus-limited.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers conducted egocentric social network analysis with 423 sexual assault survivors on a US college campus, mapping 3,863 individual network members. Network composition — including the number of other survivors present, the number of disclosure recipients and the presence of unsupportive responders — was associated with PTSD, depression and anxiety symptoms. Survivors with minoritized identities were less likely to disclose to, or report supportive responses from, network members who did not share their race or sexual orientation.

**Why it matters:** Post-assault support is usually studied as one survivor and one confidant. Treating it as a network shows that the shape of someone's social world — not just whether they told anyone — tracks their symptoms, and that identity matching shapes who gets told at all.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Whether a survivor recovers may depend less on telling someone than on who else is in the room. Lead on the network map itself — nearly 4,000 named people. Open on the moment of first disclosure. Call Allyson Blackburn at Illinois Urbana-Champaign. Frame carefully: association, not causation, and no implication that survivors are responsible for their networks.
- **Wider angle:** For a campus-affairs or investigative outlet: the shift from bystander-intervention training to "supporter" training, and whether university programs are equipped to reach the specific people survivors actually turn to — often peers who share their identity and have no training at all.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional (cannot establish causation), single university campus, self-reported symptoms and network member characteristics reported by the survivor rather than the network members themselves, undergraduate population may not generalize.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch independently find that stress explains less than the standard story says once it is measured properly, adjusted for, or actually manipulated. Hourly smartphone and chest-sensor sampling found stress did not suppress exercise and if anything nudged activity up ("Stress doesn't stop you exercising", PMID 42612762); two experiments failed to show stress amplifying Pavlovian-to-instrumental transfer, a load-bearing assumption in relapse models ("Stressing people out doesn't change how cues pull them toward a habit", PMID 41680576); a daytime chewing signature predicted sleep bruxism *after* adjusting for perceived stress, the factor grinding is usually blamed on (PMID 42681265); and stress-linked impulsivity in an affluent sample showed up only for $100 stakes, vanishing at $10,000 (PMID 42693377). Alongside these, resilience — stress's protective counterpart — worked only as a broad main effect and failed to buffer the highest-risk youth (PMID 42697249).

**Why pitch this now:** Ecological momentary assessment, wearables and deliberately confound-stripped samples are arriving in bulk at the same time, and they are shrinking a construct that has underwritten a decade of health-behavior interventions, relapse-prevention models and workplace wellness programs. The interesting story is not one null result but a coordinated methodological squeeze on how stress got measured in the first place.

**Angle:** A reported feature on what happens to psychology's most-used explanatory variable when researchers stop asking people to remember their stress and start sampling it by the hour, adjusting for it, or inducing it under controlled conditions — following the researchers running these designs and the interventions built on the older evidence. Aimed at general science readers who have absorbed "stress causes X" as background common sense.

**Potential headlines:**
- What stress stops predicting when you measure it every hour
- Stressed people didn't skip the gym, and the cues still pulled just as hard
- The stress excuse you tell yourself may not survive a chest sensor
