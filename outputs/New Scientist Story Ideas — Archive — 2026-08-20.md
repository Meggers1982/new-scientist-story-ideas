### 1. People are increasingly choosing partners with similar height and education levels — and the trend is accelerating

**PMID:** 42608467 | **DOI:** 10.1038/s41562-026-02556-8
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 9/10 — Very large human cohort (N>440,000), counterintuitive accelerating trend in mate choice, clean genomic methodology, and a strong societal hook around inequality and social sorting — near-ideal NS Mind material.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Using a novel genomic method to reconstruct parental haplotypes from close relatives, researchers analysed data from 245,884 UK Biobank and 194,325 Estonian Biobank participants to estimate how strongly people choose partners with genetically similar traits. Across 69 traits, they found evidence of assortative mating — people pairing with those genetically similar to themselves — for height, educational attainment, overall health, and TV-watching habits. Crucially, assortment for both height and education has accelerated in recent generations: among those born after 1980 versus before 1960 in the Estonian cohort, the genetic signal for height assortment more than doubled.

**Why it matters:** If people are increasingly mating with those of similar stature and education, this could amplify genetic and social inequalities across generations, making the rich-and-tall more likely to pair together over time and concentrating certain traits within social strata.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Tall people are increasingly choosing tall partners — and it's getting more extreme with every generation" A genomic study of nearly half a million people finds that assortative mating — choosing partners who are genetically similar to you — has more than doubled for height in just two generations, and education is following the same trajectory. It was once thought that people loosely preferred similar partners but that the effect was modest and stable. A new analysis of 440,000 UK and Estonian genomes suggests otherwise: not only are people pairing with those of similar height and educational background, but this tendency has accelerated markedly in recent decades. The implications go beyond romance — if like increasingly pairs with like at a genetic level, social and biological inequalities could compound across generations in ways that self-reinforce. The study introduces a novel genomic technique that detects mate-choice patterns invisible to older methods. Why it fits: NS Mind readers care deeply about human social behaviour, inequality, and what drives partner choice — this finding links all three in a surprising, data-rich way.

**The study:** Researchers conducted a scoping review of 70 catamenial epilepsy (CE) studies drawn from 233 screened, examining how the condition is defined and diagnosed. But a new scoping review finds that about-monthly seizure cycles appear in men, women, and children alike, and that most studies diagnosing CE didn't even define it consistently or monitor patients long enough to tell. The finding suggests a biological rhythm unrelated to menstruation may be behind the pattern, raising questions about whether thousands of women have been diagnosed — and treated — on a false premise. **Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers retrospectively analysed intracranial and scalp EEG recordings from 20 focal epilepsy patients undergoing presurgical monitoring, identifying 507 total seizures. Among these, 133 (26.2%) were 'epileptic arousals' — seizures that manifest only as waking from sleep. **Why it matters:** If more than a quarter of seizures in focal epilepsy patients are invisible to standard monitoring and look identical to normal waking, many patients may be undertreated and their sleep chronically disrupted without anyone realising — with implications for seizure counts, driving safety, and treatment decisions.

**Story angles:**

New research suggests that roughly one in four seizures in focal epilepsy patients are 'epileptic arousals' — events that look, to the outside world, like someone simply waking up. Using invasive brain recordings, researchers found that standard scalp EEG misses two-thirds of them entirely. The only reliable clue may be the heart: heart rate surges during these events are far larger than during normal awakenings. For patients whose seizure counts determine whether they can drive or how their medication is adjusted, missing 26% of events is a serious clinical blind spot. Why it fits: Sleep disruption and hidden illness are core NS Mind themes; this reframes what a 'seizure' looks like in a dramatic way.
