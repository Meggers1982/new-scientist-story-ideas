### 1. People with bipolar disorder or schizophrenia accumulate chronic physical illness decades early — and young women fare worst

**Journal:** *The Lancet Psychiatry* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42586082 | **DOI:** 10.1016/S2215-0366(26)00196-3
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — enormous, population-wide cohort with a genuinely startling age-and-sex gradient; limited by hospital-registry data and no lived-experience input.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers tracked 178,665 people in Queensland, Australia, through statewide hospital registries from 2000 to 2023 — 30,189 with schizophrenia-spectrum or bipolar-spectrum disorders (median age 32) matched 4:1 to hospital patients without them. They counted not single illnesses but how many separate *body systems* had developed chronic disease. The gap widened the more systems were involved: adjusted hazard ratios rose from 1.85 for one affected system to 3.16 for five or more, and among women under 25 the hazard ratio for five-system multimorbidity reached 9.24.

**Why it matters:** The familiar statistic is that severe mental illness shortens life by 15–20 years; this quantifies the machinery behind it, showing disease accumulating across multiple organ systems from young adulthood rather than clustering in later life.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The counterintuitive hook is that the youngest patients show the largest excess, not the oldest — a 23-year-old woman recently diagnosed with bipolar disorder is at nine times the risk of having five body systems affected within 20 years. Open on the moment of first psychiatric admission as an under-recognized moment of physical-health triage. Call Sean Halstead and Dan Siskind at the University of Queensland; Toby Pillinger at King's College London for outside comment.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a health-policy or general-interest outlet on the structural reasons psychiatric patients fall out of physical medicine — "diagnostic overshadowing," fragmented services, and what integrated young-adult clinics in Denmark and Finland are trying instead.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot establish causation); hospital registry data means the comparison group is also hospital patients, likely underestimating the gap versus the general population; no ethnicity data; medication effects not disentangled from illness effects; several co-authors report extensive pharmaceutical industry ties (study itself unfunded).

### 2. Lithium's reputation as the anti-suicide drug does not hold up against valproate

**Journal:** *Psychological Medicine* | **Published:** 21 September 2026
**PMID:** 42765379 | **DOI:** 10.1017/S0033291726105881
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — directly challenges a strongly held clinical belief with both new cohort data and a network meta-analysis; limited by observational confounding and low event numbers.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using 20 years of Hong Kong Hospital Authority records, researchers compared 1,379 people with bipolar disorder who started lithium monotherapy with 5,556 who started valproate, balancing the groups statistically. There was no significant difference in suicide risk (weighted HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.54–1.08). A network meta-analysis pooling 12 observational studies and 3 trials reached the same conclusion: both drugs beat no treatment (lithium HR 0.46; valproate HR 0.63), but neither beat the other.

**Why it matters:** Lithium is widely described as uniquely protective against suicide, a claim that shapes prescribing for a drug with demanding monitoring and real toxicity. This suggests the anti-suicide effect may be a mood-stabilization effect rather than something special about lithium — though observational data cannot settle it.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the deflation: one of psychiatry's most-repeated claims — "lithium is the only drug shown to reduce suicide" — survives as "mood stabilizers reduce suicide." Open on the clinical dilemma faced by a patient weighing lithium's blood tests and thyroid risks against valproate. Call Francisco T. T. Lai at the University of Hong Kong; worth pairing with a UK lithium researcher such as Joseph Hayes at UCL for the counterargument.
- **Wider angle:** A specialist-outlet piece on why definitive head-to-head trials of old, off-patent psychiatric drugs almost never get funded — the meta-analysis here couldn't even pool its three RCTs because they were methodologically incompatible.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot establish causation); confounding by indication is near-certain, as lithium tends to be given to different patients than valproate; suicide is a rare outcome so confidence intervals are wide; single-territory cohort with prescribing patterns that may not generalize; meta-analytic component rests almost entirely on observational studies.

### 3. Walking more lifts your mood, and your mood gets you walking — but screen time only pushes one way

**Journal:** *Psychological Medicine* | **Published:** 21 September 2026
**PMID:** 42765378 | **DOI:** 10.1017/S0033291726105297
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — an intensively sampled, passively sensed within-person design that separates cause-candidates from correlations; limited by modest N and small effect sizes.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Over 14 days, 128 people (mean age 37.8; 82 of them offspring of a parent with bipolar disorder) reported positive and negative affect five times daily while their phones passively logged step counts and screen-on time. Using dynamic structural equation modeling, positive affect and step count fed each other bidirectionally; higher positive affect predicted *less* subsequent screen time, and more screen time predicted subsequent negative affect. Negative affect, strikingly, predicted nothing at all. Familial risk didn't change these dynamics — it just shifted everyone's baseline mood down.

**Why it matters:** The asymmetry is the story: good mood appears to be the engine of the behavior–feeling loop, while bad mood is largely a passenger — which cuts against the intuition that low mood is what drives people to the sofa and the phone.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Everyone assumes feeling bad is what makes you scroll. In this data, feeling *good* is what makes you put the phone down and go outside — and the loop runs identically whether or not you carry familial risk for bipolar disorder. Open on a participant's ordinary Tuesday as reconstructed from five mood pings and a step counter. Call Sarah Sperry at the University of Michigan or Manon Hillegers at Erasmus MC.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a psychology or technology outlet on what "passive sensing" studies are quietly revealing about screen time — that the effects are real but tiny, and that the interesting question has shifted from how much people use their phones to what mood state they are in when they pick them up.

**Caveats:** Small N (128) with only 32 participants having any mood-disorder history and just 3 with recurrent disorder, so the risk-moderation null is underpowered; self-reported affect; effect sizes are very small (β ≈ 0.03–0.07); screen-on time doesn't capture what people were doing; 14-day follow-up only; Dutch cohort.

### 4. Brain blood flow is lowest in bipolar disorder with psychosis — and higher flow tracks worse symptoms

**Journal:** *Psychological Medicine* | **Published:** 24 September 2026
**PMID:** 42779463 | **DOI:** 10.1017/S0033291726105844
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — very large multi-site imaging sample and a neat dissociation between diagnosis and symptom severity; limited by small bipolar subgroup and null cognitive findings.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers pooled arterial spin labeling scans — an MRI method that measures blood flow without injected tracer — from 2,412 people with schizophrenia, 2,278 with schizoaffective disorder, 158 with psychotic bipolar disorder and 401 neurotypical controls, mapping resting cerebral blood flow across 60 brain regions. People with psychotic bipolar disorder showed the most pronounced reduction in gray matter blood flow (d = −0.259), with hypoperfusion across 25 regions. Yet *within* patients, higher overall blood flow correlated with worse psychotic symptoms, and hyperperfusion of the right superior temporal sulcus tracked psychotic, manic and depressive symptoms alike.

**Why it matters:** The finding splits trait from state — chronically low perfusion may mark who has the disorder, while transient overactivity may mark how ill they are right now — and supports efforts to sort psychosis by biology rather than by DSM label.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Diagnosis by category barely predicted brain perfusion; a "biotype" derived from neurophysiology did. Lead on the awkward fact that the bipolar group, usually thought of as the milder end of the psychosis spectrum, had the largest perfusion deficit. Call Paulo Lizano at Beth Israel Deaconess, and Carol Tamminga at UT Southwestern for the biotype framework.
- **Wider angle:** A longer feature for a neuroscience or medical outlet on the decade-long attempt to replace psychiatric diagnoses with biomarker-defined "biotypes" — what B-SNIP has and hasn't delivered, and why no brain measure has yet made it into a clinic.

**Caveats:** Observational design; the bipolar group (n=158) is far smaller than the schizophrenia groups, so the largest effect rests on the smallest sample; cross-sectional, so trait-versus-state interpretation is inferred, not demonstrated; medication effects on cerebral blood flow are a known confound and hard to exclude; no CBF–cognition associations survived correction.

### 5. Long-acting antipsychotic injections help some people with intellectual disability — and may be harming those prescribed them off-label

**Journal:** *BMJ Mental Health* | **Published:** 8 September 2026
**PMID:** 42711066 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bmjment-2026-302830
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a clever within-person design answering a question almost no trial has touched, with a genuinely divergent result for off-label use.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Using 20 years of Hong Kong health records, researchers identified 1,675 adults with intellectual disability who had been on both long-acting injectable and oral antipsychotics at different times, and compared each person to themselves. Among those with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder, injections were linked to fewer hospitalizations and dramatically fewer extrapyramidal side effects (IRR 0.34), though more cardiovascular admissions. Among those *without* such a diagnosis — where the drugs are prescribed off-label, typically for challenging behavior — injections were associated with *more* all-cause and somatic hospitalizations and no benefit for behavior problems.

**Why it matters:** Antipsychotics are prescribed to a large fraction of people with intellectual disability who have no psychotic illness, a practice campaigners have challenged for years with little hard outcome data; this provides some.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The same drug, the same people, opposite outcomes depending on why it was prescribed. Open on the scale of off-label antipsychotic use in intellectual disability services and what "deprescribing" would actually involve. Call Esther Chan at the University of Hong Kong, and Shoumitro Deb at Imperial College London, a long-standing critic of overprescribing in this population.
- **Wider angle:** An investigative or policy piece for a UK health outlet revisiting the STOMP program — the decade-old NHS drive to stop overmedicating people with learning disabilities and autism — and whether prescribing has actually fallen.

**Caveats:** Observational design and self-controlled case series cannot rule out that people were switched to injections precisely when they were deteriorating; single territory; off-label subgroup may differ systematically in ways within-person design cannot fix; several authors declare extensive pharmaceutical funding including from manufacturers of long-acting injectables.

### 6. The body's own cannabis-like chemicals fall as mania lifts — unless the person uses cannabis

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 15 December 2026 (epub 16 September 2026)
**PMID:** 42735776 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122483
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a clean longitudinal within-patient design and a plausible biological story about why cannabis complicates bipolar recovery, but only 23 patients.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Twenty-three people with bipolar I disorder had blood drawn during an acute manic episode and again after clinical stabilization, with the endocannabinoids anandamide and 2-AG measured by mass spectrometry. In non-users of cannabis, both molecules fell significantly as mania resolved. In cannabis users, levels stayed flat despite equivalent clinical improvement — and endocannabinoid levels didn't track symptom severity scores in either group.

**Why it matters:** It hints that recovery from mania involves a biochemical reset that cannabis use blocks, offering a candidate mechanism for the well-documented but poorly explained link between cannabis and worse bipolar outcomes. Early stage — a hypothesis worth testing, not a clinical finding.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Two patients leave the ward looking equally well; only one of them has biochemically returned to baseline. Lead on the gap between feeling better and being better. Call Isabella Pacchiarotti or Eduard Vieta at Hospital Clínic de Barcelona; Michael Berk at Deakin for the broader bipolar-biomarker view.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a science or drug-policy outlet on the endocannabinoid system as a mood regulator — a signaling network discovered only because researchers went looking for what cannabis binds to, and which is now implicated in everything from appetite to stress recovery.

**Caveats:** Small N (23); no healthy control group sampled in parallel; cannabis use self-reported and group sizes not stated; plasma endocannabinoids are an indirect proxy for brain signaling; medication changes between the two timepoints are an obvious confound; multiple authors report extensive pharmaceutical industry payments.

### 7. People with serious mental illness want AI companions — but only ones built with them, not for them

**Journal:** *Community Mental Health Journal* | **Published:** 25 September 2026
**PMID:** 42789145 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10597-026-01732-4
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — rare first-person data from a group usually discussed rather than consulted, and an irresistible quote; limited by qualitative secondary analysis and an author's commercial stake.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers reanalyzed three focus groups held across supportive housing facilities in New York City with residents living with schizophrenia-spectrum disorder, bipolar disorder or major depression. Participants were simultaneously enthusiastic and wary: they valued AI for reminders, daily functioning and simple companionship — one said "if no one told you I love you today, you have someone that cares for you" — while raising pointed concerns about data misuse, overreliance and losing their own critical thinking. Trust rose specifically when tools were personalized and co-designed with people who have lived experience.

**Why it matters:** Companion chatbots are being pushed hardest at exactly the people most likely to be isolated and least likely to be consulted about the design; this is a rare case of asking them directly.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The quote in the title is the hook, and the surprise is how sophisticated the skepticism is — these are not naive users, they're worried about surveillance and about outsourcing their own judgment. Open on a resident in supportive housing weighing loneliness against privacy. Call Karen Fortuna at Dartmouth's Patient Innovation Lab — and note upfront that she holds equity in a companion-AI company.
- **Wider angle:** A reported feature for a technology or social-affairs outlet on the emerging market in AI companionship for isolated populations — who is buying it, whether it substitutes for staffed human support, and what regulators are doing about apps that say "I care about you."

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (three focus groups, N not reported); secondary analysis of data gathered for another purpose; single city and single housing-model population may not generalize; senior author holds an equity interest in Companion, Inc.; no outcome data whatsoever — this is perceptions only.

### 8. A blood test may show whether lithium is reaching someone's brain

**Journal:** *International Clinical Psychopharmacology* | **Published:** 4 September 2026
**PMID:** 42742966 | **DOI:** 10.1097/YIC.0000000000000637
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — an appealing proof-of-concept linking gene activity in blood to brain structure, but cross-sectional and non-randomized.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** In 116 people experiencing a depressive episode within bipolar disorder, researchers compared gene expression in circulating immune cells between those on long-term lithium and those not, then linked that expression to diffusion MRI measures of white matter. Five genes differed (SEPTIN11, DGKK and TPPP3 up; LEF1-AS1 and PLXDC1 down), and crucially the relationship between those genes' activity and white matter microstructure existed *only* in lithium-treated patients.

**Why it matters:** Lithium works well for some people and not others, and there is currently no way to tell early which is which. A peripheral transcriptomic signature that tracks brain white matter would be a step toward a blood-based readout of the drug's biological effect — though it is nowhere near a clinical test.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Seventy-plus years after lithium entered psychiatry, nobody can say why it works or predict who it will help. Lead on that embarrassment, then on the idea of reading the drug's brain effects from a blood sample. Call Francesco Benedetti and Sara Poletti at San Raffaele in Milan.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a chemistry or history-of-medicine outlet on lithium itself — the cheapest, oldest, most effective and least understood drug in psychiatry, now also a strategic mineral, and increasingly hard to persuade young psychiatrists to prescribe.

**Caveats:** Observational and cross-sectional (cannot establish causation); lithium-treated and untreated patients were not randomized, so the groups likely differ in illness course and other medications; single center; modest N for a transcriptomics study; no replication cohort; gene expression in blood is an indirect proxy for brain biology.

### 9. Half of people with anorexia or bulimia also meet criteria for a personality disorder — but almost nothing is known about why

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychosomatic Research* | **Published:** 17 September 2026
**PMID:** 42777340 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpsychores.2026.112997
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a rigorous credibility audit of an entire literature with a striking negative verdict, but it's a review of reviews with no new data.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** An umbrella review pulled together 61 meta-analyses yielding 213 estimates on what co-occurs with anorexia, bulimia and binge-eating disorder, then re-pooled the data and graded how trustworthy each estimate was. Prevalence figures held up reasonably well — any personality disorder in 49% of anorexia and 54% of bulimia cases, non-suicidal self-injury in 37% of bulimia, tobacco use disorder in 47.7% of binge-eating disorder, bipolar disorder in 9.1% of binge-eating disorder. But not a single estimate of a *risk factor* or *outcome* reached convincing or even highly suggestive credibility.

**Why it matters:** It draws a sharp line between what the eating-disorder literature can claim (these conditions travel together) and what it cannot (which causes which, or what happens next) — a distinction routinely blurred in both clinical guidance and coverage.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** After decades of research and hundreds of meta-analytic estimates, we can say what co-occurs with eating disorders and essentially nothing about why or what follows. Lead on that gap and what it means for someone being assessed today. Call Michele Fornaro at Federico II University of Naples.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-focused piece for a specialist or academic-audience outlet on umbrella reviews as an accountability tool — the growing practice of grading whole evidence bases and how often headline findings turn out to be "weak credibility."

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of existing meta-analyses of observational studies — inherits every bias in the source literature; restricted to three eating-disorder diagnoses, excluding ARFID and others; prevalence estimates carry very wide intervals (bipolar disorder in BED: 3–23%); no causal inference possible.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- The diagnostic-category problem recurs. Last run, "Bipolar disorder and schizophrenia look identical in blood tests at the first psychotic episode" (PMID 42112712) and "Memory circuits go wrong in different ways in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder" (PMID 42679550) both probed whether biology respects DSM boundaries. This run, "Brain blood flow is lowest in bipolar disorder with psychosis — and higher flow tracks worse symptoms" (PMID 42779463) lands on the same fault line from a much larger imaging sample, and with an added twist: the perfusion deficit was largest in the smallest, supposedly mildest group.
- The "tidy evidence, messy patient" thread continues. The trial-eligibility study from Naples (PMID 42575197) is echoed by the same senior author's umbrella review this run, "Half of people with anorexia or bulimia also meet criteria for a personality disorder — but almost nothing is known about why" (PMID 42777340), which finds that not one risk-factor or outcome estimate in an entire comorbidity literature reaches convincing credibility.
- Long-acting injectable antipsychotics get a second, better-powered look. "Loading the dose of a long-acting antipsychotic injection upfront shortens hospital stays for women" (PMID 41924987, N=87, retrospective) is followed by "Long-acting antipsychotic injections help some people with intellectual disability — and may be harming those prescribed them off-label" (PMID 42711066), a within-person analysis of 1,675 people over 20 years — a substantial step up in design quality on the same drug class.

**Advances**
- Biomarker ambition has shifted from crude to targeted. Last run's negative result on routine blood counts (PMID 42112712) is answered this run by "A blood test may show whether lithium is reaching someone's brain" (PMID 42742966), which links peripheral gene expression to white matter microstructure — a more specific, though still preliminary, version of the same "read the brain from blood" idea.
- The overturning genre recurs with a bigger target. "Serotonin abnormality blamed on borderline personality disorder turns out to belong to depression" (PMID 42173043) reattributed one biomarker; "Lithium's reputation as the anti-suicide drug does not hold up against valproate" (PMID 42765379) questions one of psychiatry's most-repeated prescribing claims, using both a Hong Kong cohort and a network meta-analysis.

**Counterpoints**
- Two explanations for the cannabis–bipolar link now sit side by side. "Genes for addiction — not for drinking or smoking as such — explain why bipolar disorder and substance problems travel together" (PMID 42636965) located the overlap in inherited dependence liability; "The body's own cannabis-like chemicals fall as mania lifts — unless the person uses cannabis" (PMID 42735776) proposes a state-level biochemical mechanism instead. Neither rules the other out, and the pairing is a ready-made piece on trait versus state accounts of comorbidity.

**New themes**
- Physical multimorbidity arrives as a topic in its own right with "People with bipolar disorder or schizophrenia accumulate chronic physical illness decades early — and young women fare worst" (PMID 42586082); nothing in the previous digest addressed bodily disease burden directly.
- The digital thread has moved from prediction to sensing and companionship: last run's algorithm bake-off (PMID 42600789) is replaced by passive phone sensing of mood and steps (PMID 42765378) and by first-person views on AI companions from people in supportive housing (PMID 42789145).
- Off-label prescribing in intellectual disability (PMID 42711066) is a new population for this topic and opens onto the UK's STOMP program.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Three studies in this batch describe what happens to the bodies of people with serious mental illness, and the picture is worse and starts earlier than the standard "15–20 years of life lost" line implies. The Queensland cohort found chronic disease accumulating across five or more organ systems from young adulthood, with a hazard ratio of 9.24 in women under 25 (PMID 42586082); the Hong Kong within-person study found that antipsychotic injections prescribed off-label for challenging behavior in intellectual disability were followed by *more* somatic hospitalizations with no behavioral benefit, and that even on-label use raised cardiovascular admissions (PMID 42711066); and the lithium-versus-valproate analysis removes the main justification for accepting lithium's thyroid and renal monitoring burden — its supposedly unique anti-suicide effect — in favor of no difference (PMID 42765379). The passive-sensing study adds the one lever that points the other way: positive mood and step counts feed each other, while low mood predicts nothing (PMID 42765378).

**Why pitch this now:** The physical-health penalty of severe mental illness is usually reported as a single mortality statistic; this batch breaks it into organ systems, ages, prescribing decisions and a plausible behavioral entry point, all within a month, and it does so as regulators and services in the UK and Australia are being pressed on deprescribing and on integrated young-adult clinics.

**Angle:** Follow the physical body through a psychiatric career — first admission at 23 as an unrecognized moment of medical triage, the mood stabilizer chosen partly on a claim that may not hold, the injection given for behavior rather than illness, and the modest, real gains available from simply moving more. Aimed at a general science-literate readership interested in how medicine divides the mind from the rest of the patient.
