### Study 1: Heavy phone use and depression travel together, but one may not be driving the other
**PMID:** 42748880 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Sample description overstates wave completion** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Chinese college students (N=851 retained) were surveyed four times across nine months, from September 2025 to June 2026"
- **Abstract says:** "Analyses retained 851 participants with at least two observed waves; 189 eligible students were first observed at T2."
- **Problem:** The study ran four waves, but the 851 analyzed participants were not all surveyed four times — inclusion required only two observed waves. The Caveats line notes the 189 T2 entrants, but the body text contradicts it by implying a complete four-wave sample.
- **Suggested fix:** "Chinese college students were surveyed across four waves from September 2025 to June 2026; 851 participants with at least two completed waves were retained for analysis."

**Issue B. NS fit score of 7 rests entirely on self-report** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — a rare four-wave, within-person test of the phone-causes-depression story that largely dissolves it; limited by self-report and a student sample."
- **Abstract says:** All measures are questionnaire-based; the authors' own conclusion is that the forward association "was model-dependent and did not establish a mechanism, causal effect, or statistically reliable directional difference."
- **Problem:** The design is genuinely strong for its class, but the entire evidence base is self-reported, single-country and single-population, and the headline finding is a model-dependent null. A 7 is at the top of what this can carry.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10, with the same rationale text.

**Issue C. "Wider angle" overclaims the methodological reckoning** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "how the shift from cross-lagged to random-intercept models has quietly demolished a decade of 'X predicts Y' findings across psychology — screens, social media, loneliness, sleep"
- **Abstract says:** Reports only that in this sample the forward PSU→depression path was modest and model-dependent; makes no claim about the wider literature.
- **Problem:** "Demolished a decade of findings" is hype that outruns a single four-wave student study, and the digest's novelty tag "Overturns prior research" leans the same way. The angle is legitimate as a reported feature, but it should be flagged as a thesis to be tested with outside methodologists, not an established state of play.
- **Suggested fix:** "...how the shift from cross-lagged to random-intercept models has prompted re-examination of a decade of 'X predicts Y' findings across psychology — screens, social media, loneliness, sleep — and what, if anything, survives the reanalysis."

### Study 2: Seeing drug-related images breaks down basic sensory filtering in people who use methamphetamine
**PMID:** 42740334 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal attribution of GABAergic "damage" to stimulant use** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "what GABAergic damage from stimulants means for long-term cognition"
- **Abstract says:** "Adults who use methamphetamine exhibit altered gating responses and elevated spontaneous gamma activity in the somatosensory cortices, likely reflecting disruptions in GABAergic circuitry."
- **Problem:** The study is cross-sectional and group-comparative; it cannot establish that methamphetamine caused the inhibitory differences, nor that the differences constitute "damage." The digest's own Caveats say direction cannot be established, so the angle text contradicts the caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "...and what apparent GABAergic differences in people who use stimulants might mean for long-term cognition."

**Issue B. Timing claim not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a drug cue can degrade low-level sensory filtering within a fraction of a second"
- **Abstract says:** "Immediately before the first stimulation of each trial, participants viewed either a substance-related or neutral image."
- **Problem:** The abstract gives no cue-to-stimulus interval and reports no latency data, so the "fraction of a second" figure is unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "a drug cue presented immediately beforehand can degrade low-level sensory filtering"

**Issue C. "Breaks down" / "collapse" overstate the effect size** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** Headline: "Seeing drug-related images breaks down basic sensory filtering"; pitch: "why its collapse implies drug cues hijack the brain far earlier in processing"
- **Abstract says:** "only those in the methamphetamine group exhibited weaker gating in the substance-cue compared to the neutral image condition"; "Substance-related cues accentuated gating deficits."
- **Problem:** The finding is a relative weakening of gating in one group, not a breakdown or collapse of the mechanism.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Drug-related images weaken basic sensory filtering in people who use methamphetamine"; pitch: "why this weakening implies..."

Note: the 7/10 score here is defensible — objective MEG measures, a within-subject experimental cue manipulation and a control group offset the modest N.

### Study 3: What gamblers believe about gambling predicts their trajectory better than how well they manage emotions
**PMID:** 42143790 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Treatment implication reverses the authors' own clinical recommendation** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "If beliefs about one's own thinking, rather than raw emotional coping, drive the escalation of gambling problems, treatment should target those beliefs directly — which is what metacognitive therapy does"
- **Abstract says:** "...a potential protective role of emotion regulation in the cycle. These findings highlight the important role of metacognitions in GD vulnerability and provide clinical insights such as incorporating emotion regulation training in Metacognitive Therapy based interventions for GD."
- **Problem:** The authors recommend *adding* emotion regulation training to metacognitive therapy, not substituting belief-targeting for emotional coping work. The digest's framing ("rather than raw emotional coping", NS fit note "demotes the fashionable emotion-regulation explanation") drops the protective finding and turns a two-wave observational result into a treatment prescription.
- **Suggested fix:** "Metacognitions were the stronger prospective predictor, but emotion regulation appeared to buffer the vicious cycle — which is why the authors suggest building emotion regulation training into metacognitive therapy for gambling disorder rather than choosing between them."

**Issue B. Example belief items not sourced from the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "beliefs such as 'gambling helps me control my thoughts' or 'my urge to gamble is uncontrollable'"
- **Abstract says:** Refers only to "positive and negative metacognitions about gambling"; no item wording is given.
- **Problem:** Plausible paraphrases of standard instrument items, but unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** Attribute to the measure explicitly, or soften: "beliefs about the usefulness of gambling thoughts and about the uncontrollability of gambling urges".

**Issue C. Disclosed conflict omitted from Caveats** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Caveats: ... funders include Chongqing municipal social science and education commission programs."
- **Abstract says:** "Given their role as Editor-in-Chief and an Associate Editor of Addictive Behaviors respectively, Prof. Marcantonio Spada and Prof. Anise M. S. Wu had no involvement in the peer review of this article..."
- **Problem:** Two of the study's authors hold senior editorial positions at the journal that published it. The digest proposes calling both as sources without noting this or that they are co-authors rather than independent commentators.
- **Suggested fix:** Add to Caveats: "Two authors (Spada, Wu) are the journal's Editor-in-Chief and an Associate Editor and were recused from peer review; both are study co-authors, not independent commentators."

### Study 4: Self-control protects teenagers from internet addiction — except when they are being punished
**PMID:** 42727287 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal language in headline and body for cross-sectional data** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** Headline: "Self-control protects teenagers from internet addiction"; body: "Lower self-control predicted more internet addiction, partly via stress"
- **Abstract says:** "self-control is significantly and negatively associated with IA, with negative life events partially mediating this relationship"; the paper frames everything as "association patterns."
- **Problem:** "Protects" and "predicted" assert causation and temporal order from a single-timepoint survey. The Caveats correctly say the design cannot establish causation, so the headline contradicts them.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Self-control is linked to less internet addiction in teenagers — except under punishment-related stress"; body: "Lower self-control was associated with more internet addiction, with stress partly accounting for the link."

**Issue B. "Symptom network" misnames the network** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "academic stress sitting at the center of the symptom network"
- **Abstract says:** "Network analysis further revealed that learning stress occupied a central position within the network structure."
- **Problem:** The network includes self-control components, stressor domains and internet addiction indicators, not symptoms alone.
- **Suggested fix:** "academic (learning) stress sitting at the centre of the overall network"

**Issue C. Nationality of sample inferred, not stated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A survey of 4,305 Chinese high school students"
- **Abstract says:** "a large-scale survey of 4305 high school students" (author affiliations: Zhejiang Normal University, China)
- **Problem:** Reasonable inference from affiliation, but unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "A survey of 4,305 high school students, conducted by researchers at Zhejiang Normal University in China"

### Study 5: A blood marker flags which methamphetamine users will still have thinking problems a year later
**PMID:** 42379379 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline presents a group-level association as individual-level prediction** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "A blood marker flags which methamphetamine users will still have thinking problems a year later"
- **Abstract says:** "higher baseline nectin-4 levels were associated with greater total errors... after one year of treatment... Larger studies are needed to replicate the association between nectin-4 levels and executive function."
- **Problem:** The paper reports regression/GEE associations, not a validated predictive test — no cut-off, sensitivity, specificity or discrimination statistics are reported. "Flags which users will" implies a usable individual-level screen. The "Why it matters" paragraph hedges appropriately, but the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "A blood marker is linked to how much thinking ability recovers a year into methamphetamine treatment"

**Issue B. Nectin-4 described with a function not given in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "plasma nectin-4, a cell adhesion molecule involved in synapse formation"
- **Abstract says:** "Nectin-4, a cell adhesion molecule linked to addiction severity"
- **Problem:** The synapse-formation role may be true in the wider literature but is unverifiable from abstract only; the abstract characterises it by its addiction-severity link.
- **Suggested fix:** "plasma nectin-4, a cell adhesion molecule previously linked to addiction severity"

### Study 6: Semaglutide blocks three different relapse triggers for fentanyl in rats
**PMID:** 42228850 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Blocks" overstates the cue-induced result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** Headline: "Semaglutide blocks three different relapse triggers for fentanyl in rats"
- **Abstract says:** "all doses of semaglutide fully prevented drug- and stress-induced reinstatement... while the mid (0.056 mg/kg) and higher doses (0.078 mg/kg) most effectively reduced cue-induced fentanyl seeking." Paper title: "attenuates cue-, drug-, and stress-induced fentanyl seeking."
- **Problem:** Cue-induced seeking was reduced, not blocked, and only at two of three doses. The body text gets this right; the headline flattens it.
- **Suggested fix:** "Semaglutide blunts all three classic relapse triggers for fentanyl in rats"

**Issue B. Novelty framing omits prior GLP-1 opioid findings** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 5/10 — the first test of the blockbuster weight-loss drug against opioid seeking, and it works on cue, drug and stress triggers"; "Anecdotal reports of GLP-1 drugs dampening cravings for alcohol, nicotine and opioids have run far ahead of the evidence"
- **Abstract says:** "a growing preclinical and clinical literature suggests that glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) agonists may serve as new treatments for OUD. Exendin-4/exenatide and liraglutide have been found to reduce opioid intake, cravings, and cue-, drug-, and stress-induced opioid seeking."
- **Problem:** It is the first test of *semaglutide* specifically for opioid seeking, but other GLP-1 agonists have already shown the same three-trigger profile in opioids, and the abstract explicitly describes an existing preclinical and clinical literature — so "run far ahead of the evidence" and the framing of the triple effect as the novelty both need qualifying.
- **Suggested fix:** "...the first test of semaglutide specifically against opioid seeking, extending earlier results with exenatide and liraglutide to a once-weekly agent."

### Study 8: The immune system may be running the brain chemistry of addiction
**PMID:** 42425280 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Explanation for past anti-inflammatory trial failures asserted as finding** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the reason anti-inflammatory drugs have failed inconsistently is that they were given at the wrong phase of the cycle"; and in Why it matters, "the drug-specific, phase-specific framing explains why anti-inflammatory approaches have given messy results so far"
- **Abstract says:** "We outline a novel hypothesis that control of a newly defined neuroimmune-glutamate circuit and inflammasome is heavily dependent upon the type of addictive drug as well as on phase of the addiction cycle."
- **Problem:** The abstract offers this as an untested hypothesis and makes no claim about previous anti-inflammatory trials. Stating it as "the reason" presents a prediction as an established explanation.
- **Suggested fix:** "The authors' hypothesis implies that drug type and phase of the addiction cycle determine whether immunomodulation helps — a possible explanation, as yet untested, for inconsistent results from anti-inflammatory approaches."

The rest — tripartite synapse of the nucleus accumbens, inflammasome, opponent process, polysubstance predictions, Gipson at the University of Kentucky, and the review/hypothesis framing — is accurate.

### Study 10: Heavy digital media use is tied to higher BMI, and comfort eating sits in the middle of the link
**PMID:** 42785564 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Caveat asserts self-reported BMI, which the abstract does not state** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Caveats: ... self-reported BMI and behavior"
- **Abstract says:** "The data collection tools included Descriptive Information Form, Digital Addiction Scale (DAS), Palatable Eating Motives Scale (PEMS), BMI and MEDLIFE index."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not specify whether height and weight were measured or self-reported; a nutrition and dietetics department may well have measured them. Unverifiable from abstract only — asserting it as a limitation is as much a factual claim as asserting the opposite.
- **Suggested fix:** "self-reported questionnaire measures throughout; the abstract does not state whether BMI was measured or self-reported"

The statistics are otherwise handled well: both indirect effects were significant but small (β 0.048 for BMI, β −0.032 for MEDLIFE), and the digest correctly notes the mediation was more pronounced for BMI.

### Study 11: Gambling harm in Kazakhstan concentrates in middle-aged men, not the young
**PMID:** 41887939 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline contrast is with older, not younger, participants** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Gambling harm in Kazakhstan concentrates in middle-aged men, not the young"
- **Abstract says:** "Men and middle-aged individuals (45-59 years) were found to be most vulnerable to gambling addiction, while women and elderly participants showed lower levels of addiction."
- **Problem:** The explicit low-severity comparison group in the abstract is elderly participants; younger adults are not separately reported. The "not the young" contrast is an inference, and the abstract gives no figures for the 18–30 band.
- **Suggested fix:** "Gambling severity in Kazakhstan peaked among men aged 45 to 59 — not the demographic the stereotype predicts"

**Issue B. "Kazakh adults" conflates nationality with ethnicity** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Two samples of Kazakh adults aged 18–65"
- **Abstract says:** "The research included two samples of individuals aged 18 to 65" (study conducted in Kazakhstan; ethnicity not reported).
- **Problem:** "Kazakh" denotes an ethnic group; the abstract reports only that the study was carried out in Kazakhstan.
- **Suggested fix:** "Two samples of adults aged 18–65 in Kazakhstan"

The rehabilitation-effectiveness claim is correctly attributed to the authors and correctly flagged in Caveats as lacking design detail.

### Study 12: When one man gambles online, the whole extended family pays the debt — and swears an oath
**PMID:** 42704516 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline misattributes who swears the oath** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "When one man gambles online, the whole extended family pays the debt — and swears an oath"
- **Abstract says:** "Distinctive contextual mechanisms emerged, including the extended family's collective debt-clearing in exchange for religious oaths"
- **Problem:** The family clears the debt; the oath is given in exchange, by the gambling member. The headline reads as though the family swears it. The body text handles this correctly ("extended families collectively clearing a member's debts in exchange for religious oaths to stop").
- **Suggested fix:** "When one man gambles online, the extended family pays the debt — and he swears an oath"

The N=9, Siirt setting, four themes, perception-not-direct-experience limitation and non-generalisability are all accurately carried over, and the pitch correctly frames this as lived experience rather than evidence of effectiveness.

### Study 13: Gambling, gaming, social media and porn use may run through the same chain of thought
**PMID:** 42801890 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Shared engine" framing edges into a claim the authors explicitly disclaim** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The hook is the shared engine: the same loop of rehearsing how good something will feel seems to sit behind gambling, gaming, scrolling and porn."
- **Abstract says:** "The findings suggest possible cross-domain relevance of EMSs, desire thinking, and craving, but do not establish temporal or causal ordering, diagnostic equivalence, or a single underlying addiction process."
- **Problem:** The authors go out of their way to rule out the single-underlying-process reading; "the shared engine... sits behind" all four behaviours is exactly that reading. The digest's Caveats flag the cross-sectional design and the unrecorded referent, but not this explicit disclaimer, so the pitch angle's hook rests on a stronger result than the paper supports.
- **Suggested fix:** "The hook is a possible shared ingredient rather than a shared disorder: the loop of rehearsing how good something will feel shows up alongside all four behaviours — though the authors stress this does not establish a single underlying addiction process." Add the disclaimer to Caveats.

Sample details (811 Iranian adults, 43.4% male, mean age 27.70), the serial-versus-parallel model comparison, the unrecorded self-selected activity and the Griffiths funding and consultancy disclosures are all accurate.
