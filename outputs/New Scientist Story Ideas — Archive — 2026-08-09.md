**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

### 2. The brain's 'autopilot' region switches off consciousness in surprisingly different ways depending on whether drugs or injury are to blame

**Journal:** *Brain: a journal of neurology* | **Published:** 2026 Aug 7
**PMID:** 42565506 | **DOI:** 10.1093/brain/awag272
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — Counterintuitive finding (pharmacological and pathological unconsciousness have different neural fingerprints) in a human fMRI study with a clear societal hook around consciousness and hidden awareness, but penalised for very small combined sample (N=38) and single-centre design.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers used functional MRI to map how nine basal ganglia brain nuclei and 19 neurotransmitter systems contribute to loss of consciousness, comparing healthy volunteers under propofol anaesthesia (n=16) with patients with disorders of consciousness including minimally conscious state and unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (n=22). The study found that pharmacologically induced and pathologically induced loss of responsiveness involve distinct neurotransmitter signatures across individual basal ganglia regions — for example, propofol anaesthesia was linked to norepinephrine and acetylcholine changes in the putamen and ventral pallidum, while pathological disorders of consciousness were associated with serotonin and dopamine changes in the caudate. Notably, the substantia nigra — a dopamine-rich structure — showed the largest difference between patients who could respond to mental-imagery tasks and those who could not, suggesting it may be a key marker of residual awareness.

**Why it matters:** Distinguishing the neural fingerprints of drug-induced unconsciousness from those of brain injury could help clinicians better assess hidden awareness in patients who appear unresponsive, and point toward more targeted treatments for disorders of consciousness. It also deepens our understanding of what the brain actually does when it 'switches off.'

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Being knocked out by anaesthesia and being in a coma are not the same thing in your brain" New brain imaging suggests that losing consciousness from propofol and losing it from severe brain injury involve completely different neural mechanisms — meaning the two states may be far less similar than medicine has assumed. A Cambridge-led study scanned both healthy volunteers under propofol anaesthesia and patients in minimally conscious or vegetative states, mapping how different brain structures and chemical systems contributed to unresponsiveness in each group. They found strikingly different neurotransmitter signatures — drugs hijack norepinephrine and acetylcholine circuits, while brain injury disrupts serotonin and dopamine pathways. Perhaps most provocatively, a dopamine-rich region called the substantia nigra most reliably distinguished patients who retained hidden awareness from those who did not — raising the possibility of a biological marker for covert consciousness. Why it fits: Questions about consciousness, hidden awareness in unresponsive patients, and what anaesthesia actually does to the brain are perennial NS Mind topics that resonate with a broad, curious readership.

The study is observational, conducted at a single UK centre, and cannot establish causal direction. The DOC patient group is heterogeneous, mixing minimally conscious and unresponsive wakefulness states *Editor's fact-check note: Sample sizes confirmed from abstract: propofol group n=16, DOC patients n=22.
