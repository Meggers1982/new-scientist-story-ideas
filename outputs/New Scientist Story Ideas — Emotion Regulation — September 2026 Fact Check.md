# Fact-Check Report: New Scientist Story Ideas — Emotion Regulation — September 2026
**Checked:** 2026-09-08 | **Studies reviewed:** 22
**Publication:** New Scientist | **Section:** New Scientist Mind

---

### Study 1: Distracting yourself from something upsetting makes it hit harder the next time you see it
**PMID:** 42693580 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Recognition-accuracy result narrowed to negative pictures** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Images processed under high load were also recognized less accurately."
- **Abstract says:** "Negative pictures that had previously been presented on high-load compared to low-load trials also had lower recognition accuracy."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports the recognition deficit for negative pictures specifically, relative to low-load negatives — not for images in general.
- **Suggested fix:** "Negative images seen under high load were also recognized less accurately than those seen under low load."

**Issue B. Habituation mechanism stated more firmly than the abstract does** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "works only in the moment and prevents the habituation that would otherwise blunt the stimulus later"
- **Abstract says:** "results suggest a mechanism through which disengaged emotion regulation strategies may fail to attenuate and may even increase emotional responses to stimuli when encountered again outside of the initial regulation context"
- **Problem:** "Prevents habituation" asserts a mechanism the authors only propose; the data show equivalence to novel pictures, not a demonstrated blocking of habituation.
- **Suggested fix:** "works only in the moment and may prevent the habituation that would otherwise blunt the stimulus later"

**Issue C. Late positive potential named as the index** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "reduced brain response (the late positive potential) to negative images at the time"
- **Abstract says:** "working memory load reduced picture processing" / "Electrocortical Processing" (title); the LPP is not named.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only — plausible for this lab, but the specific ERP component is not stated.
- **Suggested fix:** "reduced the electrocortical response to negative images at the time (confirm the ERP component in the methods)"

*NS fit note:* 7/10 is defensible for a within-subject experimental EEG design with N = 73, though 6 would also be fair given the standardized-picture paradigm.

### Study 2: Deliberately dampening your own good mood softens the crash that follows
**PMID:** 42468402 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Flattens the peak" is not reported in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Then the catch: the strategy that smooths the drop also flattens the peak."
- **Abstract says:** "the negative emotional contrast from before versus after the sadness induction was less pronounced in the dampening condition compared to the relaxation condition."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports only the reduced contrast; it does not report that dampening lowered peak positive affect. Unverifiable from abstract only, and it is presented as a study finding.
- **Suggested fix:** "Then the likely catch — smoothing the drop plausibly means starting from a lower high, which the paper should be checked on before the pitch leans on it."

*Note (not an issue):* Michelle Newman is a co-author on this paper (Penn State), so the pitch should describe her as an author rather than an independent outside voice.

### Study 3: In depression, reappraisal is barely used — and distraction only helps when you actually want to feel good
**PMID:** 42660575 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. NS fit score high for a small self-report ESM study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — undermines the therapy world's favorite strategy in daily life, though N is modest."
- **Abstract says:** "a 7-day experience-sampling design in a sample of clinically depressed individuals (N = 65)"; "Lagged multilevel models"; authors call for future studies of "a wider array of emotion goals."
- **Problem:** A 7 rests on N = 65, entirely self-reported strategy and affect data, no comparison group, and a within-person observational design. The claim being sold ("reappraisal doesn't work in real life") is large relative to the evidence.
- **Suggested fix:** "**NS fit:** 6/10 — undermines the therapy world's favourite strategy in daily life, but N = 65 and all measures are self-reported."

Study facts, statistics and direction of effect are otherwise accurately reproduced (reappraisal infrequent and unrelated to later affect; NA-reduction goal → acceptance → improved affect; distraction helpful only with a concurrent goal to increase PA, opposite pattern without it).

### Study 4: People will pay money to find out exactly how much a coming pain will hurt
**PMID:** 41347565 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Never bid" overstates the non-bidding third** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a third of participants never bid"
- **Abstract says:** "Two thirds of the sample regularly made bids."
- **Problem:** The remaining third did not bid *regularly*; the abstract does not say they never bid at all.
- **Suggested fix:** "a third of participants did not bid regularly"

**Issue B. NS fit score for an explicitly exploratory study with no N reported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — pushes back on the fashionable 'information avoidance' story with real heat pain and real money; exploratory design tempers it."
- **Abstract says:** "This exploratory study tested whether participants actively seek information…"; no sample size is given, and the pain-reduction result applies to "Some participants."
- **Problem:** A 7 rests on an exploratory design with an unreported N and a subgroup-only pain effect.
- **Suggested fix:** "**NS fit:** 6/10 — pushes back on the fashionable 'information avoidance' story with real heat pain and real money, but the design is explicitly exploratory and the sample size is not stated in the abstract."

*Minor wording note:* the abstract says "suppression as an emotion regulation strategy"; the digest's "expressive suppression" adds a qualifier the abstract does not use.

### Study 5: Being properly listened to makes advice work better — and shows up as synchrony between two brains
**PMID:** 42288208 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Strangers" not stated in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "strangers in a laboratory task"
- **Abstract says:** "Seventy-four female dyads (the regulator and the target) were recruited to complete the IER task."
- **Problem:** The relationship between dyad members is not specified. Unverifiable from abstract only — and it matters, since acquaintance dyads would change the interpretation of the listening manipulation.
- **Suggested fix:** "laboratory dyads (check whether partners were strangers or acquainted)"

N (74 female dyads), the fNIRS hyperscanning design, the behavioural advantage of active listening for reappraisal, and the frontal pole–dlPFC synchrony pattern are all accurately reproduced.

### Study 6: Couples who acknowledge each other mid-argument pull each other out of bad moods in real time
**PMID:** 42441643 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Isolates the mechanism" overstates an observational finding** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It isolates a single, trainable behavior as the moment-to-moment mechanism of couple co-regulation"
- **Abstract says:** "These findings underscore the importance of partner validation as a potential behavioral mechanism of interpersonal emotion regulation (i.e., coregulation), suggesting that it may facilitate better adjustment to parenthood."
- **Problem:** The authors call validation a *potential* mechanism; the digest presents it as identified and established, and adds "trainable," which was not tested. This also contradicts the digest's own caveat that the design is observational.
- **Suggested fix:** "It points to a single, specific behaviour as a potential moment-to-moment mechanism of couple co-regulation"

**Issue B. Temporal resolution ("second by second", "timed to the second")** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "coded second by second for affect" / "a specific behavior whose effect on your partner's mood can be timed to the second"
- **Abstract says:** "video recorded and coded for affective behaviors using the Specific Affect Coding System. Using state-space grids and grid-sequence analysis, we tracked real-time affective exchanges."
- **Problem:** The coding resolution is not stated. Unverifiable from abstract only; "timed to the second" also implies a causal precision the design does not deliver.
- **Suggested fix:** "coded continuously for affective behaviour" / "a specific behaviour whose association with the partner's next affective state can be tracked in real time"

**Issue C. "First child" not stated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "filmed while both partners were pregnant with their first child"
- **Abstract says:** "93 expectant-parent couples"; "The transition to parenthood represents a critical period."
- **Problem:** First-time parenthood is implied by the framing but not stated. Unverifiable from abstract only. (The phrasing "both partners were pregnant" is also loose.)
- **Suggested fix:** "filmed during the pregnancy, before the couple became parents"

### Study 7: Five weeks of slow breathing changes how the amygdala and prefrontal cortex covary in size
**PMID:** 42669395 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Randomized" not stated in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "large randomized biofeedback trial with a rare active-comparison design" / "in a randomized design where one group was asked to make their heart rhythms *less* variable"
- **Abstract says:** "Baseline and post-intervention MRI scans were collected from 151 participants (100 younger and 51 older adults) who completed 5 weeks of daily biofeedback sessions to either increase (Osc + condition) or decrease (Osc- condition) HRV."
- **Problem:** Random assignment is not mentioned. Unverifiable from abstract only (the parent trial may well be randomized, but it should be confirmed before the word is used in a pitch).
- **Suggested fix:** "large biofeedback trial with a rare active-comparison design (confirm random assignment in the parent trial)"

**Issue B. "Daily 20 minutes of paced breathing" not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Open on the daily 20 minutes of paced breathing volunteers did at home."
- **Abstract says:** "5 weeks of daily biofeedback sessions"; no session duration or breathing protocol is given, and the Osc− arm is not described as slow breathing.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only; also note the headline's "slow breathing" describes the Osc+ arm, not both conditions.
- **Suggested fix:** "Open on the daily home biofeedback sessions volunteers completed for five weeks."

The structural covariance results (right amygdala–left mPFC opposing patterns; left amygdala–right OFC inverse association in Osc+) are accurately reproduced, and the "clinical meaning is unproven" hedge is appropriate.

### Study 8: Four weeks of memory training changes how six-year-olds' brains handle upsetting images
**PMID:** 42632448 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Source localization presented without its "exploratory" label** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "with source estimates pointing to reduced prefrontal activation"
- **Abstract says:** "Exploratory source localization further suggested reduced estimated activation in prefrontal-related regions."
- **Problem:** The abstract flags this analysis as exploratory and the activations as estimated; the digest presents it as a supporting result on equal footing with the LPP finding.
- **Suggested fix:** "with exploratory source estimates tentatively pointing to reduced prefrontal activation"

N (78), the three-arm randomized design, 16–20 sessions over ~four weeks, and the single-group LPP effect are all accurate, and the caveats correctly note the absence of behavioural outcomes.

### Study 9: Depressed patients get real, short-lived relief from telling someone — then the cycle restarts
**PMID:** 41885910 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline narrows the sample to depression** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Depressed patients get real, short-lived relief from telling someone"
- **Abstract says:** "Participants (N = 205; Mage = 31.87…) with depression or anxiety disorders"
- **Problem:** The sample is mixed depression/anxiety patients; the body text says so but the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Patients with depression or anxiety get real, short-lived relief from telling someone"

**Issue B. Causal verb for a within-person association** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "and both drove more emotional suppression next time"
- **Abstract says:** "Both were associated with increased expressive suppression at the subsequent measurement."
- **Problem:** "Drove" implies causation; the abstract calls the whole set of findings "preliminary evidence" for cyclical patterns. The caveats note this, but the body language contradicts them.
- **Suggested fix:** "and both were followed by more emotional suppression at the next report"

### Study 10: Persistent grumpiness and explosive tempers look like different things in children's brains
**PMID:** 42702353 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Referral-rate claim not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Irritability is one of the commonest reasons young children are referred to mental health services"
- **Abstract says:** "Irritability is a common and impairing feature of childhood psychopathology"
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only; the specific referral claim needs an external citation before it appears in a pitch.

Sample (160 children, mean age 6.17, irritability-enriched community screening), the tonic/phasic distinction, and the two independent effects (tonic → ventral striatal connectivity during nonreward/failure; phasic → amygdala–prefrontal and striatal–cingulate differentiation) are accurately reported, and "partially separable" matches "partially dissociable."

### Study 11: Social stress, not trauma, is what stops refugees leaning on other people to manage their feelings
**PMID:** 42619365 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal framing on a cross-sectional survey** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Social stress, not trauma, is what stops refugees leaning on other people to manage their feelings" / "identifying social conditions in the host country as the thing that severs people from their main emotional resource"
- **Abstract says:** "A sample of 561 refugees in Australia completed a survey…"; "Higher social stress was associated with elevated depression through reduced engagement of all interpersonal ER strategies"; "social stressors may be particularly harmful for refugee mental health and may be an important target mechanism."
- **Problem:** Both the headline ("is what stops") and the "Why it matters" line assert causation and direction from a single-timepoint self-report survey with SEM-estimated indirect paths. The caveats acknowledge this, but the body text contradicts them.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Social stress, not trauma, tracks with refugees turning away from other people to manage their feelings." Why it matters: "…pointing to social conditions in the host country as a candidate target, if the direction of effect holds up."

**Issue B. Specific stressor examples not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Social stressors — isolation, discrimination, loss of community —"
- **Abstract says:** "social and practical post-migration stressors"
- **Problem:** The content of the social stressor factor is not itemised in the abstract. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Social stressors (as measured by a post-migration stressor scale)"

### Study 12: How well you read your own body — not how you think about feelings — separates the active from the inactive
**PMID:** 42413746 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Psychologically indistinguishable" contradicts the attitudes result** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the highly active group was psychologically indistinguishable from the merely moderately active"
- **Abstract says:** "No differences emerged between the Moderate and High groups in emotional regulation or interoception. Approach and Avoidance toward physical effort significantly differed across all three physical activity levels."
- **Problem:** The Moderate/High null applies only to emotion regulation and interoception; the two groups *did* differ on attitudes toward physical effort, which is one of the study's main outcomes. The pitch hook as written is not supported. (The digest's own body text notes attitudes differed across all three levels, so the pitch angle contradicts the study description.)
- **Suggested fix:** "the highly active group looked no different from the merely moderately active on emotion regulation and interoception — though their attitudes toward physical effort still differed"

Everything else checks out: N = 1,073, mean age 35.4, ANCOVAs controlling gender, age, education and BMI, the interoception and anxiety/depression pattern, the Approach/Avoidance predictors, and the "moderate levels may be sufficient" conclusion.

### Study 13: Fathers are slower than mothers to respond to a distressed baby — and three-way play stresses infants most
**PMID:** 42269373 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Heart rate read as "distress" and "stress"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Fathers are slower than mothers to respond to a distressed baby — and three-way play stresses infants most"
- **Abstract says:** "fathers showed delayed responses to infant arousal, whereas triadic interactions elicited stronger associations and higher HR levels"
- **Problem:** The measured variable is infant heart rate/arousal, not distress; higher HR in triadic free play could reflect engagement or excitement. The caveats note HR is a coarse index, but the headline goes further than the abstract's language.
- **Suggested fix:** "Fathers are slower than mothers to respond to a baby's arousal — and three-way play raises infant heart rate most"

**Issue B. Counterbalancing claim not verifiable** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "fixed session order not counterbalanced as described"
- **Abstract says:** "Eighty families with three-month-old infants completed three successive two-minute free-play sessions corresponding to these contexts."
- **Problem:** The abstract says the sessions were successive but does not state the order or whether it was counterbalanced. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "session order and whether it was counterbalanced is not specified in the abstract — check the methods"

- **As written:** "Inga Niedtfeld's group (Central Institute of Mental Health, Mannheim)."
- **Abstract says:** "Niedtfeld I, Hepp J, Schellhaas S, Steffen-Klatt A, Klaasen van Husen D, Held V." — no affiliations are listed.

### Study 15: Whether you cry in front of someone depends on how agreeable you are — and on whose sadness it is
**PMID:** 42537321 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Null result in other crying contexts asserted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "while showing no such suppression in other crying contexts"
- **Abstract says:** "Japanese young adults with higher agreeableness scores tend to suppress crying in the presence of others in situations involving empathic responses to others' distress, specifically when crying was triggered by a close person's sadness."
- **Problem:** "Specifically when" implies context-dependence, but the abstract does not report explicit null findings for the other five categories. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "with the effect specific to that context in the reported analyses"

N (169), the Japanese university sample, the six crying categories, and the empathy/personality measures are accurate, and the caveats correctly flag retrospective self-report and single-country sampling.

### Study 16: Mothers who bottle up their feelings have toddlers with more screen time
**PMID:** 42447742 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Wider-angle claim goes beyond this study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the emerging evidence that parental screen use predicts children's better than any rule does"
- **Abstract says:** "Higher levels of maternal non-interactive screen media use were linked to greater non-interactive and interactive screen media use among toddlers."
- **Problem:** This study measured no screen-time rules, so it cannot support a comparison between parental modelling and rule-setting. Unverifiable from abstract only; needs external sourcing or rewording.
- **Suggested fix:** "the evidence that parental screen use tracks children's — and what that means for 'digital parenting' advice built around limits"

The core findings are correctly reproduced: N = 296 mothers of 18–36-month-olds, suppression linked to maternal non-interactive use and both forms of toddler use, no reappraisal associations, maternal non-interactive use linked to both forms of toddler use. The pitch's explicit "this is correlational" instruction is good practice.

### Study 17: Savoring works just as well on the biggest highs as on the small ones
**PMID:** 42398883 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Absence of any interaction" overstates the Bayesian result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Bayesian analysis supported the absence of any interaction — with weak hints that savoring works slightly *better* on high-arousal material"
- **Abstract says:** "Bayesian results were consistent with the absence of an interaction between stimulus arousal level and condition for the LPP, but yielded anecdotal evidence that savoring might be more effective at increasing arousal ratings of high- versus low-arousing pictures."
- **Problem:** The null-interaction evidence applies to the LPP specifically; for arousal ratings there was anecdotal evidence *of* an interaction. "Any interaction" is too broad.
- **Suggested fix:** "Bayesian analysis supported the absence of an interaction for the LPP, with anecdotal evidence of one in the opposite direction for arousal ratings — savouring may work slightly better on high-arousal material."

**Issue B. "Fails" overstates the down-regulation literature** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Down-regulating negative emotion by engaging with content fails when the content is intensely arousing"
- **Abstract says:** "engaged emotion regulation strategies… have been found to be better suited to low-arousing stimuli"
- **Problem:** "Better suited to low-arousing stimuli" is not the same as failing at high arousal.
- **Suggested fix:** "Engaged strategies for down-regulating negative emotion work better on low-arousing content"

### Study 18: We rarely use one coping strategy at a time — and only two combinations actually lower distress
**PMID:** 41806361 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Direction of the dysregulation–strategy-use finding is reversed** — Severity: Major

- **As written:** "people with greater emotion dysregulation used more strategies but had more negative affect" / "people who struggle most throw the most strategies at a problem and feel worse"
- **Abstract says:** "Higher event intensity and lower emotion dysregulation predicted greater strategy use during an emotional event. Additionally, greater event intensity and emotion dysregulation predicted greater daily negative affect."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that *lower* emotion dysregulation predicted using more strategies. The digest reverses this, and the entire pitch angle ("quantity versus quality — people who struggle most throw the most strategies at a problem") is built on the reversed finding. Pitched as written, this would be a factual error in print.
- **Suggested fix:** "people with *less* emotion dysregulation used more strategies per event, while greater dysregulation predicted more daily negative affect" — and rebuild the pitch angle around the fact that better regulators, not worse ones, deploy more strategies at once.

**Issue B. "Combinations" mislabels the effective strategies** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "We rarely use one coping strategy at a time — and only two combinations actually lower distress" / "only situation selection (removing yourself) and reinterpretation predicted lower daily negative affect"
- **Abstract says:** "Within instances of polyregulation, greater use of situation selection and reinterpretation predicted lower daily negative affect."
- **Problem:** These are two individual strategies whose use within polyregulation episodes predicted lower negative affect — not two strategy combinations. The headline's first clause ("We rarely use one strategy at a time") is also not quantified anywhere in the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "When people stack coping strategies, only two of them predict lower distress"

### Study 19: Sensory sensitivity, not IQ, predicts whether self-injury in autistic children gets better or worse
**PMID:** 42657937 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline's "not IQ" contradicts the abstract** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Sensory sensitivity, not IQ, predicts whether self-injury in autistic children gets better or worse"
- **Abstract says:** "Longitudinally, improvement in sensory features (p = 0.026) and higher T3 IQ (p = 0.039) significantly raised odds of decreasing SIB severity."
- **Problem:** Higher later IQ was a significant predictor of improvement alongside sensory features. The headline sets up a contrast the data do not support (the only IQ-related null is that baseline DQ did not predict worsening, and that DQ/IQ group differences emerged only at T3). The digest body reports the IQ finding correctly, so the headline contradicts the entry.
- **Suggested fix:** "Easing sensory sensitivities — and a higher IQ later on — predict whether self-injury in autistic children improves"

**Issue B. "Nearly all these preschoolers" stretches the prevalence figures** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Lead with the prevalence figure — nearly all these preschoolers showed some self-injury at some point"
- **Abstract says:** "At T1, 74% of the sample were characterized as having mild, moderate, or severe SIB… only 9% did not have SIBs at either timepoint."
- **Problem:** The 91% figure applies to the 122 children assessed at both timepoints, not to the full preschool sample, where 74% had SIB at T1. The digest body handles this correctly; the pitch line rounds it up.
- **Suggested fix:** "Lead with the prevalence figure — three-quarters of these preschoolers showed some self-injury at the first assessment, and among those followed to school age only 9% were free of it at both timepoints"

### Study 20: Sixteen hours of home audio reveals which mothers snap when a child gets very upset
**PMID:** 41642821 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Pitch asserts a null the abstract does not report** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "And the finding that 'how well you regulate emotions' in general does not predict how you respond to your own crying child."
- **Abstract says:** "These findings emerged after controlling for maternal difficulties with emotion regulation."
- **Problem:** Controlling for general emotion regulation difficulties is not the same as showing that general emotion regulation does not predict maternal responses. The abstract reports no test of that null. Unverifiable from abstract only, and it is the pitch's second hook.
- **Suggested fix:** "And the finding that a parenting-specific vulnerability predicted responses even after general emotion regulation difficulties were accounted for."

Sample (53 families, children 3–5), the 16-hour recordings, 4–10 coded episodes per child, LIWC emotion-word analysis, and the diathesis-stress interaction (increases in child negative intensity → more nonsupport and less positive emotion talk, only in high trait distress-reactivity mothers) are all accurate.

### Study 21: Virtual reality meditation shifts children's heart rhythms in four short sessions
**PMID:** 41484577 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. Framing is consistent with the abstract: N = 127, ages 9–12, four VR mindfulness sessions, increased high-frequency power and physiological coherence, improved emotional clarity and mood repair, greater HRV gains in Year 5 children, and the contested low-frequency interpretation flagged by the authors. The entry is appropriately explicit that the absence of a comparison group means novelty or expectation cannot be ruled out, and the NS fit of 5/10 is well calibrated to that.

### Study 22: Men in treatment for violence describe consent in a long-term relationship as something that stretches
**PMID:** 42647097 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Unprompted" and "after a certain number of years" are not supported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "these are men currently in treatment for intimate partner violence explaining, unprompted, why they think consent works differently after a certain number of years together"
- **Abstract says:** "To explore men's perceptions of sexual consent in intimate relationships, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 29 men… some viewed it as implied within committed relationships"
- **Problem:** Semi-structured interviews designed to explore consent are by definition prompted, and the abstract frames the "taken for granted" position in terms of commitment, not relationship duration.
- **Suggested fix:** "these are men currently in treatment for violence in intimate and family contexts explaining, in interviews about consent, why they think it works differently inside a committed relationship"

The three themes (fundamental, taken for granted, burden), the elasticity metaphor, N = 29, the treatment population and the University of Montréal authorship are all accurately reproduced, and the caveats correctly rule out generalization.
