### 1. Heavy phone use and depression travel together, but one may not be driving the other

**Journal:** *Addictive Behaviors* | **Published:** September 15, 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42748880 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.addbeh.2026.108859
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a rare four-wave, within-person test of the phone-causes-depression story that largely dissolves it; limited by self-report and a student sample.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Chinese college students (N=851 retained) were surveyed four times across nine months, from September 2025 to June 2026, on problematic smartphone use (PSU) and depressive symptoms. Using random-intercept cross-lagged panel models, the researchers found that roughly half of the variance in PSU and around 60% of the variance in depressive symptoms was stable between-person difference — some people simply sit high on both. The within-person forward path from PSU to later depressive symptoms was small (β ≈ 0.13), the reverse path was not significant, the difference between directions was not statistically reliable, and the effect vanished altogether under an alternative model (LCM-SR).

**Why it matters:** Most of the correlation between problematic phone use and low mood appears to be a trait-like co-occurrence rather than a month-to-month process in which phone use pushes mood down — which weakens one of the most widely repeated claims in popular psychology.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Everyone "knows" the phone is making them sad. Open on a student who deletes an app every January — then show what happens when you track the same people four times and model within-person change instead of between-person snapshots: the effect shrinks to near nothing and flips depending on which model you run. Worth calling the corresponding authors Xinguo Zheng (Northwest Normal University) and Qi Hui (Xi'an University of Science and Technology), and an independent methodologist on why RI-CLPM keeps deflating screen-time findings.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-reckoning feature for a science or policy outlet on how the shift from cross-lagged to random-intercept models has quietly demolished a decade of "X predicts Y" findings across psychology — screens, social media, loneliness, sleep — and what that means for school phone bans already written into law.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot establish causation); all outcomes self-reported; single population of Chinese college students, may not generalize; 189 participants first observed at wave 2; findings explicitly model-dependent by the authors' own account.

### 2. Seeing drug-related images breaks down basic sensory filtering in people who use methamphetamine

**Journal:** *Psychological Medicine* | **Published:** September 15, 2026
**PMID:** 42740334 | **DOI:** 10.1017/S0033291726105686
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a clean human brain-imaging result showing drug cues intrude on a process that isn't supposed to be cognitive at all; modest sample.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Seventy-two adults (35 who regularly use methamphetamine) underwent magnetoencephalography during a paired-pulse somatosensory gating task — a basic test of whether the brain suppresses its response to a repeated touch. Before each trial, participants glimpsed either a substance-related or a neutral image. The methamphetamine group showed weaker gating overall, and crucially only they gated worse after a drug image; they also had elevated spontaneous gamma activity in somatosensory cortex, consistent with disrupted GABAergic inhibition.

**Why it matters:** It suggests cue reactivity in addiction isn't confined to reward circuitry and craving — a drug cue can degrade low-level sensory filtering within a fraction of a second, which may help explain why environments saturated with reminders are so destabilizing.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the surprise: a picture of a pipe makes the brain worse at ignoring a repeated touch on the hand. Open with the paradox of relapse triggered by walking down a familiar street, then explain what sensory gating is and why its collapse implies drug cues hijack the brain far earlier in processing than craving models assume. Tony W. Wilson's group at the Institute for Human Neuroscience, Boys Town National Research Hospital, is the call.
- **Wider angle:** For a health or neurology outlet: sensory gating as an underused clinical readout. The same paired-pulse measure is abnormal in schizophrenia and PTSD — a piece on whether a cheap, passive index of cortical inhibition could become a cross-diagnostic biomarker, and what GABAergic damage from stimulants means for long-term cognition.

**Caveats:** Small N (72, with 35 in the exposure group); cross-sectional and observational — cannot show whether gating deficits precede or follow methamphetamine use; single center; polysubstance use and abstinence duration not detailed in the abstract.

### 3. What gamblers believe about gambling predicts their trajectory better than how well they manage emotions

**Journal:** *Addictive Behaviors* | **Published:** October 2026
**PMID:** 42143790 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.addbeh.2026.108735
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a longitudinal head-to-head that demotes the fashionable emotion-regulation explanation; only two waves.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Five hundred eighty-five Chinese adult lifetime gamblers (mean age 30, 48% women) completed online questionnaires at two time points. In a cross-lagged panel model, metacognitions about gambling — beliefs such as "gambling helps me control my thoughts" or "my urge to gamble is uncontrollable" — were stronger prospective predictors of gambling disorder tendency than emotion regulation ability was. Negative metacognitions were themselves predicted by earlier gambling disorder severity, indicating a self-feeding loop, with emotion regulation appearing to buffer it.

**Why it matters:** If beliefs about one's own thinking, rather than raw emotional coping, drive the escalation of gambling problems, treatment should target those beliefs directly — which is what metacognitive therapy does, and which differs from standard cognitive behavioral approaches focused on gambling fallacies.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is that the problem isn't that gamblers can't handle their feelings — it's what they believe about their own urges, and the belief that an urge is uncontrollable helps make it so. Open on the vicious circle: losing more makes you believe you can't stop, which makes you less able to stop. Call Marcantonio M. Spada (London South Bank University) on metacognitive therapy, and Anise M. S. Wu (University of Macau) on gambling in Macau.
- **Wider angle:** For a features desk: the quiet spread of metacognitive therapy out of anxiety and depression into addiction, gaming and smartphone use — treating the belief about the thought rather than the thought. Pair with the awkward politics of gambling research funding.

**Caveats:** Observational, two waves only (limits causal inference); all self-reported; online convenience sample of Chinese gamblers, may not generalize; funders include Chongqing municipal social science and education commission programs.

### 4. Self-control protects teenagers from internet addiction — except when they are being punished

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** September 11, 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42727287 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107785
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — large sample and a genuinely odd non-linear reversal; cross-sectional and the reversal needs replicating.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** A survey of 4,305 Chinese high school students broke self-control into self-regulation and impulse inhibition, and negative life events into separate domains, then combined mediation and network analysis. Lower self-control predicted more internet addiction, partly via stress — with interpersonal and academic stress carrying the strongest indirect links, and academic stress sitting at the center of the symptom network. The anomaly: within the punishment-related stress domain, higher self-control was associated with *higher* internet addiction.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the tidy assumption that self-control is uniformly protective, hinting that highly self-controlled teenagers under punitive pressure may use the internet as a compensatory escape — a hypothesis worth testing rather than a conclusion.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the reversal: the self-disciplined kid under heavy punishment at home or school looked *more* hooked online, not less. Open on the exam-pressure cooker of Chinese high school and the way academic stress sat at the hub of the whole network. Call corresponding author Wenjun Xu (Zhejiang Normal University), and an independent researcher on the Compensatory Internet Use Model.
- **Wider angle:** An education-outlet piece on what network analysis does to school mental health policy — if academic stress is the central node, then screen-time rules are treating the symptom while the exam system supplies the disease.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional (cannot establish causation); self-reported throughout; Chinese high school students only, may not generalize; the non-linear punishment effect is a sub-analysis and could be a chance finding.

### 5. A blood marker flags which methamphetamine users will still have thinking problems a year later

**Journal:** *Behavioural Brain Research* | **Published:** October 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42379379 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bbr.2026.116353
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a one-year prospective human cohort with a plausible biological marker; needs replication before it means anything clinically.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers in Taiwan measured plasma nectin-4, a cell adhesion molecule involved in synapse formation, in 126 patients with methamphetamine use disorder and 106 healthy controls, and tested executive function with the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test at baseline and again after a year of treatment. Patients had substantially higher nectin-4 levels, and higher baseline levels predicted more total and perseverative errors, fewer categories completed and lower conceptual scores a year later.

**Why it matters:** Executive function strongly shapes who stays in treatment, so a single blood draw that forecasts cognitive recovery would be genuinely useful — but this is one cohort and the mechanism linking a synaptic adhesion molecule to card sorting is unestablished.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open with the clinical problem: two people start the same methamphetamine treatment program and a year later one can plan their week and the other can't. The surprise is that the difference may be readable in blood at the start. Call corresponding author Tzu-Yun Wang at National Cheng Kung University Hospital, and ask a neuroscientist outside the group what nectin-4 is actually doing at the synapse.
- **Wider angle:** For a specialist medical or biotech outlet: the hunt for peripheral biomarkers in psychiatry, where almost nothing has made it to the clinic. Nectin-4 is already the target of an approved cancer antibody-drug conjugate — a story on molecules with double lives across oncology and psychiatry.

**Caveats:** Observational (cannot establish causation); single center; no replication cohort; medication, abstinence and comorbidity effects on nectin-4 not resolved in the abstract; patients lost to follow-up unquantified here.

### 6. Semaglutide blocks three different relapse triggers for fentanyl in rats

**Journal:** *Behavioural Pharmacology* | **Published:** October 1, 2026
**PMID:** 42228850 | **DOI:** 10.1097/FBP.0000000000000880
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — the first test of the blockbuster weight-loss drug against opioid seeking, and it works on cue, drug and stress triggers; held back because it is rodents only.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Male Sprague-Dawley rats trained to self-administer fentanyl were given acute subcutaneous semaglutide at three doses, then exposed to the three classic relapse triggers: drug-associated cues, a priming infusion of fentanyl, and a pharmacological stressor (yohimbine). All doses fully prevented drug- and stress-induced reinstatement of fentanyl seeking; the two higher doses most effectively cut cue-induced seeking.

**Why it matters:** Anecdotal reports of GLP-1 drugs dampening cravings for alcohol, nicotine and opioids have run far ahead of the evidence; this is a controlled demonstration for fentanyl specifically, in the species where the relapse model was built — nothing more, but it strengthens the case for human trials.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the breadth: it isn't just that semaglutide reduced drug seeking, it shut down all three routes back to use, including stress. Open on the gap between the viral "Ozempic killed my cravings" posts and the near-absence of opioid data behind them. Patricia Sue Grigson at Penn State College of Medicine is the call — and note upfront that these are rats and that she consults for Eli Lilly.
- **Wider angle:** For a business or health-policy outlet: GLP-1 receptor agonists are being quietly repositioned as a general-purpose anti-craving class, from alcohol to gambling. Who is funding the human trials, what the regulators would need, and what happens to addiction medicine if a weekly injection outcompetes a treatment infrastructure built on daily dosing and clinic attendance.

**Caveats:** Animal study — findings may not translate to humans; male rats only; acute dosing, no chronic administration or tolerance data; senior author declares consultancy for Eli Lilly; no human craving or abstinence outcomes.

### 7. Real-world reports flag unexpected psychiatric effects for the first pill approved for postpartum depression

**Journal:** *Australasian Psychiatry* | **Published:** September 8, 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42711976 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10398562261481737
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — first large post-marketing safety sweep of zuranolone, with signals the label doesn't carry; disproportionality analyses cannot establish risk.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers mined the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System from Q3 2023 to Q2 2025 for reports naming zuranolone — the first oral drug approved for postpartum depression — as primary suspect, finding 648 patients and 299 preferred terms, analyzed with four standard signal-detection algorithms. Nervous system and psychiatric events dominated. Beyond labeled effects, intrusive thoughts and "feeling drunk" produced strong signals, alongside panic attack, suicidal ideation, dissociation, brain fog and speech disturbance, plus reports of exposure via breast milk.

**Why it matters:** Zuranolone is a neuroactive steroid acting on GABA-A receptors, the same family as benzodiazepines, and the signal pattern raises the question of whether the old GABAkine liabilities — sedation, dissociation, dependence potential — carry over into a drug given to new mothers.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on what zuranolone was sold as: a two-week course that lifts postpartum depression fast, a genuine advance. Then the awkward follow-up — what the first two years of real-world reports look like, including "feeling drunk" and breast milk exposure. Be explicit that spontaneous reporting systems show signals, not rates. Call the authors at Taizhou People's Hospital, and a pharmacovigilance methodologist plus a perinatal psychiatrist for balance.
- **Wider angle:** For a general-interest or women's health outlet: a feature on how little we know about any psychoactive drug in lactation, and why the breastfeeding mother remains one of medicine's least-studied patients despite a wave of new perinatal psychiatric drugs.

**Caveats:** Spontaneous reporting data — reporting bias, no denominator, cannot establish causation or incidence; confounding by indication (suicidal ideation is a feature of severe postpartum depression); no comparison arm; abstract does not report confounder adjustment.

### 8. The immune system may be running the brain chemistry of addiction

**Journal:** *Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews* | **Published:** October 2026
**PMID:** 42425280 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2026.106859
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a coherent new framework with testable predictions for polysubstance use; it is a hypothesis paper built mostly on preclinical work.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** A review and hypothesis paper proposing that the glutamate disruption at the heart of addiction — specifically in the tripartite synapse of the nucleus accumbens — is orchestrated by neuroimmune signaling and the inflammasome, rather than merely accompanied by it. The authors argue this neuroimmune-glutamate circuit behaves as an opponent process whose direction depends on which drug is used and which phase of the addiction cycle someone is in, and they derive predictions for common polysubstance combinations.

**Why it matters:** If immune signaling gates the synaptic changes that drive relapse, then existing immunomodulatory drugs become candidate addiction treatments — and the drug-specific, phase-specific framing explains why anti-inflammatory approaches have given messy results so far.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is a reframe readers won't expect: addiction as partly an immune condition, and the reason anti-inflammatory drugs have failed inconsistently is that they were given at the wrong phase of the cycle. Open with the clinical oddity of someone whose withdrawal looks like being ill. Call lead author Cassandra D. Gipson at the University of Kentucky, and press her on which immunomodulators are closest to a human trial.
- **Wider angle:** A longer feature for a science magazine or specialist outlet on the neuroimmune turn across psychiatry — depression, schizophrenia, now addiction — and the pattern of early enthusiasm followed by underwhelming trials. What would count as the field finally getting it right?

**Caveats:** Review and hypothesis paper, not new data; evidence base is predominantly rodent; no human trial yet tests the central prediction; polysubstance predictions are untested.

### 9. Psychedelics may work by loosening the scaffolding that holds brain circuits in place

**Journal:** *Biological Psychiatry* | **Published:** September 15, 2026
**PMID:** 41765343 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.biopsych.2026.02.011
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — an elegant structural explanation for why one dose has effects lasting months; a review, and the human evidence is thin.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** This review synthesizes cellular and molecular evidence that classic psychedelics — psilocybin, LSD, DMT — do more than stimulate 5-HT2A receptors and the usual plasticity cascades (CaMKII, ERK, mTOR, BDNF). The authors make the case that the brain's extracellular matrix, and in particular the perineuronal nets that encase certain neurons and lock circuits into their adult configuration, is transiently loosened by psychedelics, reopening critical periods of plasticity that normally close in childhood.

**Why it matters:** It offers a physical answer to the puzzle of why a single dosing session can shift depression, PTSD or substance use for months — the drug may be briefly dissolving the structural cement that makes habits durable.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the image: psychedelics as a solvent for the mesh that keeps adult brains fixed. Open on the long-standing mystery of why psilocybin's benefits outlast the drug in the body by months. For addiction specifically, ask whether habit circuits need their nets loosened before new learning sticks — and what that implies about timing therapy to the window. Call corresponding author Xiaohui Wang (Changchun Institute of Applied Chemistry), plus an independent perineuronal net researcher.
- **Wider angle:** For a features desk: the critical-period reopening idea as a unifying theory of several very different interventions — psychedelics, ketamine, MDMA, even certain learning regimes — and the uncomfortable possibility that a brain with loosened nets is more suggestible as well as more plastic. That has real implications for how psychedelic therapy sessions are supervised.

**Caveats:** Review article, no new data; evidence predominantly from rodent work; the ECM mechanism has not been demonstrated directly in human brains; claims about addiction outcomes extrapolate from small clinical trials.

### 10. Heavy digital media use is tied to higher BMI, and comfort eating sits in the middle of the link

**Journal:** *Appetite* | **Published:** September 24, 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42785564 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.appet.2026.108795
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — identifies a specific psychological route rather than another bare correlation, but cross-sectional and the mediated share is small.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** A cross-sectional survey of 610 Turkish young adults (mean age 21.6) measured digital addiction, motives for eating palatable food, body mass index and adherence to a Mediterranean lifestyle. Higher digital addiction scores were associated with higher BMI and lower Mediterranean lifestyle adherence, and palatable eating motives partially mediated both — though the indirect path accounted for only a small fraction of the total effect, and more so for BMI than for lifestyle.

**Why it matters:** It moves the screens-and-weight conversation past "sitting still" toward reward-driven eating motives — eating for pleasure, coping or social reasons — as one modest mechanism, which is a more tractable target than screen time itself.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is that the harm may not be the sitting, it's what the scrolling does to why you eat. Open on the specific scene of eating while watching, surrounded by food advertising engineered for exactly that moment. Be clear that the mediated effect is small and the design cannot show direction. Call corresponding author Rukiye Melisa Atan at Bandırma Onyedi Eylül University.
- **Wider angle:** For a food or public-health outlet: the erosion of the Mediterranean diet among young adults in Mediterranean-adjacent countries, and what replaces it when meals migrate from the table to the phone. A strong reported piece with regional data rather than a mechanism story.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational — cannot establish causation or mediation direction; self-reported BMI and behavior; single-country student-age sample, may not generalize; "digital addiction" is a contested construct.

### 11. Gambling harm in Kazakhstan concentrates in middle-aged men, not the young

**Journal:** *The Journal of General Psychology* | **Published:** October–December 2026
**PMID:** 41887939 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00221309.2026.2647742
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — useful corrective to the "young men on apps" stereotype in a rapidly liberalizing gambling market; the intervention arm is poorly specified.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Two samples of Kazakh adults aged 18–65 — 782 assessed for gambling severity with the South Oaks Gambling Screen, and 148 enrolled in a rehabilitation program — were analyzed with regression alongside thematic analysis of semi-structured interviews. Men and 45- to 59-year-olds showed the highest severity; women and older participants the lowest. Low income and below-average education were both associated with greater dependency, and the authors report the rehabilitation program as highly effective.

**Why it matters:** Gambling expansion in Central Asia is outpacing research on it, and the age profile here cuts against the Western assumption that the at-risk group is young men with betting apps — but the effectiveness claim is reported without enough design detail to assess.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the demographic surprise: the people deepest in trouble were men in their fifties, not twenty-somethings, in a country where gambling has grown fast. Open on the social-protection angle — low income and low education compounding risk. Call Olga Tapalova at Abai Kazakh National Pedagogical University and press hard on what the rehabilitation program actually consisted of and whether it had a control group.
- **Wider angle:** For a global-affairs or investigative outlet: Central Asia as the gambling industry's next frontier, with regulation, casino-zone policy and online operators arriving faster than treatment services or research capacity.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional for the risk-factor component; self-reported screening instrument; rehabilitation effectiveness reported without described control condition or follow-up period in the abstract; single-country sample, may not generalize.

### 12. When one man gambles online, the whole extended family pays the debt — and swears an oath

**Journal:** *Journal of Gambling Studies* | **Published:** September 7, 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42704516 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10899-026-10552-w
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — vivid, genuinely novel cultural mechanisms in a setting where gambling is illegal; only nine interviewees, all gamblers.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Semi-structured interviews with nine married or formerly married men in Siirt, Türkiye, who currently or formerly gambled online were analyzed thematically. Four themes emerged: entry and the experience of addiction; disintegration of the nuclear family; household economic collapse and drift into crime; and stigma operating through kinship. Two mechanisms were distinctive to the setting — extended families collectively clearing a member's debts in exchange for religious oaths to stop, and stigma spreading through kin networks as a threat to collective family honor.

**Why it matters:** Almost all evidence on gambling's family harms comes from Western, individualist, legal-market settings; this shows the harm is routed through entirely different social machinery where gambling is illegal and kinship obligations are strong.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the oath — a family pooling money to clear a son's online betting debt, in exchange for a religious vow. It is a debt-relief and relapse-prevention system with no clinic in it, and it tells you something about where support actually comes from when the law offers none. Call Ahmet Bektaş at Gümüşhane University. Treat it as reporting on lived experience, not evidence of what works.
- **Wider angle:** For a long-form or international outlet: offshore betting sites in countries where gambling is banned — how the apps reach users, how money moves, and why prohibition tends to produce the harms without any of the harm-reduction infrastructure of regulated markets.

**Caveats:** Very small qualitative sample (N=9); all participants were gamblers, so family impacts are perceived rather than directly reported by affected relatives; single province; no quantitative outcomes; findings not generalizable.

### 13. Gambling, gaming, social media and porn use may run through the same chain of thought

**Journal:** *Addictive Behaviors* | **Published:** September 23, 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42801890 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.addbeh.2026.108869
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a decent-sized test of a shared cognitive pathway across four behaviors, undercut by a design flaw the authors concede.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** A community sample of 811 Iranian adults (43% male, mean age 27.7) completed self-report measures of early maladaptive schemas, desire thinking, craving and four problematic behaviors: gambling, gaming, social media use and pornography use. A serial model — schemas feeding desire thinking, which feeds craving, which feeds severity — fit acceptably, while a parallel model did not, and the serial indirect path held for all four behaviors. Critically, participants rated desire thinking and craving for a self-chosen activity that researchers did not record.

**Why it matters:** It supports the idea that deep-seated self-beliefs formed early fuel a specific mental habit — elaborating on anticipated pleasure — which then becomes craving across very different behaviors; but the unrecorded referent means the craving measures can't be matched to the behavior being predicted.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the shared engine: the same loop of rehearsing how good something will feel seems to sit behind gambling, gaming, scrolling and porn. Open on the moment before the behavior — the mental rehearsal, not the act. Call Mehdi Akbari at Kharazmi University, and Mark D. Griffiths at Nottingham Trent for the counterargument that lumping these together is premature. Disclose Griffiths's gambling industry funding and consultancy.
- **Wider angle:** For a psychology or health outlet: the diagnostic turf war over "behavioral addictions." Gambling disorder is in the manuals, gaming disorder partly so, social media and pornography are not — a piece on who decides, what evidence is required, and what happens to people seeking help for a condition that formally does not exist.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional — no temporal or causal ordering; all self-report; the target activity for desire thinking and craving measures was not recorded, limiting behavior-specific interpretation; single-country community sample, may not generalize; co-author Mark D. Griffiths reports research funding from Norsk Tipping and GambleAware and consultancy for gambling companies.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Self-control is not uniformly protective.** The new finding that highly self-controlled Chinese teenagers under punishment-related stress show *more* internet addiction ("Self-control protects teenagers from internet addiction — except when they are being punished", PMID 42727287) lines up with last month's backfire result that self-regulatory strategies tracked *higher* short-video addiction in low-inhibitory-control users ("Trying harder to self-regulate makes short-video addiction worse — for some people", PMID 42374874). Two independent samples, two different designs, same uncomfortable implication for screen-time advice.
- **Behavioral "addictions" are still being built out of cross-sectional self-report.** "Gambling, gaming, social media and porn use may run through the same chain of thought" (PMID 42801890) and "Heavy digital media use is tied to higher BMI, and comfort eating sits in the middle of the link" (PMID 42785564) repeat the pattern flagged last month in love addiction (PMID 42533520), AI addiction (PMID 42364395) and autistic traits/gaming (PMID 41742036) — contested constructs, assumed mediation direction, no temporal ordering.
- **Gambling research keeps moving out of the Western frame.** "Gambling harm in Kazakhstan concentrates in middle-aged men, not the young" (PMID 41887939) and "When one man gambles online, the whole extended family pays the debt — and swears an oath" (PMID 42704516) extend the geographic broadening seen last month in the Hong Kong card-pack audit (PMID 42611551) and the Spanish adolescent gambling-migration analysis (PMID 42599567).

**Counterpoints**

- **Emotion regulation demoted.** Last month, emotion-regulation difficulties were the explanatory engine in both "Exercise addiction and eating disorders look similar but come from different emotional places" (PMID 42504448) and "'Love addiction' is linked to distress and impulsivity — but only through a loss of emotional control" (PMID 42533520). This month's two-wave gambling study finds metacognitive beliefs outpredict emotion regulation ability, with the latter acting only as a buffer ("What gamblers believe about gambling predicts their trajectory better than how well they manage emotions", PMID 42143790).
- **The screens-cause-distress arrow gets weaker.** Previous entries treated internet use as an active ingredient — short-video use raising depressive symptoms via addiction in older adults (PMID 42374855), and reduced internet addiction predicting self-harm remission in boys (PMID 42633531). The new four-wave random-intercept analysis largely dissolves that within-person path ("Heavy phone use and depression travel together, but one may not be driving the other", PMID 42748880), and the authors concede the result flips with the model used.

**Advances**

- **Addiction neuroscience keeps leaving the reward circuit.** August gave dual-brain synchrony in abstinent heroin users (PMID 41941931) and a split of impulsivity into two near-non-overlapping connectomes (PMID 42562118); this month adds drug cues degrading basic somatosensory gating within milliseconds ("Seeing drug-related images breaks down basic sensory filtering in people who use methamphetamine", PMID 42740334). Three different tools, same message: the damage and the cue response are not confined to dopaminergic reward pathways.
- **Perinatal psychiatry thread continues.** "Women who smoke are twice as likely to develop postpartum depression" (PMID 42604887) is followed by the first large post-marketing sweep of zuranolone (PMID 42711976) — addiction-adjacent questions about GABAkine liability and breast milk exposure in the same patient group.

**New themes**

- **A treatment and biomarker pipeline appears.** The previous digest was almost entirely observational psychology and epidemiology. This batch brings semaglutide against three fentanyl relapse triggers (PMID 42228850), a plasma marker forecasting one-year executive function in methamphetamine use disorder (PMID 42379379), a neuroimmune-glutamate framework for relapse (PMID 42425280) and perineuronal net loosening as the durable mechanism behind psychedelics (PMID 41765343).
- **Methodology as the story.** The RI-CLPM deflation (PMID 42748880) and the network-analysis reframing of adolescent internet addiction (PMID 42727287) both make the analytic choice, rather than the finding, the newsworthy element.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Four studies in this batch point to addiction treatment being rebuilt out of materials borrowed from other fields — a weight-loss drug that shut down cue-, drug- and stress-induced fentanyl seeking in rats (PMID 42228850), a cell adhesion molecule already targeted by an approved cancer drug that forecasts cognitive recovery a year into methamphetamine treatment (PMID 42379379), an immunology-derived framework arguing that inflammasome signaling gates the glutamate changes behind relapse (PMID 42425280), and a structural account of psychedelics as transient solvents for the perineuronal nets that lock adult habits in place (PMID 41765343). Running alongside them is a cautionary case: the first two years of real-world reports on zuranolone, a first-in-class central nervous system drug, surfacing intrusive thoughts, "feeling drunk" and breast milk exposure that the label does not carry (PMID 42711976).

**Why pitch this now:** Anecdotal claims that GLP-1 drugs erase cravings for alcohol, nicotine and opioids have outrun the evidence by at least two years, and this is the moment when the actual preclinical and biomarker work is landing — along with a live example, in zuranolone, of what the first two years after approval look like when a novel psychoactive drug meets real patients.

**Angle:** A feature on addiction medicine's import trade: why the most promising candidate treatments for relapse were designed for obesity, cancer and inflammation, what it says about a field that has produced almost no new drugs of its own in decades, and what the post-marketing record of the last first-in-class psychiatric drug suggests about how the next wave will actually behave. For readers who have followed the GLP-1 story and want the pharmacology and the conflicts of interest rather than the testimonials.
