### 1. Low-dose lithium is linked to fewer dementia diagnoses than valproate in older adults

**Journal:** *Psychiatry Research* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 22 June 2026)
**PMID:** 42372694 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117300
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a big effect size on a question readers have heard about for years, but observational and the authors are admirably blunt about bias.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Using a "target trial emulation" of multicenter electronic health records in the TriNetX network, researchers compared adults aged 60+ who were taking lithium at genuinely low doses (all serum levels ≤0.40 mmol/L) with matched patients taking valproate, excluding anyone with prior dementia, mild cognitive impairment, bipolar disorder or Parkinson's. After 1:1 propensity matching on 23 covariates (853 per group), all-cause dementia over two years occurred in 3.1% of lithium users versus 8.3% of valproate users (HR 0.38). Mortality was also lower with lithium; hospitalization did not differ.

**Why it matters:** Lithium has been floated as a dementia-preventing drug for a decade on thin evidence; this is one of the first analyses to pit low doses against an active comparator rather than against no treatment, which removes some — not all — of the usual confounding. It strengthens the case for the randomized trials now under way rather than settling anything.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The oldest drug in psychiatry keeps refusing to go away. Open on the strangeness of a 19th-century salt, prescribed at doses so low they would be considered subtherapeutic for mood, showing up again in a dementia analysis — then walk readers through why an "active comparator" design matters and why the authors explicitly refuse to call it causal. Worth calling George Perry (University of Texas at San Antonio), a co-author and long-standing Alzheimer's pathology skeptic, plus an independent pharmacoepidemiologist to pick holes in the depletion-of-susceptibles problem.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a health-policy or pharmacy outlet on target trial emulation itself — how health-record "emulated trials" are being used to triage which old, off-patent drugs are worth the cost of a real RCT, and the growing worry that they generate more hypotheses than anyone can afford to test.

**Caveats:** Observational — cannot establish causation; surveillance and prescribing bias plausible (lithium users get monitored more); modest matched sample (853/group, only 25 dementia events in the lithium arm); diagnoses from clinical coding; 730-day primary follow-up is short for dementia.

### 2. People with bipolar disorder treated with lithium develop dementia less often than those on valproate

**Journal:** *Psychiatry Research* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 16 June 2026)
**PMID:** 42349060 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117284
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a larger, independent cohort pointing the same way as entry 1, which is what lifts it; still observational, and a near-simultaneous use of the same database.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** A retrospective cohort in the TriNetX US records network followed adults aged 45+ with bipolar disorder who started lithium or valproate for the first time between 2000 and 2025; after 1:1 propensity matching, 3056 remained per group. All-cause dementia occurred in 7.1% of lithium users versus 10.8% of valproate users (HR 0.69), with lower risks of unspecified dementia and suicide attempt, and no excess end-stage renal disease or mortality — the two harms clinicians most often cite when avoiding lithium.

**Why it matters:** People with bipolar disorder carry elevated dementia risk, and the two first-line mood stabilizers have rarely been compared head-to-head for cognitive outcomes. The kidney-safety null is arguably the more clinically consequential part.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Two independent teams, two different patient populations, same direction of travel within weeks of each other — lead on the convergence rather than on either paper alone. Open on a 50-something with bipolar disorder weighing a switch off lithium because of kidney fears, then show what the data do and don't say. Call Tsung-Wei Hsu's group (I-Shou University / Kaohsiung Medical University), and pair it with the lithium trialists in the UK for balance.
- **Wider angle:** For a specialist psychiatry or medicine outlet: valproate's reputational collapse. Between fetal-exposure restrictions and now cognitive-outcome signals in older adults, is there any population left for whom valproate is the obvious first choice?

**Caveats:** Observational — cannot establish causation; residual confounding by indication (lithium tends to go to more adherent, better-monitored patients); dementia ascertained from ICD coding; no serum-level verification of dose; overlapping data source with entry 1, so the two are not fully independent replications.

### 3. Depression predicts dementia better than insomnia does — and adding poor sleep tells you almost nothing extra

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 1 October 2026 (online 16 May 2026)
**PMID:** 42144138 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.121965
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — nationally representative, 12 years of follow-up, and it deflates a popular "bad sleep causes dementia" narrative; self-reported symptoms limit it.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Researchers followed 6226 dementia-free older Americans across 13 rounds of the National Health and Aging Trends Study (2011–2023), classifying time-varying symptoms as neither, depression only, insomnia only, or both. Co-occurring symptoms raised dementia hazard versus neither (HR 1.37) and versus insomnia alone (HR 1.25) — but were statistically indistinguishable from depression alone (HR 0.94). Above age 75, the depression-only group actually had a higher hazard than the group with both.

**Why it matters:** Insomnia has become the headline risk factor in public conversation about brain health, yet here the sleep component adds essentially nothing once mood is accounted for. For anyone building risk-stratification tools, that suggests asking about mood is the higher-yield question.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Everyone has read that bad sleep will give them dementia. This cohort says that if you are also low in mood, the sleep part barely moves the needle — and that after 75 the pattern inverts in a way nobody predicted. Open on the familiar 3 a.m. lying-awake scenario and the anxiety it now generates. Call Junxin Li at Johns Hopkins School of Nursing.
- **Wider angle:** A features piece for a general-interest outlet on "risk-factor inflation" in dementia prevention — how a long list of modifiable risks (hearing, sleep, loneliness, air pollution) becomes a moral burden on individuals when much of the variance is shared, correlated and not independently actionable.

**Caveats:** Observational — cannot establish causation; depressive and insomnia symptoms self-reported; incident dementia partly ascertained via proxy report and brief cognitive testing rather than clinical adjudication; reverse causation plausible, as prodromal dementia causes mood and sleep change.

### 4. Autistic adults report far worse memory and thinking than tests can detect

**Journal:** *Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews* | **Published:** 30 September 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42815817 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2026.107011
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — the first synthesis of cognitive aging in autistic adults, with a striking subjective–objective gap; weakened by cross-sectional source studies.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** A systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 15 cross-sectional studies comparing autistic adults (diagnosed or defined by validated trait measures) aged 50+ with neurotypical peers across 13 cognitive constructs. Autistic participants reported substantially greater subjective cognitive difficulty (d = 0.90) — a large effect — while objective performance differed only modestly, and only in processing speed, verbal fluency and working memory (d = −0.28 to −0.38). Most domains showed no difference at all.

**Why it matters:** Mid-life and older autistic adults are an almost invisible population in aging research, and the headline finding is reassuring on cognitive decline while raising an awkward clinical question: if autistic people routinely report memory problems that tests do not confirm, how should memory clinics interpret those reports?

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The first generation of adults diagnosed with autism in childhood is now reaching old age, and nobody knows what their brains do next. Lead on the mismatch — people describing serious cognitive struggle that standardized testing shrugs off — and the real possibility that the tests, not the people, are measuring the wrong thing (masking, exhaustion, executive load). Call Lucy Gray and Joshua Stott at UCL.
- **Wider angle:** For a disability or health-services outlet: the coming "autistic aging" gap — care homes, memory clinics and dementia services built around neurotypical norms, and what happens to people who were never diagnosed in the first place.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of cross-sectional studies only — no longitudinal data, so nothing can be said about rate of decline; participants over 65 underrepresented; high heterogeneity across analyses; many samples defined by trait questionnaires rather than diagnosis; subjective outcomes self-reported by definition.

### 5. Loneliness after a stroke is linked to a sharply higher risk of later dementia

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 1 October 2026 (online 11 May 2026)
**PMID:** 42119905 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.121949
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — huge cohort and a clean separation of loneliness from isolation, but UK Biobank's healthy-volunteer skew and self-report limit it.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Among 394,308 UK Biobank participants — 7267 of whom had already had a stroke — researchers tracked mortality, cardiovascular events, dementia and depression over a median 10.2 years. In stroke survivors, feeling lonely was associated with a 67% higher hazard of all-cause dementia (HR 1.67), while objectively being socially isolated was not; isolation instead predicted mortality and cardiovascular death. The excess risks were significantly larger in stroke survivors than in people without stroke, and health behaviors mediated 12–55% of the associations.

**Why it matters:** Loneliness and isolation are routinely treated as the same variable; here they split apart, with the subjective feeling — not the objective headcount of contacts — carrying the dementia signal in an already-vulnerable group.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** You can be surrounded by people and still be at risk; you can live alone and not be. Open on a stroke survivor back home with carers in and out of the house all day who still describes herself as lonely, and use the paper to pull apart two things public health keeps conflating. Call Yan-Yan Liang at Guangzhou Medical University's Center for Sleep and Circadian Medicine.
- **Wider angle:** For a health-services or social-policy outlet: post-stroke rehabilitation currently measures walking, speech and swallowing. Should it measure loneliness, and what would an intervention even look like given that befriending trials have a patchy record?

**Caveats:** Observational — cannot establish causation; loneliness and isolation self-reported at a single baseline; 95.4% White and healthier than the general population, so may not generalize; dementia cases from linked hospital and death records; reverse causation possible if early cognitive change drives withdrawal.

### 6. People with dementia who have sleep problems are at higher risk of suicide — and depression doesn't explain it

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 25 September 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42790647 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122562
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — nationwide data, large N, and a formal mediation analysis that returns a null everyone assumed would be positive; effect size modest.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Linking Taiwan's National Health Insurance database to the national Death Registry, researchers followed 85,920 people aged 50+ newly diagnosed with dementia between 2012 and 2019, of whom 19,982 had documented sleep disturbances, recording 2325 suicide attempts or deaths through 2023. Sleep disturbance was associated with a 12% higher adjusted suicide risk (868 vs 730 per 100,000 person-years). Crucially, mediation analysis found no significant indirect effect through depression — the usual explanation.

**Why it matters:** Suicide risk in dementia is underdiscussed and under-screened, and this suggests disturbed sleep is not simply a marker of underlying depression but carries information of its own, pointing at mechanisms such as impulsivity or circadian disruption.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The assumption has always been that a person with dementia who stops sleeping and then becomes suicidal is depressed. This cohort says the depression link doesn't carry the effect. Frame around what families and clinicians actually ask about at appointments, and the three-visits-to-the-emergency-department signal (aHR 2.54) that is hiding in the same dataset. Call Yu-Pei Lin at An Nan Hospital, China Medical University.
- **Wider angle:** For a specialist palliative-care or geriatrics outlet: how rarely suicide risk is assessed after a dementia diagnosis, and how assisted-dying debates intersect with the period just after diagnosis when insight is still intact.

**Caveats:** Observational — cannot establish causation; sleep disturbance inferred from diagnostic codes and prescriptions, so misclassification likely and treated cases may differ systematically from untreated; depression also coded administratively, which weakens the mediation null; single-country health system may not generalize; absolute risk increase is small.

### 7. Nearly a third of people newly diagnosed with dementia in German family practices get an antipsychotic first

**Journal:** *Psychiatrische Praxis* | **Published:** 22 September 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42772309 | **DOI:** 10.1055/a-2939-0340
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — nearly 150,000 patients and a genuinely uncomfortable number, though it is a prescribing-pattern study rather than an outcome study.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Using the IQVIA Disease Analyzer database covering 1179 general and 214 specialist practices in Germany (2005–2024), the authors examined what was prescribed in the first year after a first dementia diagnosis in patients aged 65+ with no prior prescriptions. In general practices (n = 94,036), 27.3% received antipsychotics only and just 8.7% received anti-dementia drugs only, with 54.2% receiving neither. In specialist practices (n = 55,341), only 22.2% went untreated and anti-dementia drugs were started first nearly three times as often.

**Story angles:**

Call Jens Bohlken at the University of Leipzig's ISAP institute.

### 8. A hidden background signal in the brain's electrical noise predicts who develops Parkinson's or Lewy body dementia

**Journal:** *Sleep* | **Published:** 16 September 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42747400 | **DOI:** 10.1093/sleep/zsag248
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a real prognostic signal from a cheap, widely available test, in a well-characterized high-risk cohort; only 26 converters.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Resting EEG was recorded in 134 people with isolated REM sleep behavior disorder — who act out dreams and are at very high risk of later synucleinopathy — and 51 healthy controls; 26 converted to Parkinson's disease or dementia with Lewy bodies during follow-up. By separating rhythmic oscillations from the non-rhythmic "aperiodic" background using FOOOF parameterization, the team showed both posterior 4–8 Hz periodic power (HR 1.34) and aperiodic offset (HR 7.46) independently predicted conversion. Among converters, cognitive scores tracked the aperiodic slope, while motor scores tracked the offset.

**Why it matters:** The aperiodic component of EEG was long treated as noise to be discarded. Here it carries the strongest prognostic signal in the dataset — and it comes from a test any sleep lab already runs.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** For decades the flat, fractal background of the EEG was thrown away as junk; it turns out to predict which dream-enacting sleepers go on to develop dementia with Lewy bodies. Open on an RBD clinic, where patients already know they are in a waiting room for neurodegeneration and desperately want to know when. Call Ki-Young Jung at Seoul National University Hospital.
- **Wider angle:** For a neuroscience-literate outlet: the aperiodic revolution. A methods shift (FOOOF and its successors) is quietly rewriting a century of band-power findings across psychiatry, aging and ADHD — how many published "alpha" and "theta" results were actually measuring the background?

**Caveats:** Modest number of converters (26) limits precision of hazard estimates; single-center cohort; follow-up duration not fully specified; cognitive correlations are cross-sectional within converters; needs independent replication before any clinical use.

### 9. Motor symptoms outrun thinking problems in people destined for Parkinson's — and the gap shows up years early

**Journal:** *Brain* | **Published:** 5 October 2026
**PMID:** 42213896 | **DOI:** 10.1093/brain/awag193
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a 766-patient longitudinal cohort with a blind validation set and a personalized progression model; the output is a trial tool rather than a lifestyle finding.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** The FARPRESTO consortium trained a Disease Course Mapping model on cognitive and motor scores from 766 people with isolated REM sleep behavior disorder, extracting three individual parameters — how early, how fast, and whether motor or cognitive decline comes first — then tested it blind in 49 patients who also had dopaminergic imaging and high-density EEG. Motor scores progressed 35% faster than cognitive scores overall; the parameters distinguished converters from non-converters 73% of the time, and those who went on to develop parkinsonism declined earlier than those who developed dementia. Earlier progression correlated with presynaptic dopaminergic loss and with higher theta-band phase synchronization.

**Why it matters:** It turns a heterogeneous, decades-long prodrome into three numbers per person, which is exactly what disease-modifying trials need as end points — and it suggests the motor-first/cognition-first split is detectable before either syndrome declares itself.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Can you tell, years in advance, whether someone will get Parkinson's or dementia? This model says it is partly written into how fast their motor scores drift relative to their cognitive ones. Open with a patient in an RBD clinic doing a routine pegboard test that turns out to carry prognostic weight. Call Dario Arnaldi at the University of Genoa, or Ronald Postuma at McGill for an outside view.
- **Wider angle:** For a drug-development or science-policy outlet: the end-point problem. Why neurodegeneration trials keep failing partly because they measure the wrong thing at the wrong time, and how "disease course mapping" borrowed from Alzheimer's research is spreading across the field.

**Caveats:** Blind test set is small (n = 49) and heavily male; 73% discrimination is useful but not clinically decisive; model trained and tested within largely Italian specialist sleep centers, so may not generalize; biomarker correlations are cross-sectional at baseline.

### 10. Tiny white-matter lesions damage the brain's acetylcholine supply lines — and that's what drags cognition down in Parkinson's

**Journal:** *Brain* | **Published:** 5 October 2026
**PMID:** 42117428 | **DOI:** 10.1093/brain/awag172
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — rare combination of cholinergic PET, lesion mapping and mediation analysis in 127 patients; mediation on cross-sectional data is the weak point.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** In 127 people with mid-to-advanced Parkinson's disease but no dementia, researchers combined quantified white-matter lesion burden (FLAIR MRI), basal forebrain volumes and ¹⁸F-FEOBV PET, which maps cholinergic nerve terminals. Lesions around the ventricles — not deep lesions — were associated with reduced cholinergic terminal density in an insular–limbic–frontal–cingulum cluster, and affected key Ch4 projection pathways in 81–88% of patients. Mediation analysis indicated the lesions hit cortical cholinergic terminals directly, plus an indirect route via basal forebrain shrinkage, and that this cholinergic damage statistically carried the link between lesion burden and memory and executive performance.

**Why it matters:** It supplies a concrete mechanism linking "small vessel disease" — the incidental white blobs routinely seen on scans of older people — to cognitive decline, and it means blood-pressure and vascular risk management could plausibly protect a neurotransmitter system, not just blood vessels.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Those white specks your scan report calls "age-related changes" may be cutting cables. Lead on the image: long cholinergic axons running from the base of the brain to the cortex, severed where lesions cluster around the ventricles. Open on a patient told their scan showed "nothing to worry about". Call Nicolaas Bohnen at the University of Michigan.
- **Wider angle:** For a cardiology or general medicine outlet: the brain as a vascular organ. A piece on how midlife hypertension control is being reframed as cognitive medicine, and the awkward fact that white-matter hyperintensities are reported to patients with almost no guidance on what to do about them.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional — mediation analysis cannot establish a causal sequence; single-center; Parkinson's patients without dementia, so findings may not transfer to typical aging or Alzheimer's; basal forebrain volume is an MRI proxy, not a direct cell count.

### 11. Genetic sleuthing shows many "sporadic" cases of frontotemporal dementia are actually inherited

**Journal:** *Brain* | **Published:** 25 September 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42789481 | **DOI:** 10.1093/brain/awag326
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a clean, surprising number from 3524 genomes with direct implications for genetic counseling; the technical framing makes it harder to tell simply.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Researchers ran genome-wide identity-by-descent analysis across 3524 people of European ancestry on the ALS–frontotemporal dementia spectrum, from Australia, New Zealand and the UK. They found 793 pairs of first- to sixth-degree relatives, 31.5% of them previously unrecognized; 15.9% of people classified as having "sporadic" FTD and 5.5% of sporadic ALS turned out to be genetically related to another affected person in the cohort. Reconstructing 73 new pedigrees from apparently unrelated patients also revealed cross-continent founder networks for SOD1 and C9orf72, and narrowed a candidate locus on chromosome 13q21.33.

**Why it matters:** "Sporadic" has always been a diagnosis of ignorance — it means no known affected relatives, not no inheritance. Quantifying how often hidden relatedness lurks behind that label changes how families should be counseled and makes previously underpowered pedigrees usable for gene discovery.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Two patients in different countries, told their disease was random bad luck, turn out to be second cousins. Open on exactly that and use it to explain reduced penetrance — why a family can carry a dementia gene for generations without anyone noticing. Call Kelly Williams or Ian Blair at Macquarie University Brain Institute.
- **Wider angle:** For a genetics or bioethics outlet: consumer DNA databases and research cohorts are now capable of telling people things about inherited neurodegenerative risk that nobody consented to learn, including about relatives they have never met. Who gets told what?

**Caveats:** European-ancestry cohort only — findings may not generalize to other populations; relatedness within a recruited research cohort may overstate rates in the wider patient population; the new 13q21.33 candidate locus is based on a single extended pedigree and remains unconfirmed; clinical classification of "sporadic" varies between contributing centers.

### 12. Sleeping light instead of deep is linked to early damage in the brain's small blood vessels

**Journal:** *Journal of Sleep Research* | **Published:** 23 September 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42779207 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jsr.70448
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — home sleep monitoring plus sensitive diffusion imaging in a population cohort is a neat combination, but cross-sectional and N = 154.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** In 154 participants of the population-based Hamburg City Health Study (mean age 63, 42% women), researchers combined portable WatchPAT home sleep diagnostics with brain MRI measures sensitive to very early microstructural change. A sleep profile weighted toward light sleep relative to deep sleep was associated with higher peak-width of skeletonized mean diffusivity — an early marker of small vessel disease — and with thinner cortex. **Why it matters:** It suggests the brain consequences of shallow sleep may be visible before anything shows up on a standard clinical scan — useful for designing prevention trials, though it cannot say whether poor sleep damages vessels or damaged vessels fragment sleep.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The damage may be happening before the scan can see it. Lead on PSMD, the diffusion measure that picks up microvascular change while the conventional read-out still says "normal", and on the fact that deep sleep — not REM, the glamorous stage — carried the signal. Open on someone in their sixties wearing a finger-worn sleep monitor at home for a week. Call Bastian Cheng at University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf.
- **Wider angle:** For a consumer-tech or health outlet: your smartwatch reports "deep sleep" every morning. A piece on what these devices actually measure versus polysomnography, and whether research-grade home monitors like WatchPAT are about to make population sleep–brain studies routine.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional — cannot establish causation, and reverse causality is explicitly acknowledged by the authors; small N (154); single city cohort; sleep staging derived from a peripheral-arterial-tone device rather than full polysomnography; multiple imaging outcomes tested with several nulls.

### 13. Four in ten older people with epilepsy struggle with everyday tasks — and one in six at a level seen in dementia

**PMID:** 42704320 | **DOI:** 10.1002/epi.70478

### 14. Hearing loss makes people feel their memory is failing — but tests suggest something else is going on

**Journal:** *Scandinavian Journal of Psychology* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 13 May 2026)
**PMID:** 42126868 | **DOI:** 10.1111/sjop.70115
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a useful deflation of a much-hyped link, but small and cross-sectional, and essentially a partially null result.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Researchers at the Danish Dementia Research Centre assessed 143 adults aged 55+ (60 community-dwelling, 83 audiology clinic patients) with pure-tone audiometry, two subjective cognitive complaint scales, a depression scale and tests of episodic memory and processing speed. People with moderate-to-severe hearing loss reported more cognitive complaints and performed worse on memory and processing speed. But once age, sex, depressive symptoms and objective test scores were entered into regression models, hearing level no longer had an independent effect on subjective complaints.

**Why it matters:** Hearing loss is currently the single largest modifiable risk factor in the Lancet Commission's dementia list, and "I can't remember" complaints from people who are hard of hearing are a common clinic presentation. This suggests the complaint is substantially carried by mood and genuine cognitive performance rather than by deafness itself.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** If you cannot hear what was said, you cannot remember it — and you may conclude your memory is going. Open in an audiology waiting room with a patient convinced they are developing dementia, then unpack what disappears when depression is accounted for. Call Asmus Vogel at Rigshospitalet's Danish Dementia Research Centre.
- **Wider angle:** For a health or consumer outlet: the hearing-aid-and-dementia question after ACHIEVE. Do the devices protect cognition, who benefits, and how much of the public messaging has outrun the evidence?

**Caveats:** Small N (143) and mixed recruitment from two very different sources; cross-sectional — cannot establish causation; primary outcomes self-reported; pure-tone audiometry measures tone detection, not speech-in-noise ability, which may be the more cognitively relevant measure; correlations are weak (rho ≈ 0.21–0.25).

**The study:** A retrospective analysis of electronic records from 284 people with advanced dementia enrolled in a home-based palliative care service in Singapore examined "final place of care" — where care happened in the last three days of life — rather than the usual metric of place of death. ## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- **Mood keeps absorbing the variance assigned to other risk factors.** "Depression predicts dementia better than insomnia does" (PMID 42144138) and "Hearing loss makes people feel their memory is failing — but tests suggest something else is going on" (PMID 42126868) both show a headline exposure losing its independent effect once depressive symptoms are in the model. That extends last month's within-person finding that a bad mood week knocks about a point off an MCI patient's cognitive score (PMID 40859820): depression is not just a comorbidity in this literature, it is a persistent confounder of how other risks are estimated.
- **Iatrogenic harm remains a live seam.** "Nearly a third of people newly diagnosed with dementia in German family practices get an antipsychotic first" (PMID 42772309) sits directly alongside the previous digest's "Most cases of hypersexuality in neurological illness are caused by the treatment" (PMID 42330690) — two different routes to the same uncomfortable point, that a sizeable share of the behavioral burden in dementia and Parkinson's is prescription-shaped.
- **Caregiver biology, continued.** "A biological marker in caregivers' blood predicts depression months later" (PMID 42804652) keeps the relational-physiology thread running from last month's heart rate synchrony study (PMID 42398884) and the caregiver-mortality reappraisal (PMID 42721295), all three in the same small cluster of journals.

**Advances**
- **Lithium moves from advocacy to data.** Last month's "The case for reviving lithium — at a fraction of the psychiatric dose" (PMID 42268602) was a narrative review arguing for a trial that has not run. This month two active-comparator emulated trials in the same EHR network report in the same direction: low-dose lithium versus valproate in over-60s (PMID 42372694, HR 0.38) and lithium versus valproate in bipolar disorder (PMID 42349060, HR 0.69), the latter also returning a kidney-safety null. The "mechanism-rich, trial-poor" pattern logged in topic memory has not broken — but the observational evidence is now comparator-controlled rather than versus no treatment.
- **TriNetX as the field's default instrument.** The COMISA study (PMID 42640940) used it last month; both lithium papers use it this month. Worth flagging as a methods story in its own right, since entries 1 and 2 are not independent replications.
- **Epilepsy and cognitive aging moves from pathology to function.** Previous entries addressed hemisphere-asymmetric tau in historical brains (PMID 42715912) and the dissolution of the epilepsy–depression association under competing-risk adjustment (PMID 42155275). "Four in ten older people with epilepsy struggle with everyday tasks" (PMID 42704320) now puts an everyday-function number on the same population.
- **Neurodivergent aging broadens beyond intellectual disability.** The previous batch established burden and measurement failure in intellectual disability (PMIDs 42623159, 42609160, 42659675). This batch adds the first synthesis of cognitive aging in autistic adults (PMID 42815817) and the diagnostic-communication problem in Down syndrome (PMID 42725843) — same structural gap, two new populations, and in both cases the issue is whether the instruments and the conversations are fit for purpose.
- **Small vessel disease gains a neurotransmitter mechanism.** "Tiny white-matter lesions damage the brain's acetylcholine supply lines" (PMID 42117428) and "Sleeping light instead of deep is linked to early damage in the brain's small blood vessels" (PMID 42779207) advance the vascular-to-cognition chain sketched last month in the CADASIL mouse and postmortem work (PMID 41622715), this time in living humans with imaging rather than in a rare genetic model.

**Counterpoints**
- **Insomnia's standing as a dementia risk factor takes a knock.** "Depression predicts dementia better than insomnia does — and adding poor sleep tells you almost nothing extra" (PMID 42144138) cuts against the framing of last month's COMISA study, where apnea-plus-insomnia carried a 34% excess dementia hazard (PMID 42640940). Both flag reverse causation; neither resolves it, but the NHATS result suggests the insomnia component may be doing less work than the sleep-medicine literature implies.
- **Depression as universal explanation fails a formal test.** "People with dementia who have sleep problems are at higher risk of suicide — and depression doesn't explain it" (PMID 42790647) returns a mediation null where the previous digest's mood-cognition work (PMID 40859820) would predict depression to be the connecting variable.

**New themes**
- The isolated REM sleep behavior disorder prodrome as a prediction testbed: aperiodic EEG signal (PMID 42747400) and personalized disease course mapping (PMID 42213896) — nothing comparable in the previous batch.
- Hidden heritability behind "sporadic" diagnosis: identity-by-descent analysis in FTD/ALS (PMID 42789481).
- End-of-life and access inequity as measurable service questions: African Caribbean help-seeking (PMID 40939289) and final place of care in Singapore (PMID 40913758).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch each take a named "modifiable risk factor" for dementia and find that what matters is the subjective state rather than the objective exposure — or that the risk shrinks once mood is in the model. Insomnia adds essentially nothing to dementia prediction once depression is accounted for (PMID 42144138); after a stroke, feeling lonely predicts dementia while being socially isolated does not (PMID 42119905); hearing level loses its independent link to cognitive complaints once depressive symptoms and actual test scores are included (PMID 42126868). Running the other way, "Autistic adults report far worse memory and thinking than tests can detect" (PMID 42815817) shows the mismatch in reverse, with a large subjective deficit and only modest objective one.

**Why pitch this now:** The public prevention message is built on a checklist of discrete, independently actionable risks — hearing, sleep, social contact — and four studies published within weeks suggest those items are heavily correlated, partly interchangeable with mood, and not separable in the way the checklist implies.

**Angle:** A reported feature on what the dementia risk-factor list is actually measuring, following one or two of these risks from epidemiological estimate to the clinic room to the health-promotion poster, and asking researchers what survives when you adjust for how people feel. For a general readership that has been told to buy hearing aids, fix their sleep and see more friends, and is quietly keeping score.

**Potential headlines:**
- Three dementia risk factors that shrink when you measure them more carefully
- Loneliness counts, having people around doesn't — and sleep barely registers next to mood
- The dementia prevention checklist is asking you to fix things it can't tell apart

**Potential outlets:**
- **New Scientist Features** — Mind is already running the individual studies; a features slot can carry the cross-study reordering and the measurement argument that no single item supports.
- **The Guardian science desk** — the Lancet Commission framing is British, widely promoted in UK public health messaging, and the feature is in effect an evidence audit of it.
- **The Atlantic** — strong fit for the moral-burden strand: health advice that converts shared, correlated risk into personal responsibility.
- **Undark** — would take seriously the methods spine here, that adjusting for depression repeatedly dissolves effects the field has been promoting as independent.
