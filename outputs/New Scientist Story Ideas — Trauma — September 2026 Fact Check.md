# Fact-Check Report — New Scientist Mind digest, run date 2026-09-04

### Study 1: Common mental disorders are lasting longer than they did a decade ago
**PMID:** 42687372 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Core numbers check out: N = 6,194 (18–75, interviewed 2019–2022 and 2023–2024), NEMESIS-2 N = 6,646, broad persistence 42.7% → 57.9%, and the four significant determinants.

**Issue A. Predictors presented as ranked** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Note that childhood trauma was among the strongest predictors of a disorder that refuses to lift."
- **Abstract says:** "Higher broad persistence was significantly associated with younger age, living in a city, childhood trauma, and near-daily drug use in multivariable analyses."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports four significant associations without ranking their magnitude. "Among the strongest" is not supported.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that childhood trauma was one of four factors significantly associated with greater persistence."

**Issue B. Cohort-comparison description** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "comparison across two cohorts uses partly different DSM criteria"
- **Abstract says:** "Twelve-year trends were examined by comparing DSM-IV persistence in NEMESIS-3 with NEMESIS-2 (N = 6,646; interviewed 2007-2009 and 2010-2012) among adults aged 18-64 years."
- **Problem:** The 12-year comparison was deliberately restricted to DSM-IV diagnoses in both cohorts, so the caveat misdescribes the design. The real restriction the digest omits is that the trend comparison covers ages 18–64 only, not the full 18–75 sample.
- **Suggested fix:** "the 12-year trend comparison uses DSM-IV criteria in both cohorts and is restricted to adults aged 18–64"

**Issue C. Incidence-versus-persistence framing** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The widely reported rise in mental ill health may be less about more people falling ill and more about fewer people getting better"
- **Abstract says:** "Recent increases in common mental disorder prevalence could partly reflect greater persistence of existing disorders."
- **Problem:** The study measured persistence only; it did not compare incidence trends, so it cannot apportion the rise between the two. The abstract's "could partly reflect" is weaker than the digest's either/or tilt.
- **Suggested fix:** "The widely reported rise in mental ill health may partly reflect existing disorders lasting longer, not just more people falling ill"

### Study 2: Mass shooting survivors describe time slowing down as they ran
**PMID:** 42647049 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 42, 74% women, mean age 42.1, within one month, four themes, subjective slowing and sequence gaps — all accurate.

**Issue A. Claims about what dominates encoding** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "it suggests memory reorganizes around survival action rather than around the timeline" and "a combination that maps onto how spatial escape planning appears to dominate encoding"
- **Abstract says:** "Survivors recalled vivid sensory impressions, detailed spatial awareness organized around escape, and temporal distortions, including both subjective slowing and sequence gaps."
- **Problem:** A codebook thematic analysis of 42 narratives with no comparison group cannot establish that one form of content dominates another, or that memory is "reorganized" relative to some baseline. These are themes identified in prompted written accounts, not measured relative weights.
- **Suggested fix:** "it is one of the very few structured looks at what people describe encoding during a real mass-casualty event, and escape-related spatial detail sat alongside distorted time in survivors' accounts"

**Issue B. "Consistently"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "survivors consistently described the shooting unfolding in slow motion while whole chunks of sequence vanished"
- **Abstract says:** "temporal distortions, including both subjective slowing and sequence gaps" were part of the contextual-memory theme.
- **Problem:** Thematic prevalence isn't quantified in the abstract; "consistently" implies near-universal endorsement.
- **Suggested fix:** "survivors described the shooting unfolding in slow motion alongside gaps in the sequence of events"

**Issue C. NS fit score** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "NS fit:** 7/10"
- **Problem:** The evidence base is 42 prompted, written self-reports, predominantly female, single event, no comparison group, and purely qualitative. The rarity of the sample is genuine, but a 7 overstates the evidential weight for a Mind feature built on interpretive claims about encoding.
- **Suggested fix:** "NS fit:** 6/10 — rare, ethically difficult data on what survivors report from an extreme event; qualitative, prompted self-report with no comparison group."

### Study 3: Judges deciding sexual abuse cases still rely on cues that science has discredited
**PMID:** 41308177 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

All percentages from both studies match the abstract exactly, including the 13% relying on all three content criteria.

**Issue A. "Discredited" overstates the abstract's language** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "cues that science has discredited" / "Decades of memory research show that demeanor, displayed emotion, and apparent trauma are poor guides to whether an account is truthful"
- **Abstract says:** "Highly contested criteria (i.e., emotion, trauma, nonverbal behaviour, authentic impression) are also regularly used."
- **Problem:** The authors describe these criteria as "highly contested," not discredited, and the abstract does not characterize the state of the evidence base. The digest asserts settled consensus.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Judges deciding sexual abuse cases still rely on contested credibility cues"; body: "The criteria the authors call highly contested — demeanour, displayed emotion, apparent trauma — are turning up in real verdicts."

**Issue B. Published erratum not noted** — Severity: Minor

- **Abstract says:** "Erratum in Memory. 2026 May;34(5):608. doi: 10.1080/09658211.2026.2643966."
- **Problem:** The digest cites percentages from an article that carries a subsequent erratum; the digest does not flag this. Whether the erratum touches any reported figure is unverifiable from the abstract alone.
- **Suggested fix:** Add to Caveats: "A published erratum (Memory 2026;34(5):608) exists — check the correction before quoting figures."

### Study 4: Psychiatric diagnoses barely predict how patients actually fare
**PMID:** 42644771 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 685 outpatients, the seven dimensions, R² = 0.08 for pain, ΔR² values (0.55, 0.41, 0.38), somatization as most robust predictor, and the reversed-order trauma-diagnosis result all match. The Blais royalty conflict is correctly carried into Caveats.

**Issue A. "Essentially none of the variance"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "your diagnosis explained essentially none of the variance in whether you were suicidal, functioning, or satisfied with life"
- **Abstract says:** "diagnostic categories significantly predicted pain (R2 = 0.08) but did not predict the other four outcome measures."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports non-significance, not effect sizes near zero, for those four outcomes. The actual R² values are unverifiable from the abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "your diagnosis did not significantly predict whether you were suicidal, functioning, or satisfied with life"

**Issue B. SPECTRA identified as the source of the dimensions** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "co-author Mark Blais receives royalties on the SPECTRA measure used to derive dimensions"
- **Abstract says:** dimensions were "derived from commonly used clinical assessment measures"; the conflict statement says Blais "is an author of the SPECTRA: Indices of Psychopathology, and he receives royalties on sales of the SPECTRA."
- **Problem:** The abstract never states that SPECTRA was among the measures used. The royalty interest is real and correctly disclosed, but the link to this study's dimensions is unverifiable from the abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "co-author Mark Blais receives royalties on the SPECTRA: Indices of Psychopathology (whether it was among the measures used here is not stated in the abstract)"

**Issue C. Novelty tag** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Novelty:** Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** "research examining the incremental validity of transdiagnostic dimensions over diagnostic categories remains limited."
- **Problem:** The authors position this as filling a gap and supporting dimensional models, not as contradicting an established prior finding.
- **Suggested fix:** "Novelty:** Fills an evidence gap / supports an existing challenge to categorical diagnosis"

### Study 5: Depression may come first and drive complex PTSD, not the other way around
**PMID:** 42594693 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 250, four waves over two years, RI-CLPM, stronger association with DSO than PTSD, DSO → depression from T2 to T4 — all accurate, and the population definition matches the abstract closely.

**Issue A. Headline asserts a one-way relationship the study contradicts** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Depression may come first and drive complex PTSD, not the other way around"
- **Abstract says:** "Depressive symptoms were significantly longitudinally associated with both PTSD and DSO symptoms... Additionally, DSO symptoms were also significantly longitudinally associated with depressive symptoms from T2 to T4."
- **Problem:** The study found associations in both directions, which the digest's own body text correctly calls "a self-sustaining loop rather than a one-way street." The headline's "not the other way around" contradicts both the abstract and the digest body, and "drive" imports causal language into a within-person observational model.
- **Suggested fix:** "Depression may help set off complex PTSD — and then be fed by it"

### Study 6: Veterans who broke their own moral code report more feelings of unreality
**PMID:** 41347711 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 189 Israeli male combat veterans, cross-sectional, self-report, PTSD symptoms mediating PMIE exposure → dissociation, and the commission-of-transgressions facet association all match.

**Issue A. The "asymmetry" hook is not in the abstract** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "one specific facet — having committed moral transgressions oneself, as opposed to witnessing or being betrayed — was independently associated with depersonalization and derealization" / "The surprise is the asymmetry: what a soldier did, not what was done to them, tracked with feeling unreal."
- **Abstract says:** "one of the facets of exposure to PMIEs, the commission of moral transgressions, was significantly associated with depersonalization/derealization symptoms."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports one facet as significantly associated; it does not report that the betrayal or witnessing facets were non-significant. The contrast that carries the whole pitch is unverifiable from the abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "one facet — having committed moral transgressions oneself — was significantly associated with depersonalization and derealization (whether the betrayal and witnessing facets were non-significant is not reported in the abstract; confirm from the full text before pitching the contrast)"

**Issue B. "Independently"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "was independently associated with depersonalization and derealization"
- **Abstract says:** "was significantly associated with depersonalization/derealization symptoms"
- **Problem:** "Independently" implies adjustment for the other PMIE facets, which the abstract does not state.
- **Suggested fix:** "was significantly associated with depersonalization and derealization"

### Study 7: Whether you "have PTSD" can depend on which scoring rule your clinician uses
**PMID:** 42635035 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 248, mean age 36.5, 79% female, CAPS-5 plus PCL-5, good internal consistency/test-retest/convergent validity with limited specificity, 18–38% classification range, thresholds 31–33 — all accurate.

**Issue A. Screening classification described as diagnosis** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "a diagnosis rate that swings from 18% to 38% based on the scoring model" and headline "Whether you 'have PTSD' can depend on which scoring rule your clinician uses"
- **Abstract says:** "Model-derived probable PTSD classification rates varied substantially (18-38%) across symptom-cluster algorithms," and concludes the PCL-5 "should not be used as a stand-alone diagnostic tool."
- **Problem:** The swinging figure is a self-report screening classification rate, not a diagnosis rate, and no clinician assigns a PTSD diagnosis by choosing a PCL-5 cluster algorithm. As written, a reader could conclude their diagnosis is an artefact of a scoring choice.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Whether a questionnaire flags you as probably having PTSD depends on how the symptoms are grouped." Body: "a probable-PTSD screening rate that swings from 18% to 38% based on the scoring model — the authors stress the PCL-5 should not be used as a stand-alone diagnostic tool."

### Study 8: Avoidance may be the weakest sign of PTSD in refugees
**PMID:** 42635367 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 267, 61% male, mean age 25.2, CFA/SEM, the three clusters as stronger indicators than avoidance, and the non-significant resilience–avoidance association all match.

**Issue A. Hyperarousal singled out as the dominant cluster** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "while hyperarousal — plausibly a rational response to continuing insecurity about status, housing, and family — dominated"
- **Abstract says:** Results: "Hyperarousal, negative alterations in cognition and mood (NACM), and re-experiencing symptom clusters were stronger indicators of PTSD, compared to avoidance." Plain Language Summary: "Negative alterations in cognition and mood, hyperarousal, and re-experiencing symptoms were respectively stronger indicators of PTSD than avoidance."
- **Problem:** The abstract's two orderings differ, and the plain-language summary places NACM first. The abstract supports "hyperarousal is an important cluster," not that it dominated the others.
- **Suggested fix:** "while hyperarousal and negative alterations in cognition and mood — plausibly reflecting continuing insecurity about status, housing, and family — carried far more weight"

**Issue B. Headline generalizes to refugees as a group** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Avoidance may be the weakest sign of PTSD in refugees"
- **Abstract says:** "267 (61% male) young adults (Mage = 25.21) with a refugee background who had experienced trauma," in Sweden; "Further research is warranted to replicate and extend these results."
- **Problem:** The finding comes from young adults with a refugee background in one host country; the headline reads as a property of refugees generally.
- **Suggested fix:** "Avoidance may be the weakest sign of PTSD in young refugees resettled in Sweden"

### Study 9: Childhood maltreatment predicts depression severity — inflammation does not
**PMID:** 42673683 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 133, first-episode drug-naïve, hs-CRP ≥10 mg/L excluded, β = 0.362 (PHQ-9) and 0.288 (MADRS), eveningness significant for PHQ-9 only, hs-CRP not independently associated, BMI the strongest correlate of CRP — all accurate.

**Issue A. "Predicts" in a cross-sectional study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Childhood maltreatment predicts depression severity — inflammation does not"
- **Abstract says:** "This cross-sectional study included 133 first episode drug-naïve patients... Childhood maltreatment showed consistent associations across self-reported and clinician-rated severity."
- **Problem:** Nothing was predicted over time; these are cross-sectional associations, and the abstract uses "associated with" throughout.
- **Suggested fix:** "Childhood maltreatment tracks depression severity — inflammation doesn't"

**Issue B. Severity restriction omitted from the null's conditions** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "narrow BMI distribution may have masked a real association"
- **Abstract says:** "In this sample, with moderate-to-severe depression and a relatively narrow BMI distribution, no independent association between hs-CRP and depressive severity was observed."
- **Problem:** The authors condition their null on two sample features; the digest carries only one. A restricted severity range limits the variance available to detect a CRP–severity association.
- **Suggested fix:** "sample restricted to moderate-to-severe depression and a narrow BMI distribution — either may have masked a real association"

### Study 10: A history of childhood adversity doesn't stop chronic pain treatment working
**PMID:** 42166956 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 324, 5-week interdisciplinary inpatient program in Germany, three ACE groups, 83.3% ≥1 ACE, 18.5% ≥4, higher baseline distress, emotional abuse as a predictor — all accurate.

**Issue A. Equivalent improvement across ACE groups is not reported** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "all groups improved significantly over the program, with the high-ACE group still ending at higher absolute distress" / "people carrying four or more childhood adversities improved as much as everyone else" / "early adversity set the starting point, not the slope of recovery"
- **Abstract says:** "While patients showed significant improvement in these measures by the end of the rehabilitation program, those with more ACEs continued to report higher levels of distress."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports improvement for the sample and persistently higher distress in the high-ACE group. It does not report a group-by-time interaction test or per-group change scores, so "improved as much as everyone else" and "not the slope of recovery" — the entire hook — are unverifiable from the abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "patients improved significantly over the programme, though those with more ACEs still ended at higher absolute distress; whether the size of improvement was equivalent across ACE groups is not reported in the abstract"

**Issue B. Authors' conclusion points the other way** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It complicates the fatalistic reading of the ACE literature"
- **Abstract says:** "Patients with a history of multiple ACEs experience greater initial distress and require more comprehensive, trauma-informed and psychosomatically oriented care approaches to achieve optimal rehabilitation outcomes."
- **Problem:** The authors' takeaway is that high-ACE patients need more intensive care, not that adversity is unproblematic for treatment response. The digest's optimistic frame should acknowledge this.
- **Suggested fix:** Add: "The authors' own conclusion is that multiple-ACE patients need more comprehensive, trauma-informed care to reach good outcomes."

### Study 11: A 17-minute video handed out during a forensic exam may help after sexual assault
**PMID:** 42635087 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 50 (25/25), SAMFE within seven days, SMART design, n = 36 re-randomized (18/18), 80% rating STAR extremely acceptable, 94%/90% retention — all accurate, and the pitch correctly warns that efficacy was not tested.

**Issue A. Headline implies benefit that was not measured** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "A 17-minute video handed out during a forensic exam may help after sexual assault"
- **Abstract says:** "This study examined the acceptability and feasibility of a 17-minute video... STAR and TextSTAR are acceptable and feasible brief interventions for recent sexual assault survivors."
- **Problem:** No symptom outcomes were tested against the control arms; "may help" is exactly the inference a feasibility trial cannot support, and the headline is what an editor reads first even though the body and Caveats are careful.
- **Suggested fix:** "A 17-minute video handed out during a forensic exam proves deliverable — and welcome — after sexual assault"

### Study 12: A new screening tool aims to catch PTSD that starts in pregnancy, not just at birth
**PMID:** 41936021 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

25 candidate items, EFA n = 114, 17-item two-factor solution, 68.36% of variance, α = .954 and .897, ROC n = 52, cutoff ≥11, 90.9% sensitivity, 61% specificity, validation pending — all accurate.

**Issue A. Novelty tag** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Novelty:** First-in-class"
- **Abstract says:** "While existing perinatal PTSD tools show validity, they vary in diagnostic agreement, and most are focused on PTSD following childbirth."
- **Problem:** Perinatal PTSD screening tools already exist; this one extends the window across conception and pregnancy. "First-in-class" overstates it.
- **Suggested fix:** "Novelty:** Extends an existing tool class (first to span conception, pregnancy and postpartum)"

**Issue B. Specific missed presentations not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "so PTSD following miscarriage, fertility treatment, or a frightening pregnancy is routinely missed"
- **Abstract says:** "Unfortunately, gaps remain in practice for identifying perinatal PTSD," with the tool aimed at events "during conception, pregnancy and the postpartum."
- **Problem:** The named examples and the claim that they are "routinely missed" are unverifiable from the abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "so trauma arising at conception or during pregnancy — not just at birth — falls outside what current screening asks about"

### Study 13: Neglect, not the drinking itself, links parental substance abuse to child exploitation
**PMID:** 41974448 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 738 children aged 10–18 in Lahore urban slums, directly exposed to paternal substance abuse, SEM, neglect as mediator, gender control barely changing the model — all accurate.

**Issue A. Headline and pitch imply full mediation** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Neglect, not the drinking itself, links parental substance abuse to child exploitation" / "it wasn't the father's substance use that tracked with exploitation so much as the collapse of supervision that followed it"
- **Abstract says:** "PSA was significantly associated with both CSE and CED and was strongly linked to CN. CN significantly predicted both forms of child vulnerability and mediated the relationships between PSA and these outcomes... all paths remained significant and consistent in direction."
- **Problem:** Paternal substance abuse retained significant direct associations with both outcomes; the abstract describes mediation, not that the direct link disappeared. "Not the drinking itself" reverses the emphasis.
- **Suggested fix:** "Caregiver neglect is the link between paternal substance abuse and child exploitation" / "the father's substance use was still directly associated with exploitation, but much of the relationship ran through the collapse of supervision"

**Issue B. "Parental" and "drinking"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Neglect, not the drinking itself, links parental substance abuse to child exploitation"
- **Abstract says:** "paternal substance abuse (PSA)"; children "directly exposed to paternal substance abuse."
- **Problem:** The exposure measured was paternal, not parental (the digest body gets this right), and "substance abuse" is not specifically alcohol.
- **Suggested fix:** "Neglect, not the substance use itself, links paternal addiction to child exploitation"

### Study 14: Rejection and violence, not everyday discrimination, drive trauma symptoms in trans people
**PMID:** 42658623 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

PRISMA-guided meta-analysis, 13 sources / 10 unique samples, GMSR measure, r = .39 rejection, .36 violence, .25 discrimination — all accurate.

**Issue A. Causal verb and a false negative in the headline** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Rejection and violence, not everyday discrimination, drive trauma symptoms in trans people"
- **Abstract says:** "a small mean effect size was found for the association between PTSS and discrimination (r = .25)... These results highlight the magnitude of the association between distal gender minority stressors and PTSS, particularly for experiences of rejection and violence."
- **Problem:** Two errors in one line: "drive" is causal language for a meta-analysis of cross-sectional associations (the digest's own pitch says "Stick to association; none of these are longitudinal"), and discrimination did show a small but real association, so "not everyday discrimination" misstates the result as a null.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rejection and violence track trauma symptoms in trans people more strongly than everyday discrimination"

**Issue B. Criterion A claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The authors argue these stressors sit outside the conventional Criterion A definition of a traumatic event"
- **Abstract says:** "The findings have implications for improving diagnostic procedures and treatment planning for the TGD population"; the title refers to "Broadening conceptualizations of trauma."
- **Problem:** Criterion A is never mentioned in the abstract. The direction of the authors' argument is consistent with the digest, but the specific claim is unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "The authors argue for broadening how trauma is conceptualized for this population" — and confirm the Criterion A framing from the full text.

**Issue C. Size comparison to "recognized stressors"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "show associations with post-traumatic stress symptoms comparable in size to many recognized stressors"
- **Abstract says:** reports only the three effect sizes; no benchmarking against other stressors.
- **Problem:** No comparison to Criterion A events or other stressors appears in the abstract. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "show moderate associations with post-traumatic stress symptoms despite not meeting the diagnostic threshold for a traumatic event"

### Study 15: Earthquake survivors who build a "trauma identity" fare worse
**PMID:** 42036873 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 285 (65.3% female), 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, cross-sectional SEM, the memory → cognitions → identity/safety-seeking → symptoms → mental health pathway, and the identity/PTSD versus safety-seeking/CPTSD split all match.

**Issue A. Headline asserts an outcome the design can't order in time** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Earthquake survivors who build a 'trauma identity' fare worse"
- **Abstract says:** "A cross-sectional design was employed... These mediators were positively associated with PTSD and CPTSD symptoms, which negatively predicted mental health."
- **Problem:** "Build" and "fare worse" imply a process unfolding over time toward a later outcome. Everything was measured at one time point, which the digest's own Caveats note ("SEM path direction assumed rather than tested"), so the headline sits at odds with the caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "Earthquake survivors with a stronger 'trauma identity' report worse mental health"

### Study 16: Trouble naming your feelings may link childhood trauma to a stiffer heartbeat
**PMID:** 42579979 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 152 university students, mean age 22.19, ECG-derived HRV (RMSSD), childhood trauma negatively associated with HRV, serial mediation via alexithymia and anxiety — all accurate, and the pitch's warning against "childhood trauma damages your heart" is appropriate.

**Issue A. "Accounted for" is stronger than the abstract's wording** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Serial mediation analysis indicated that alexithymia and anxiety accounted for the association both independently and in sequence."
- **Abstract says:** "Serial mediation analysis indicated that alexithymia and anxiety symptoms were statistically associated with the relationship between childhood trauma and HRV through both independent and sequential indirect pathways."
- **Problem:** The authors deliberately use hedged, association-only phrasing ("Within a cross-sectional associational framework"). "Accounted for" implies the pathways explain the association.
- **Suggested fix:** "Serial mediation analysis found alexithymia and anxiety statistically associated with the trauma–HRV relationship through both independent and sequential indirect pathways."

### Study 17: How a therapist asks questions changes the trauma story a client tells
**PMID:** 42684797 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

62 sessions, 24 client interviews, participants from Iraq/Afghanistan/Syria/Lebanon in Isfahan, micro- plus macro-level analysis, the interpretive-question and open-ended-prompt findings, and the multilingual/cultural modulation all match. The digest correctly notes the study measured coherence, not accuracy.

**Issue A. "Reliably reshapes"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "while this work suggests retelling in supportive settings reliably reshapes narratives"
- **Abstract says:** "interpretive questions and reformulative practices appeared associated with changes in temporal sequencing, evaluative framing and narrative coherence"
- **Problem:** "Reliably" claims consistency and predictability that a qualitative, single-site study with no control condition cannot establish — and it undercuts the digest's own careful framing elsewhere in the entry.
- **Suggested fix:** "while this work suggests retelling in supportive settings can reshape how a narrative is sequenced and framed"

**Issue B. "Refugees and immigrants"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "62 therapy sessions and 24 client interviews with refugees and immigrants from Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria, and Lebanon"
- **Abstract says:** "How therapist discourse shapes trauma narratives in immigrant clients... 24 client interviews with participants from Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria and Lebanon"
- **Problem:** The abstract describes immigrant clients; refugee status is not stated and matters because the wider angle turns on asylum procedure.
- **Suggested fix:** "62 therapy sessions and 24 client interviews with immigrant clients from Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria, and Lebanon"

### Study 18: Who drops out of trauma treatment isn't predicted by how ill they are
**PMID:** 42222912 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. Framing is consistent with the abstract: N = 76 women hospitalized 2019–2023, 96% with PTSD / 74% ED / 70% both, 43.4% premature termination, associations with sexual risk-taking, impulsive departures and fewer prior hospitalizations, no sociodemographic or symptom-severity differences, and comparable outcomes for those who left and returned. The plain-language summary supports both the "behavioural indicators, not initial symptom severity" framing and the re-engagement angle, and the 4/10 fit score is well calibrated to a small single-center sample. One note: the "retrospective chart-based design" characterization in Caveats is unverifiable from abstract only, but it errs on the conservative side.
