### Study 1: Sleeping nine hours or more is linked to earlier death — sleeping six is not
**PMID:** 42744622 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Effect estimate reported without its confidence interval, which is borderline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Long sleepers had a 17% higher risk of death from any cause after adjustment"
- **Abstract says:** "long sleep duration was significantly associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality (adjusted hazard ratio: 1.17, 95% confidence interval: 1.01, 1.35)"
- **Problem:** The point estimate is reported correctly, but the lower bound of the CI is 1.01 — the single positive finding in the paper only just clears significance. A digest that frames this as the counterweight to "the dominant public health message" should show the reader how fragile the estimate is, especially since the pitch angle leans hard on the asymmetry between long and short sleepers.
- **Suggested fix:** "Long sleepers had a 17% higher risk of death from any cause after adjustment (HR 1.17, 95% CI 1.01–1.35 — only just clearing significance); short sleepers showed no significant excess risk for all-cause or cardiovascular death."

**Issue B. Author first names not verifiable from the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call the Institute of Epidemiology and Preventive Medicine team at National Taiwan University (Kuo-Liong Chien, Jiun-Yu Wang)"
- **Abstract says:** "Lin YC(1), Chien KL(1)(2)(3), Lin HJ(3)(4), Su TC(5), Wang J(1)(6)(7)"
- **Problem:** Initials match, and the institutional attribution is correct, but the expanded first names — particularly "Jiun-Yu Wang" for "Wang J" — cannot be confirmed from the abstract. Unverifiable from abstract only. Worth a check before a writer cold-calls.
- **Suggested fix:** Verify both first names against the journal's author list before the pitch goes out.

**NS fit note:** 7/10 is defensible on design grounds (large prospective cohort, hard mortality outcome, 30.8-year median follow-up) but the exposure is a single self-reported questionnaire item at baseline and the headline association is borderline. I would score it 6.

### Study 2: How you use your phone predicts depression far better than how long you use it
**PMID:** 42759324 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline asserts a comparison the authors explicitly declined to test** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "How you use your phone predicts depression far better than how long you use it"
- **Abstract says:** "time spent using social networking sites was weakly associated with depression symptoms (r = 0.11), whereas problematic use showed a larger association (r = 0.29); no correlation ratio was calculated."
- **Problem:** Two things. "Predicts" implies prospective prediction from what is a synthesis of cross-sectional correlations. And the authors state outright that they did not compute a ratio between the two correlations — so "far better" is the digest's descriptive read of two numbers side by side, not a tested contrast. The caveats mention descriptive cross-domain comparisons generally, but not this specific non-test on the headline finding.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "How you use your phone tracks depression more closely than how long you use it". Body: add "the authors note no formal correlation ratio was calculated, so the gap between 0.11 and 0.29 is a descriptive contrast, not a tested difference."

**Issue B. NS fit score sits above what the evidence base supports** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — a prospectively registered umbrella review that directly tests the assumption behind screen-time limits; pulled down by the poor quality of the underlying reviews."
- **Abstract says:** "56 of 59 reviews were rated critically low by AMSTAR-2... ODAF is not a confirmed causal mechanism."
- **Problem:** The rationale text acknowledges the problem but the number doesn't move for it. Nearly the entire evidence base is rated critically low, the framework is explicitly hypothesis-generating, and publication-bias-restricted contrasts exist for only three of five domains. A 7 puts this on par with the 30-year mortality cohort.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10, with the same rationale text.

### Study 3: Night owls don't actually sleep worse than larks — their clocks are just shifted
**PMID:** 42751860 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Most" overstates the abstract's "several"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Across quantitative and qualitative sleep measures, and across most circadian and ultradian rhythm parameters, the two groups did not differ."
- **Abstract says:** "The results did not reveal significant differences in sleep features nor in several circadian or ultradian rhythmicity parameters."
- **Problem:** "Several" tells you some parameters showed no difference; "most" makes a quantitative claim about the proportion that the abstract does not support. The distinction matters because the whole hook rests on the breadth of the null.
- **Suggested fix:** "Across quantitative and qualitative sleep measures, and across several circadian and ultradian rhythm parameters, the two groups did not differ."

Everything else checks out: N = 84 (42/42), mean age 23.3, 64 females, one week of wrist actigraphy, significant differences in all circadian and ultradian phase indicators, steeper LIDS decline in morning types, and the first LIDS-based chronotype comparison. Caveats are appropriately scoped and the null is correctly hedged.

### Study 4: Poor sleep and inactivity cluster together — and that pattern tracks bipolar disorder risk
**PMID:** 42723029 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline implies the poor-sleep/low-activity profile is the high-risk group; the abstract puts it among the lower-risk ones** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Poor sleep and inactivity cluster together — and that pattern tracks bipolar disorder risk"
- **Abstract says:** "Using the Low LE8 score group as a reference, all four comparison profiles exhibited significant protective associations for both anxiety (Hazard Ratio [HR] range: 0.65-0.78, all p < 0.001) and bipolar disorder (HR range: 0.37-0.51, all p < 0.001)"
- **Problem:** The "Low physical activity with poor sleep" profile is one of the four comparison profiles, all of which had *lower* bipolar incidence than the Low LE8 score reference group. The abstract reports only HR ranges, not profile-specific estimates, so nothing in it singles out the sleep/inactivity cluster as carrying elevated risk. A reader will take "tracks bipolar disorder risk" to mean elevated risk in that group — the opposite of the direction relative to the reference. The digest body is accurate; the headline is not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Health behaviours cluster into five patterns — and lumping them into one score hides a poor-sleep, low-activity group" or "Five distinct health-behaviour profiles, and all four healthier ones track lower bipolar risk."

**Issue B. Novelty label doesn't fit the study type** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** New mechanism"
- **Abstract says:** "These results support person-centred, pattern-based approaches over composite scoring for neuropsychiatric risk stratification and hypothesis generation."
- **Problem:** This is an observational latent-profile risk-stratification study. No mechanism is proposed or tested; the authors frame it as hypothesis generation. The label sets an expectation the paper doesn't meet.
- **Suggested fix:** Change to "New method / analytic approach."

Sample size (242,947), median follow-up (13.8 years), case counts (11,419 anxiety, 341 bipolar, 40 schizophrenia), HR ranges, the exploratory-and-null schizophrenia framing and Gao's affiliation all check out.

### Study 5: Ultramarathon runners get 96 minutes of sleep during a 217km race — and hallucinate
**PMID:** 42637627 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. The 96-minute figure is not specific to the 217km route** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Ultramarathon runners get 96 minutes of sleep during a 217km race"
- **Abstract says:** "15 male athletes (45.1 ± 10.5 years), who participated in Brazil 135 Ultramarathon (135 km: n = 4; 217 km: n = 11)... Mean total sleep time during the race was 96.67 ± 88.71 minutes"
- **Problem:** 96.67 minutes is the mean across the whole sample, which mixed two race distances. Attaching it to the 217km route in the headline implies a distance-specific figure the abstract doesn't report. The SD (88.71) is also almost as large as the mean, i.e. enormous variation between runners.
- **Suggested fix:** "Ultramarathon runners average 96 minutes of sleep across a multi-day race — and some hallucinate."

**Issue B. A statistically uninformative null is presented as a substantive finding** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Crucially, how much a runner slept during the race was uncorrelated with finish time." / "sleeping less didn't make anyone faster, so the sleep that runners sacrifice on the assumption it's costing them time appears to buy nothing"
- **Abstract says:** "Mean total sleep time during the race... was not correlated with finish time (P = .81; r = 0.10; 95% CI -0.59 to 0.90)"
- **Problem:** The confidence interval spans -0.59 to 0.90 — essentially the entire possible range of a correlation, in a sample of 13. This null carries almost no information; it cannot support "sleeping less didn't make anyone faster" as a conclusion, and the pitch builds its "surprise" on it. The caveats flag small N but not that this specific finding is uninformative rather than negative.
- **Suggested fix:** "Sleep during the race showed no correlation with finish time, though with only 13 runners the estimate is far too imprecise to rule out an effect in either direction (r = 0.10, 95% CI -0.59 to 0.90)."

Sample (15 enrolled, 13 with sleep data), age, actigraphy windows, symptom counts (sleepiness n=5, balance n=4, hallucinations n=4), the pre-race sleep deprivation and the P = .058 trends are all accurate.

### Study 6: Moderate drinkers have enlarged choroid plexus — and it tracks their sleep problems
**PMID:** 42202661 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline says "moderate drinkers"; the exposure group was moderate-to-heavy** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Moderate drinkers have enlarged choroid plexus — and it tracks their sleep problems"
- **Abstract says:** "young adults who were moderate-to-heavy drinkers (HD) or light-to-non-drinkers (LD)"
- **Problem:** The comparison group is "moderate-to-heavy" versus "light-to-non", and the digest body itself switches to calling them "heavy drinkers" two sentences later. A headline reading "moderate drinkers" invites readers who drink modestly to place themselves in the affected group, when the group as defined includes heavy drinkers and is contrasted against people who drink very little or not at all.
- **Suggested fix:** "Moderate-to-heavy drinkers have enlarged choroid plexus — and it tracks their sleep problems"

Everything else verifies: N = 796 (399 HD / 397 LD), ages 22–37, HCP source, bilateral ChP enlargement with no total CSF difference, the within-HD correlations with sleep latency and sleep disturbance plus PSQI total in males, and the sex-split direction (HD females better on habitual sleep efficiency and duration, HD males worse on sleep latency). The digest correctly avoids the causal reading that the paper's own title ("predicts sleep disruption") invites.

### Study 7: Camera-tracked cribs reveal how a baby's temperament locks in their sleep pattern
**PMID:** 42470789 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. The framing of supportive parenting reverses the reported interaction** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "suggests a fussy baby's bad nights are more self-perpetuating, unless a responsive parent is in the loop" / "supportive parenting appears to loosen the grip of a bad run of nights"
- **Abstract says:** "When caregivers were higher in supportive parenting, there was a positive association between infant negative emotionality and TST inertia."
- **Problem:** The main effect (supportive parenting → lower TST inertia) is reported correctly in "The study". But the moderation goes the other way: at *higher* levels of supportive parenting, negative emotionality was *positively* associated with sleep-time inertia. So supportive parenting did not buffer the temperament–inertia link — if anything the link was stronger where parenting was more supportive. Both the "Why it matters" and the pitch angle build on the buffering reading.
- **Suggested fix:** "Why it matters: ...suggests a fussy baby's bad nights are more self-perpetuating — and, counterintuitively, that the temperament–inertia link was strongest in the most supportive households, a result the authors leave unexplained." Drop "unless a responsive parent is in the loop" and "loosen the grip" from the pitch.

**Issue B. "Locks in" implies causation in an observational design** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Camera-tracked cribs reveal how a baby's temperament locks in their sleep pattern"
- **Abstract says:** "infant negative emotionality was associated with more TST inertia and lower PVC inertia"
- **Problem:** "Locks in" reads as a causal mechanism. This is cross-sectional questionnaire data on temperament correlated with nightly sleep metrics; the pitch angle correctly says "this is observational," but the headline doesn't.
- **Suggested fix:** "Camera-tracked cribs show a baby's temperament tracks how stuck their sleep pattern gets"

N = 208, ages 4–12 months, Nanit recruitment, up to two weeks of nightly data, TST/PVC via computer vision, DSEM, and the three main directional findings all check out — as does the digest's sharp catch that the abstract says "between-person methodologies" while reporting within-person models.

### Study 8: It's how well you sleep, not how long, that tracks your heart and metabolic risk
**PMID:** 42648984 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline denies a duration association the study actually found** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It's how well you sleep, not how long, that tracks your heart and metabolic risk"
- **Abstract says:** "Population-average cardiometabolic risk factor count was 9% higher among short sleepers, 15% to 20% higher across poor sleep quality measures... Poor sleep quality and short sleep duration were associated with higher population-level cardiometabolic risk factor burden."
- **Problem:** The authors' own conclusion names both exposures. Short sleep was associated with a 9% higher risk-factor count; the finding is that quality shows a *stronger* association, not that duration shows none. The digest body reports this correctly, so it is the headline alone that overreaches — but it is the line that survives into a pitch email.
- **Suggested fix:** "How well you sleep tracks heart and metabolic risk more strongly than how long you sleep"

Sample (25,433), survey year, the four risk factors, the 15–20% vs 9% contrast, the more-severe-combinations finding and the SES interaction for difficulty staying asleep are all accurate. NIH funding correctly carried into caveats.

### Study 9: Parents who quit sleep training describe distress, exhaustion and disrupted breastfeeding
**PMID:** 42725637 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Parents" broadens a sample of breastfeeding mothers** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Parents who quit sleep training describe distress, exhaustion and disrupted breastfeeding"
- **Abstract says:** "explore the experiences of breastfeeding mothers (n = 10) who had discontinued behavioural sleep interventions"
- **Problem:** All ten participants were breastfeeding mothers. Given that one of the five themes is specifically about the breastfeeding relationship, generalizing to "parents" flattens the population in a way that matters for the story.
- **Suggested fix:** "Breastfeeding mothers who quit sleep training describe distress, exhaustion and a disrupted feeding relationship"

**Issue B. "First structured account" is not claimed in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this is the first structured account of what the people who leave actually experienced"
- **Abstract says:** "there is a lack of qualitative research exploring: a) the reasons why families disengage from these interventions, and b) how these interventions interact with the breastfeeding relationship."
- **Problem:** A gap in the literature is not the same as a first-of-its-kind claim. Unverifiable from abstract only, and priority claims are the kind of thing an editor will be asked to defend.
- **Suggested fix:** "this is a rare structured account of what the people who leave actually experienced."

The five themes, the IPA method, and the Australian residential/inpatient setting are reported accurately, and the caveats correctly note the sample is drawn only from those who discontinued. Author first names (Andrew Lewis, Brittany Van Zanden) are unverifiable from the abstract; note also that Van Zanden holds a second affiliation at University of Technology Sydney.

### Study 10: Switching on a CPAP machine calms blood pressure the same night — even in people without hypertension
**PMID:** 42754769 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline says CPAP and implies blood pressure fell; the study used PAP and measured variability** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Switching on a CPAP machine calms blood pressure the same night — even in people without hypertension"
- **Abstract says:** "positive airway pressure (PAP) therapy... NBPF was defined as the standard deviation of systolic blood pressure (SBP) derived from PTT"
- **Problem:** Two small slips. The intervention is PAP titration generally, not CPAP specifically. And "calms blood pressure" is likely to be read as blood pressure *falling*, when the outcome is the standard deviation of PTT-derived systolic pressure across the night — a variability measure. The body text handles both correctly.
- **Suggested fix:** "Starting airway pressure therapy steadies overnight blood pressure swings within hours — even in people without hypertension"

N = 464, split-night design, 2015–2023, reductions of 20.71 and 18.97 mmHg, Cohen's d of 0.96 and 0.90, and the non-significant adjusted between-group difference all verify. The digest deserves credit for declining to repeat the abstract's own clinical recommendation ("These findings support early PAP initiation"), and for flagging the first-half/second-half circadian confound the authors don't mention.

### Study 11: The people with the worst sleep in America sit at the intersection of several identities
**PMID:** 42660768 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Caveat mischaracterizes how trans and gender nonconforming people were handled** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "trans and gender nonconforming people pooled into one stratum, likely masking heterogeneity"
- **Abstract says:** "grouping participants into 20 intersectional social strata... jointly defined by racialized identity (Black, Latine, White, multiracial/other) and sex/sexual identity (heterosexual-cisgender males, heterosexual-cisgender females, LGBQ-cisgender males, LGBQ-cisgender females, trans and gender nonconforming people)"
- **Problem:** Four racialized categories × five sex/sexual identity categories = the 20 strata, so TGNC people were crossed with racialized identity and appear across four strata, not one. The real limitation is that TGNC people were collapsed into a single sex/sexual identity category rather than disaggregated by gender identity or sexual orientation — a defensible caveat, just not the one written.
- **Suggested fix:** "trans and gender nonconforming people collapsed into a single sex/sexual identity category rather than disaggregated, likely masking heterogeneity"

N = 235,326, BRFSS 2022, the MAIHDA description, the 20 strata and the both-tails finding for multiply marginalized groups are accurate, and the digest correctly resists turning the authors' policy conclusion into a recommendation.

### Study 12: Being aware you're dreaming is the easy part — controlling the dream is not
**PMID:** 42728107 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Pitch hook asserts a comparative claim the review does not make** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The hook is the non-agentic route: the idea that what you expect to happen in a dream shapes it more reliably than what you decide to do."
- **Abstract says:** "we propose that dream control can occur through both agentic (deliberate, goal-driven) and non-agentic (implicit, expectation-driven) forms."
- **Problem:** The review proposes two coexisting routes. It makes no claim that the expectation-driven route is *more reliable* than the deliberate one — and since the authors also identify the lack of any standardized way to measure dream control, no such comparison could be supported. The hook the writer would pitch on is not in the paper.
- **Suggested fix:** "The hook is the non-agentic route: the proposal that what you implicitly expect to happen in a dream shapes it alongside — and sometimes despite — what you deliberately try to do."

The narrative-review status, the lucidity/agency/control distinction, the survey of influenceable dream aspects and individual differences, the standardized-measurement gap, and Bonamino's QUT affiliation all check out, and the caveats are appropriately blunt about the framework being proposed rather than tested.

### Study 13: Honeybees nap in bursts all day, and their sleep may hold a lesson for AI
**PMID:** 42728400 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Neuron count is not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "If a brain with under a million neurons still needs sleep to protect what it has learned, that constrains theories of what sleep is for"
- **Abstract says:** "Insects combine compact nervous systems with well-characterized learning behaviors"
- **Problem:** The figure is a commonly cited estimate for *Apis mellifera*, but it is not in this abstract — unverifiable from abstract only. The second half of the sentence also asserts that the bee brain "needs sleep to protect what it has learned," whereas the abstract only says the comparison "inform[s] hypotheses about sleep's adaptive roles in memory consolidation." The conditional "if" softens it, but a reader will take the premise as established.
- **Suggested fix:** "If a brain as compact as a bee's still needs sleep — and the authors treat memory consolidation as a hypothesis, not a settled role — that constrains theories of what sleep is for"

The review-plus-new-data structure, the automated video analysis of antennal movement over day-long recordings, the polyphasic finding in *Apis mellifera*, the list of shared vertebrate/invertebrate features, the catastrophic-forgetting proposal and both named contacts (Lei at ASU, Bazhenov at UC San Diego) verify. The caveats correctly label the AI link a proposal.

### Study 14: A child's airway is most vulnerable between the ages of five and ten
**PMID:** 42762282 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline states a conceptual window as an established fact** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A child's airway is most vulnerable between the ages of five and ten"
- **Abstract says:** "creating a transient 'window of vulnerability' between approximately 5 and 10 years of age" — in a "narrative review" offering "a conceptual, site-specific clinical framework"
- **Problem:** The authors put "window of vulnerability" in scare quotes, prefix it with "approximately," and label the whole framework conceptual. The digest's own caveats say exactly this, but the headline reads as an empirical finding about children's airways. For a paediatric-airway claim that parents might act on, the gap between headline and caveat is worth closing.
- **Suggested fix:** "Children's airways may be most vulnerable between five and ten, a new framework argues"

**Issue B. Two contextual claims not sourced to the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Pediatric obstructive sleep apnea affects a meaningful slice of children and is frequently missed" / "the framework that has shaped thinking about lymphoid tissue since the 1930s"
- **Abstract says:** "Pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is closely linked to the growth and interaction of upper airway soft tissues" and refers to Scammon's curves having "long provided a fundamental framework" without dating them.
- **Problem:** Prevalence, underdiagnosis and the 1930s date are all plausible and probably correct from the wider literature, but none is verifiable from this abstract. Unverifiable from abstract only — source them independently before the pitch.
- **Suggested fix:** Attribute both to external sources, or cut the underdiagnosis clause.

The structure-specific peaks (adenoids 6–8, palatine tonsils 8–10, anterior tongue near adult size by 8–10, posterior third into adolescence), the absence of lingual tonsil normative data, and Yoon's dual Pacific/Stanford affiliation all check out, as does the caveat noting the authors' orthodontic framing.

### Study 15: AI can spot sleep apnea from an ECG — but it isn't detecting your airway
**PMID:** 42747635 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. The authors' double-hedged inference is restated as a firm conclusion** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Specifically, they conclude the algorithms are not detecting airway obstruction at all — they are reading downstream autonomic nervous system responses in the heart's rhythm." (and headline: "but it isn't detecting your airway")
- **Abstract says:** "these findings suggest that AI systems may not directly detect airway obstruction but instead may identify downstream physiological responses, particularly autonomic nervous system alterations reflected in cardiovascular signals."
- **Problem:** "Suggest... may not directly... may identify" becomes "conclude... not at all... they are reading." Three layers of hedging removed, and "not directly" is turned into "not at all," which is a stronger claim. This matters because the mechanistic reframing is the whole reason the entry is in the digest, and the caveats correctly note it is an inference from architectural consistency rather than direct evidence — the body text contradicts that caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "Specifically, they suggest the algorithms may not be detecting airway obstruction directly — they may instead be reading downstream autonomic nervous system responses in the heart's rhythm." Headline: "AI can spot sleep apnea from an ECG — but it may not be detecting your airway."

Thirteen included studies, pooled sensitivity 0.88 and specificity 0.89, AUC above 0.90, moderate heterogeneity, the consistency-across-architectures observation, the PRISMA-DTA compliance, segment-level framing and Bulut's affiliation all verify. The caveat that segment-level accuracy overstates patient-level performance is a good catch.

### Study 16: A telehealth sleep program for children in foster care gets parents to stay the course
**PMID:** 42725804 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. Framing is consistent with the abstract. N = 45 randomized (22 SAFE-T / 23 control), children aged 2–5, telehealth delivery, 21/22 completing all sessions and the post-treatment assessment, satisfaction p = .005 with Hedges' g = 0.92, favourable qualitative feedback, 3-month follow-up and Alfano's Stony Brook affiliation all check out. The digest is explicit in three separate places that this is feasibility and acceptability only — "it doesn't yet show the sleep improves," "measured whether families would do it, not whether it worked" — which is exactly the right handling of a pilot RCT, and the NS fit of 4/10 reflects that honestly. ("Single center" is unverifiable from the abstract but is a conservative caveat rather than a claim.)

### Study 17: An epilepsy treatment cleans up the EEG without necessarily helping the child
**PMID:** 42407303 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. The digest states that clinical outcomes did not improve; the abstract reports that they did** — Severity: Major

- **As written:** "An epilepsy treatment cleans up the EEG without necessarily helping the child" / "this suggests the number can improve markedly while the child's day-to-day functioning does not follow" / "Open on a parent being told the EEG looks much better" against "a child whose life hasn't changed"
- **Abstract says:** "Seizure frequency decreased and clinical scale scores improved during follow-up." And: "IVIg therapy was associated with significant and sustained electrophysiological improvement and is accompanied by clinical improvement in children with EE-SWAS and DEE-SWAS. However, changes in SWI were not significantly correlated with functional, behavioral, or seizure outcomes"
- **Problem:** This reverses the clinical finding. Seizure frequency fell and clinical scale scores improved over follow-up. What the study found is a *dissociation between the magnitude of EEG change and the magnitude of clinical change* — SWI reduction did not predict who improved functionally or behaviourally. That is a genuine and interesting surrogate-endpoint story, and the digest's "wider angle" frames it correctly. But the headline, the "Why it matters" and the pitch's opening image all tell readers the children did not improve, which is factually wrong and could mislead a writer into calling a clinician with a false premise. (The uncontrolled design means the improvement can't be attributed to IVIg — a point the caveats make correctly — but "can't be attributed to the drug" is a different claim from "didn't happen.")
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "The EEG marker used to judge this epilepsy treatment doesn't track how the child actually does." Why it matters: "The spike-wave index is the defining measurement of this condition and is widely used to judge whether treatment is working; here both the index and the children's clinical scores improved over 12 months, but the size of the EEG change bore no relationship to the size of the clinical change — so the number cannot stand in for the outcome." Pitch: open on a parent told the EEG looks much better, and the clinician who cannot say what that means for this particular child.

N = 36, prospective enrolment, SWI from NREM sleep EEG at baseline/6/12 months, the large effect sizes, the null multivariable associations with PEDI and CBCL change, and ρ = −0.18, p = 0.29 for seizure outcome are all reported accurately, and the no-control-arm limitation is correctly flagged in both the pitch and the caveats.
