### Study 1: Genetic evidence questions why autism is diagnosed so much more often in boys
**PMID:** 42658390 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (with two moderate framing problems)

**Issue A. "Not explained by sex" applies only to early-diagnosed autism** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Early- and late-diagnosed autism showed strikingly different genetic association profiles, and these differences were not explained by sex."
- **Abstract says:** "Multiple regression models confirmed that early-specific associations were not confounded by biological sex. Conversely, the correlations between late-diagnosed autism and the sex-stratified autism GWAS were near 1, indicating that late-specific associations may be confounded."
- **Problem:** The digest generalizes a result the authors explicitly split. Only the early-diagnosis associations survived the sex control; the late-diagnosis ones may be confounded by sex. Relatedly, "If the common-variant genetics of autism are essentially the same in males and females" overshoots — the same abstract reports stronger internalizing correlations for female-diagnosed autism, i.e. some sex-linked signal.
- **Suggested fix:** "Early-diagnosed autism showed genetic association profiles that differed from late-diagnosed autism and were not confounded by sex; for late-diagnosed autism, correlations with the sex-stratified GWAS were near 1, so those associations may be sex-confounded. The genetic correlation between male and female autism was high, with few sex-specific associations, though female-diagnosed autism did show stronger genetic correlations with internalizing traits."

**Issue B. Pitch hook depends on a dissociation the paper qualifies** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the finding that the genetics say she is not a different kind of case because she is female, but because she was spotted late"
- **Abstract says:** "the correlations between late-diagnosed autism and the sex-stratified autism GWAS were near 1, indicating that late-specific associations may be confounded"
- **Problem:** The hook asserts that late diagnosis, not sex, is the meaningful genetic axis — but for the late-diagnosed group specifically, the authors say sex and diagnostic timing could not be disentangled. The exciting version of the result is the one the abstract cautions against.
- **Suggested fix:** "Lead on the finding that timing of diagnosis carves up autism genetics at least as sharply as sex does — while being clear that for late-diagnosed autism the two are statistically entangled, and the authors say so."

**Issue C. 4:1 sex ratio not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the 4:1 diagnostic sex ratio looks less like biology and more like who gets noticed"
- **Abstract says:** "Autism is a neurodevelopmental disorder with a significant male prevalence bias."
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only; the specific ratio comes from outside the paper and varies by source and ascertainment.
- **Suggested fix:** "the well-documented male bias in autism diagnoses looks less like biology and more like who gets noticed, and when"

**Issue D. "Overturns prior research" label is only half right** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** "Consistent with prior findings, we identified significantly divergent genetic associations with external traits between early- and late-diagnosed autism subtypes."
- **Problem:** The early/late divergence replicates prior work; only the sex result challenges received wisdom. The label as applied to the whole study is too broad. (On NS fit: 7/10 is defensible for a large-sample genomic analysis, but given the acknowledged confounding of the late-diagnosis result I would score it 6.)
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** Replicates prior subtype findings; challenges the assumed genetic basis of the male prevalence bias"

### Study 4: Treating parents' stress lowers autistic preschoolers' emotional meltdowns
**PMID:** 42665438 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (two moderate)

**Issue A. Causal headline for a secondary outcome with no untreated control** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Treating parents' stress lowers autistic preschoolers' emotional meltdowns"
- **Abstract says:** "Parent-reported child dysregulation and observed dyadic dysregulation decreased significantly across groups, with reduced parenting stress predicting decreased parent-reported dysregulation."
- **Problem:** Improvement occurred *across both active arms*, so the design cannot attribute the decrease to stress treatment; and the significant predictive link was to *parent-reported* dysregulation only, not to observed child dysregulation, which stayed stable. "Meltdowns" also substitutes a behavioral event for the measured construct (emotion dysregulation). The digest body and caveats get this right; the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Parents' stress falls, and reported child dysregulation falls with it — but the child's solo regulation doesn't budge"

**Issue B. Mischaracterizes what the intervention targeted** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Neither the parents' mental health nor the child was the direct target of child treatment"
- **Abstract says:** "we examined secondary outcomes of a randomized trial ... to assess downstream effects of parent stress-reduction interventions (MBSR vs. Psychoeducational Support) on emotion dysregulation"
- **Problem:** The interventions did directly target parent stress/mental health; the child was the untargeted party. As written the sentence is garbled and contradicts the trial design.
- **Suggested fix:** "The interventions targeted parenting stress, not the child, yet parent-reported child emotion dysregulation and observed dyadic dysregulation both fell across groups."

### Study 5: One in five autistic adolescents shows signs of catatonia — most often girls
**PMID:** 42610640 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one moderate)

**Issue A. "Most often girls" misstates a predictor as a majority** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "One in five autistic adolescents shows signs of catatonia — most often girls"
- **Abstract says:** "Logistic regression revealed that female gender and clinically significant separation anxiety symptoms were independent predictors of catatonia."
- **Problem:** Female sex raised the odds of catatonia; the abstract never reports the sex composition of the catatonia group. In a male-skewed autism clinic sample the absolute majority of catatonia cases may well have been boys. "Most often girls" states something the data in the abstract do not support.
- **Suggested fix:** "One in five autistic adolescents at a specialist clinic showed signs of catatonia — and girls were at higher risk"

**Issue B. Headline prevalence stated as a general fact** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "One in five autistic adolescents shows signs of catatonia"
- **Abstract says:** "A total of 107 adolescents aged 12-20 years with ASD were evaluated at a tertiary child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinic ... Catatonia was identified in 21.5% of participants."
- **Problem:** The caveats flag referral bias, but the headline reads as a population prevalence. Adding two words fixes it.
- **Suggested fix:** See fix in Issue A ("at a specialist clinic").

**Issue C. "Untreatable label" is editorializing hype** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a treatable condition hiding inside an untreatable label"
- **Abstract says:** "Early recognition and intervention may mitigate functional deterioration and improve clinical outcomes."
- **Problem:** The abstract makes no claim about autism being untreatable, and the phrase imports a contested framing (autism as something to be treated) that New Scientist Mind readers and autistic sources will push back on.
- **Suggested fix:** "a potentially treatable syndrome whose signs are easily read as part of the autism itself"

### Study 7: A quarter of people with intellectual disability on multiple antipsychotics exceed the maximum recommended dose
**PMID:** 42670819 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one moderate)

**Issue A. Omits that most of the cohort had a recorded psychotic illness** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Autism was recorded in 42–58% of cases and 'behaviours that challenge' in 77–87%."
- **Abstract says:** "Autism (42%-58%), psychotic illness (61%-71%), and behaviours that challenge (77%-87%) were the most common comorbidities."
- **Problem:** The digest selects two of three listed comorbidities and drops the one that most complicates the framing. With psychotic illness recorded in 61–71%, much of this prescribing may be for a licensed indication, which undercuts reading the >1000 mg figure purely as evidence of over-medication for behavior. The abstract also notes higher chlorpromazine-equivalent doses in those with psychosis.
- **Suggested fix:** "Autism was recorded in 42–58% of cases, psychotic illness in 61–71% and 'behaviours that challenge' in 77–87% — and doses were significantly higher in those with psychosis or behaviours that challenge."

**Issue B. Wrong institution for a named source** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call Rohit Shankar (Cornell/Plymouth)"
- **Abstract says:** "University of Plymouth, UK ... CIDER, Cornwall Partnership NHS Foundation Trust Truro, UK"
- **Problem:** Cornwall, not Cornell. A writer cold-calling on this would look careless.
- **Suggested fix:** "Call Rohit Shankar (University of Plymouth / Cornwall Partnership NHS Foundation Trust)"

### Study 8: Autistic adults imagining "what if" blame circumstances where others blame people
**PMID:** 41578156 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Pitch angle turns a difference in degree into a reversal** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "reach for physical rather than human causes"
- **Abstract says:** "while both groups showed a strong preference to produce and fixate human causes, this bias was stronger among neurotypical adults than autistic participants, who more often considered physical causes"
- **Problem:** Both groups preferred human causes; autistic adults just did so less strongly. The caveats acknowledge this ("group difference is in bias strength, not presence"), but the pitch line — and the headline's "blame circumstances where others blame people" — reads as an opposite default, which is the more exciting result a reader would wrongly infer.
- **Suggested fix:** "reach for human causes less reliably, and consider physical ones more often, than neurotypical adults do"

### Study 11: Autistic children's pupils reveal the extra effort of seeing things from another viewpoint
**PMID:** 41992731 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one moderate)

**Issue A. "Similar answers by working harder" contradicts the accuracy finding** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "autistic children may reach similar answers by working harder" and, in the wider angle, "why matched task performance can conceal wildly different internal costs"
- **Abstract says:** "Autistic participants showed reduced accuracy when rating an avatar's perspective ... Incongruency elicited stronger effects on behavioral performance and LFSW in autistic relative to neurotypical participants."
- **Problem:** Performance was *not* matched — autistic participants were less accurate and more disrupted by incongruency. The compensation-at-equal-performance story is a different, more appealing result than the one reported. The pupillometry supports greater effort, but alongside poorer performance, not despite equal performance.
- **Suggested fix:** "It suggests that difficulty taking someone else's visual perspective in autism is partly a domain-general problem with filtering out irrelevant information — and that autistic children appear to expend more cognitive effort even while performing less accurately." (And in the wider angle, drop "matched task performance" in favor of "why effort and outcome can come apart.")

### Study 15: Latino teenagers assume they'll care for an autistic sibling; others aren't so sure
**PMID:** 42537268 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Teenagers" narrows an 8–17 sample** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Latino teenagers assume they'll care for an autistic sibling"
- **Abstract says:** "a non-autistic sibling between 8 and 17 years old"
- **Problem:** Roughly half the eligible age range is pre-teen, and the pitch angle itself opens on a 12-year-old. Calling them all teenagers misstates the population and, given N=21, could mean very few participants were teenagers at all.
- **Suggested fix:** "Latino children and teenagers assume they'll care for an autistic sibling; others aren't so sure"

### Study 17: Families in Brazil and Spain are still not talking to autistic teens about sex
**PMID:** 42528308 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Risk and sex-education claims are not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Autistic young people face elevated risks of sexual abuse and exploitation and often receive less sex education than peers"
- **Abstract says:** Nothing about abuse risk or comparative sex-education receipt; it describes taboo, silence, misinformation and "limited institutional support."
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only. The claim may be well supported in the wider literature, but as the load-bearing first clause of "Why it matters" it needs its own citation rather than resting on this paper.
- **Suggested fix:** "Sexuality is rarely discussed with autistic young people, and this study documents the mechanism — not refusal, but silence plus improvisation plus institutional absence — across two very different countries. (Source separately for any claim about elevated abuse risk.)"

### Study 18: Changing the building, not the resident, may reduce aggression in care settings
**PMID:** 42605201 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline promotes one of four positive intervention categories** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Changing the building, not the resident, may reduce aggression in care settings"
- **Abstract says:** "The following interventions demonstrated positive effects ... enhanced services and revised care pathways, improved provider accountability, staff support and more livable, homelike physical environments."
- **Problem:** Physical environment was one of four categories, and the review does not rank them or pool effects. The headline reads as though building design were the primary finding; the body text is accurate, so this is a headline-emphasis problem.
- **Suggested fix:** "Changing the service, not the resident, may reduce aggression in care settings"

### Study 19: A 25-minute autism simulation shifts caregivers' attitudes — at least for a moment
**PMID:** 42556204 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. Setting (four primary health-care centers, Ras Al Khaimah), N (101), eligibility exclusions, session length and components, the four improved outcomes with p < .001, and the two quoted score changes (37.7→48.3; 22.2→28.0) all match. The digest reproduces the authors' own disclaimer about causality and durability, and the caveats cover the absent control group and follow-up. The "willingness to be near autistic people" gloss for the Personal Distance subscale is a fair rendering.
