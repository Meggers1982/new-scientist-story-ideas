### Study 1: Saying "we" instead of "I" makes people more afraid of AI
**PMID:** 40745650 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Core facts check out: Beijing Normal University affiliation, two AI-related text corpora (Studies 1A/1B), four experiments at N = 1,451, English and Chinese speakers, regulatory-scope mechanism, stronger effect for psychologically distant threat and among English speakers. *Pers Soc Psychol Bull* 2026 Sep;52(9), Epub 2025 Jul 31 — all correct.

**Issue A. Unsupported comparative claim about predictors** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The hook is that the most-studied predictor of AI anxiety here isn't knowledge, age or job exposure but a grammatical choice."
- **Abstract says:** Reports only that pronoun use "positively predicted and heightened perceived AI threat"; no comparison with knowledge, age, or occupational exposure is described.
- **Problem:** The phrasing implies the study benchmarked pronoun use against other candidate predictors and found it dominant. Nothing in the abstract supports a ranking.
- **Suggested fix:** "The hook is that a grammatical choice — not just knowledge, age or job exposure — turns out to shift how threatening people find AI."

**Issue B. Sample description not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Student and online participant samples"
- **Abstract says:** Gives only total N and language groups; no description of recruitment.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only. The caveat may well be right, but it should be confirmed against the methods before it goes in a pitch.
- **Suggested fix:** "Participant recruitment details not specified in the abstract — confirm sample composition before pitching."

NS fit 7/10 is defensible: corpora plus four experiments, with experimental manipulation supporting the causal headline.

### Study 2: Chatting with an AI that plays a foreigner softens prejudice — but only on the surface
**PMID:** 42763964 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 61 Japanese adults, one-month gap, 15-minute text interaction with an AI representing a Chinese individual, SDG discussion, pure control, gains on explicit positive attitudes and intergroup empathy, reduction in negative attitudes, null effects on intergroup anxiety and IAT — all accurately reported. Institute of Science Tokyo affiliation correct.

**Issue A. NS fit score high relative to evidence quality** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — a genuinely new use for chatbots with a clean control condition, held back by small N and pilot status"
- **Abstract says:** "This pilot study…"; "Sixty-one Japanese adults participated"; outcomes are pre/post explicit self-report plus IAT; "Our findings suggest… may mitigate some aspects of explicit intergroup bias."
- **Problem:** A 61-participant pilot with self-reported primary outcomes and null implicit effects is thin support for a 7. The score's own justification concedes the weaknesses.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10 — "novel application with a pure control, but pilot-scale N and explicit-measure-only effects."

### Study 3: AI spots hidden brain disorganization in epilepsy tissue that looks normal to pathologists
**PMID:** 42767799 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

83 FCD regions, 19 neurologically normal autopsy controls, QuPath NeuN detection (90% sensitivity, 12% false-positive rate), LASSO-regularized multinomial classifier, 70.6% subregion / 88.2% specimen accuracy, abnormal-EEG-but-histologically-normal regions resembling FCD-adjacent tissue and distinct from controls — all accurate. Mayo Clinic affiliation and named contacts correct. The pitch's "Avoid implying this changes surgical practice yet" correctly tracks the abstract's "Further investigation is warranted."

**Issue A. Novelty label mismatch** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** New mechanism"
- **Abstract says:** The paper describes a morphometric/spatial-statistical detection method and its diagnostic accuracy; no biological mechanism is proposed or tested.
- **Problem:** "New mechanism" implies a mechanistic discovery. This is a new measurement approach that reveals structure, not an account of how seizures arise.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** New method / new measurement"

### Study 4: Feeling good at using AI doesn't make students lean on it — trusting it does
**PMID:** 42804850 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 518 Chinese university students, PLS-SEM plus fsQCA, no direct self-efficacy–reliance association, single configuration combining social motivation and trust, no stable configuration without trust, instrumental motivation negatively associated with trust after holding self-efficacy constant — all correctly reported. Chongqing University of Technology and lead author Du Y correct.

**Issue A. Causal phrasing on cross-sectional survey data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Trust in the system was the decisive variable"; headline "Feeling good at using AI doesn't make students lean on it — trusting it does"
- **Abstract says:** "AI-induced self-efficacy bears no direct association with reliance; it is related to reliance only in conjunction with trust, the belief standing closest to delegation."
- **Problem:** "Decisive" and "makes / does" assert causation from a cross-sectional, self-reported survey. (The abstract's own discussion section leans causal too, but the digest should not inherit that.) The caveats flag cross-sectionality; the headline and body contradict it.
- **Suggested fix:** "Trust was the variable most closely associated with reliance" / headline: "Feeling skilled with AI isn't linked to leaning on it — trusting it is."

**Issue B. "Actually delegated" implies behavioral measurement** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "showed no direct association with how much students actually delegated thinking and task completion to it"
- **Abstract says:** Reliance is measured via survey self-report; no behavioral or log data are described.
- **Problem:** "Actually delegated" reads as observed behavior rather than a self-reported reliance scale.
- **Suggested fix:** "showed no direct association with how much students reported delegating thinking and task completion to it"

### Study 5: Two-thirds of HIV patients at a Nigerian clinic are already asking chatbots about their treatment
**PMID:** 42736503 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

427 adults on ART, one large tertiary referral centre in Kano, 75.2% aware, 72.6% ever used, 66.5% used for HIV-related queries, uses broken down as general HIV/ART info, side-effect checking, preparing clinician questions; internet access aOR 6.67; younger age, post-secondary education, smartphone ownership, English proficiency, comorbidities all listed as independent predictors — accurate. Zubairu Iliyasu / Bayero University Kano correct. Cross-sectional design and interviewer-administered questionnaire correctly flagged in caveats.

**Issue A. "Those who need it least" is an editorial inference** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "English proficiency and internet access gate who benefits, so the tool is most available to those who need it least."
- **Abstract says:** Reports predictors of use and concludes with "the need for clinician-endorsed, multilingual, and low-bandwidth chatbot designs, alongside safeguards… to ensure equitable integration."
- **Problem:** The study measures access correlates, not need. It contains no data on which patients have greater unmet information need. The equity point stands; the "need it least" flourish does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note the equity twist — English proficiency and internet access gate who can use it at all, which is why the authors call for multilingual, low-bandwidth designs."

### Study 6: People can't tell AI-generated faces from real photographs — and researchers are exploiting it
**PMID:** 42773386 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

University of Haifa, three-study validation framework, emotions (happiness, sadness, neutrality, anger), body-weight variation, Py-Feat computational validation plus human ratings, participants could not reliably distinguish generated images from real photographs, OSF openness — all accurate. NS fit 5/10 with the "stimulus-validation paper" framing is honest and well-calibrated.

**Issue A. Same-character control attributed to the whole study set** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "using the same synthetic 'character' across conditions to control for individual facial features"
- **Abstract says:** "Study 2 extended this framework to include anger as a fourth emotion while controlling for individual facial features through same-character methodology."
- **Problem:** The same-character control was introduced in Study 2 (and carried into 2b), not applied across Study 1's 72 stimuli. As written, it credits the design feature to the whole programme.
- **Suggested fix:** "…with a second study adding anger and using the same synthetic 'character' across conditions to control for individual facial features."

**Issue B. Bias domains beyond what was manipulated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "removes a confound that has dogged studies of weight bias, racial bias and age bias"
- **Abstract says:** The stimuli "systematically varied body weight and emotional expressions"; human validation confirmed perception of "target emotions, body weight, realism, age".
- **Problem:** Only weight and expression were manipulated. Racial and age bias applications are plausible extensions, not demonstrated capability.
- **Suggested fix:** "removes a confound that has dogged studies of weight bias — and in principle could be extended to race and age."

*Unverifiable from abstract only:* the caveat "participant samples are online panels" — recruitment method is not described in the abstract.

### Study 7: Sleep doctors watching the same airway video often disagree — and off-the-shelf AI doesn't fix it
**PMID:** 42766212 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

20 DISE recordings, 12 raters (7 junior, 5 senior), one general-purpose AI model without task-specific training, VOTE classification, weighted κ 0.255–0.425 for obstruction grading, pattern classification more reproducible, senior agreement with expert consensus 70.0–90.0% vs AI 35.0–85.0%, treatment exact match 70% / 55% / 50% — all accurately transcribed. Federico Leone / Istituto Auxologico Italiano correct.

**Issue A. "Performs in the range of junior clinicians" overstates the comparison** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "a generic AI model, given no training on the task, already performs in the range of junior clinicians" / "an untrained general-purpose model landed roughly where a junior specialist did"
- **Abstract says:** Consensus-agreement figures are given for senior raters and AI only (70.0–90.0% vs 35.0–85.0%); juniors are compared only on treatment exact match (55% vs AI 50%) and Jaccard (0.833 vs 0.742). Conclusion: the AI "did not match expert performance in complex decision-making."
- **Problem:** The junior-equivalence claim rests on one outcome (treatment recommendations), where the AI was still lower on both metrics. The abstract reports no junior-vs-consensus grading figure to support the broader claim, and the AI's grading floor of 35% is well below any senior figure. As the hook of the pitch, this is the kind of "more exciting result the reader might assume" the digest should not import.
- **Suggested fix:** "a generic AI model, with no training on the task, came close to junior clinicians on treatment recommendations specifically (50% exact agreement vs 55%), while falling short of experts on obstruction grading."

### Study 8: Brain waves may be the reason AI can't be conscious, argues a leading philosopher of mind
**PMID:** 42750504 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Block's claim is rendered faithfully and with appropriate hedging: "Brain waves driven by electrochemical processing may be necessary for consciousness, and if so, that will challenge consciousness in artificial intelligence (AI) and perhaps in some invertebrates." The caveats correctly identify it as a data-free, explicitly speculative commentary, and the NS fit of 5/10 is honest.

**Issue A. Surrounding-issue details not verifiable from this abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a commentary on Anil Seth's target article on biological naturalism and conscious AI"; "The same issue carries opposing commentaries from Blake Richards and Blaise Agüera y Arcas (Mila/Google) and from Lenore and Manuel Blum (Carnegie Mellon), both arguing that prediction, homeostasis and autopoiesis are inherently computational"
- **Abstract says:** Only Block's two-sentence commentary abstract is supplied; no target article, no companion commentaries, no author affiliations beyond NYU.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only. Plausible for a BBS commentary issue, but the target-article authorship, the co-commentators' names and affiliations, and their specific arguments (including "argues the opposite from computability theory" in the pitch angle) all need confirming against the issue's table of contents before a pitch relies on them.
- **Suggested fix:** Mark these lines "to verify" and confirm against the BBS issue contents.

### Study 9: Chatbots are being told about suicidal thoughts — and they don't push people toward help
**PMID:** 42298343 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Correctly identified as a Rutgers open-forum/commentary piece with no new data; caveats are appropriately strict and the "Do not frame chatbots as a clinical intervention" note is good practice. *Psychiatr Serv* 2026 Sep 1;77(9):891-893, Epub 16 Jun 2026 — correct.

**Issue A. Digest sidelines the paper's harmful-guidance claim** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The framing reorients a debate currently stuck on whether chatbots give harmful advice, toward what a chatbot should actively try to achieve in a crisis conversation."
- **Abstract says:** "they are not reliably equipped to respond effectively and often even provide harmful or unsafe guidance."
- **Problem:** The digest presents the harmful-advice question as the old debate the authors move past, when the authors in fact assert harmful guidance as a current failure and build on it. A writer pitching from this line would misstate the paper's position.
- **Suggested fix:** "The authors argue current systems not only give harmful or unsafe guidance at times, but also miss the step that matters most — getting the user to tell another person."

**Issue B. "Most reliably improves safety" overstates the evidential claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "they are not built to do the one thing that most reliably improves safety: prompting the user to tell a real person"
- **Abstract says:** Disclosure to another person is "a necessary step for safety and support"; the authors offer "an empirically grounded direction."
- **Problem:** "Necessary step" is not the same as "the one thing that most reliably improves safety," which implies a comparative effectiveness ranking the abstract does not make.
- **Suggested fix:** "they are not built to do what the authors call a necessary step for safety: prompting the user to tell a real person"

### Study 10: A questionnaire for spotting risky student drinking was built with help from ChatGPT
**PMID:** 42473812 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. 599 students, 59-item pool spanning behavioural, psychological, social and academic content, 15 items selected via ChatGPT-supported algorithms (SARAS-15), logistic regression 93.5% accuracy, AUCs of 0.88 (intermediate), 0.93 (high) and 0.96 (low) correctly summarized as 0.88–0.96 across three tiers, Cronbach's α = 0.811, AUDIT r = 0.861 — all match. Framing is consistent with the abstract, and the caveats correctly identify the circular validation (ML metrics derived from the same dataset used for item selection) and the absence of a clinical diagnostic anchor — a limitation the abstract's own confident conclusion glosses over. NS fit 5/10 is well judged.

### Study 11: Office ergonomics AI works well in the lab — then loses accuracy on new people
**PMID:** 42669165 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Six models, 810-worker training set, independent 74-participant global validation sample, nine anatomical regions, F1/AUC-ROC, SHAP interpretation, comparable binary vs ordinal performance — all correct. QUT and Nektarios Karanikas correct.

**Issue A. Headline and novelty label invert the paper's own conclusion** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Office ergonomics AI works well in the lab — then loses accuracy on new people"; "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research"; pitch angle "Lead with the deflation."
- **Abstract says:** "Generalisability varied by anatomical region, with moderate performance for high-prevalence regions (neck and lower back) and reduced accuracy for low-prevalence regions… These findings highlight the need for region-specific validation and support the feasibility of simplified, interpretable, and scalable AI-based tools." Plain-language summary: "AI-based models can support ergonomic risk assessment."
- **Problem:** Two problems. First, nothing in this abstract establishes that the models "worked well" originally — performance on external data was only "moderate" even in the best regions, so there is no documented lab-to-field fall to lead with. Second, the authors frame the result as supporting feasibility with caveats, not as overturning anything; "Overturns prior research" misrepresents both this paper and its relationship to the prior work, which is by the same group.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Office ergonomics AI generalizes unevenly — fine for necks and backs, worse elsewhere." Novelty: "External validation." Pitch: "Lead with the unevenness — a model that transfers moderately well for common complaints and poorly for rare ones."

**Issue B. Effect-size language slightly inflated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Generalization varied sharply by body region… substantially worse for low-prevalence regions"
- **Abstract says:** "Generalisability varied by anatomical region… reduced accuracy for low-prevalence regions."
- **Problem:** "Sharply" and "substantially" add magnitude the abstract does not quantify.
- **Suggested fix:** "Generalization varied by body region: moderate for high-prevalence areas such as neck and lower back, reduced for low-prevalence regions."

### Study 12: A new theory says hidden depression leaks out through your phone at night
**PMID:** 42753424 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. The three-stage Digital Leakage Effect (daytime masking depleting prefrontal executive resources; evening ego depletion and circadian decline reducing inhibitory control; nocturnal leakage via sleep fragmentation, keystroke kinematics and semantic-temporal divergence), the mapping of all nine DSM-5 criteria to passive biomarkers, the WEIRD-model cultural-calibration argument, and the "up to 60% of suicide decedents denying suicidal ideation during their final clinical encounter" figure all match the abstract. Lead author and institution correct. The digest is explicit that this is a narrative review with no empirical test, the pitch angle instructs the writer to say so, and the federated-learning/dynamic-consent proposal is correctly attributed to the authors. NS fit 4/10 is appropriate for a no-data theory piece.
