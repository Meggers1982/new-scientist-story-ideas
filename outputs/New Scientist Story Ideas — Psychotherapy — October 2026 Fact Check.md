### Study 1: Talking therapy rooted in Freud improves daily functioning in people with schizophrenia
**PMID:** 42716057 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Core study facts check out: 130 randomized (65 MPP-S+TAU / 64 TAU; one withdrew consent), outpatients aged 18–64 with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder, single-centre at the Charité (St Hedwig Hospital, Berlin), assessor-masked, minimum 30 sessions, Mini-ICF-APP at 24 and 36 months, between-group differences −3·45 (p=0·0011) and −4·07 (p=0·0002), similar adverse event frequency, two suicides, no lived-experience involvement, psychoanalytic funders. All accurate.

**Issue A. Unverifiable superlatives in "Why it matters"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Psychodynamic therapy has been largely excluded from schizophrenia guidelines for decades on the grounds that it lacks evidence and might even destabilize patients; this is the first adequately powered, long-horizon trial to suggest otherwise"
- **Abstract says:** "Despite long-standing clinical use, psychodynamic psychotherapy has little empirical support compared with other psychosocial treatments for people with schizophrenia-spectrum disorders."
- **Problem:** The abstract supports "little empirical support" but says nothing about guideline exclusion, destabilization fears, or this being the first adequately powered trial. These may be true from the literature, but they are not verifiable here and "first adequately powered" is the kind of claim a competitor will check.
- **Suggested fix:** "Psychodynamic psychotherapy has long been used clinically for schizophrenia-spectrum disorders but, as the authors note, has had little empirical support relative to other psychosocial treatments; this preregistered trial is a rare long-horizon test of it."

**Issue B. Implied null on symptoms** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the benefit was in everyday functioning rather than symptom counts"
- **Abstract says:** The primary outcome was psychosocial functioning (Mini-ICF-APP); no symptom outcomes are reported in the abstract.
- **Problem:** "Rather than symptom counts" implies symptoms were measured and did not improve. The abstract neither confirms nor denies that. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "the trial's primary outcome was everyday psychosocial functioning rather than symptom severity."

### Study 2: Comfortable therapy sessions beat gruelling ones for getting better
**PMID:** 42530984 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N (84 clients, 1,338 sessions, 33 novice therapists), the between-person smoothness effect, the within-person depth effect and the within-person smoothness uptick are all reported correctly.

**Issue A. Causal framing on correlational, naturalistic data** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Comfortable therapy sessions beat gruelling ones for getting better" … "The data say that's the session doing its job."
- **Abstract says:** "clients who generally experienced smoother, more comfortable therapeutic interactions demonstrated significantly better overall symptom trajectories" — a between-person association in a naturalistic training-clinic dataset.
- **Problem:** The headline and pitch assert that smooth sessions produce recovery. This is a between-person correlation; clients who are improving may simply rate sessions as smoother. The digest's own Caveats say direction of effect "cannot be settled," so the body text contradicts the caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Clients who find therapy comfortable fare better — the opposite of 'no pain, no gain'". Pitch: "The data suggest that's not a wasted hour: comfort tracked better outcomes, though the study can't say which came first."

**Issue B. "Next week" vs. next session** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a session that was smoother than usual was followed by a slight *uptick* in symptoms the next week"
- **Abstract says:** "within-person smoothness was associated with a slight increase in next-session symptoms in the autoregressive model (b = 0.05, p = .001)"
- **Problem:** The abstract specifies next *session*, not next week; weekly spacing is assumed, not stated.
- **Suggested fix:** "...was followed by a slight *uptick* in symptoms at the next session."

**Issue C. NS fit score too high for the evidence** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10"
- **Problem:** 84 clients in a single university training clinic, novice therapists, both predictor and outcome client self-reported, no randomization, and the headline effect is between-person. A 7 overstates the robustness of a result being pitched as overturning folk wisdom about therapy.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 5/10, with the rationale rewritten to lead on the counterintuitive hook while noting the design is naturalistic and entirely self-reported.

### Study 3: Therapy for childhood OCD works — but not by fixing how children think
**PMID:** 42800322 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Design, N (130 randomized, 90 with usable neurocognitive data, 87 matched controls), 14 sessions of family-based CBT vs psychoeducation plus relaxation training, week-16 endpoint, null findings on differential treatment effect, mechanism and baseline prediction/moderation are all correct, as is the funding/employment disclosure in Caveats.

**Issue A. Symptom improvement not reported in this abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "CBT helped these children, and relaxation training helped them too"
- **Abstract says:** Reports neurocognitive outcomes and that "Symptom alleviation was neither predicted nor moderated by baseline neurocognitive performance"; it does not report the magnitude or significance of symptom improvement in either arm.
- **Problem:** The pitch's premise (both therapies worked) comes from the parent TECTO trial, not this abstract. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** Attribute it: "In the parent TECTO trial both arms showed symptom improvement [verify against the main trial report]; here, neither improvement tracked neurocognitive change."

**Issue B. Pre-selected vs exploratory outcome conflated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Children with OCD improved modestly on decision-making and processing speed relative to controls"
- **Abstract says:** "moderate improvement after treatment, relative to controls, on one pre-selected outcome (decision-making; ges = .020) and small improvement on one exploratory outcome (processing speed; ges = .010)"
- **Problem:** The two findings carry different evidential weight; processing speed was exploratory and the effect smaller.
- **Suggested fix:** "Children with OCD improved moderately on one pre-specified measure (decision-making) and slightly on an exploratory one (processing speed), relative to controls."

**Issue C. "Needs revising" overstates the authors' conclusion** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "if cognitive change and symptom change float free of each other, the circuit-based story needs revising"
- **Abstract says:** The findings "raise questions about the clinical relevance of these changes and whether performance-based neurocognitive differences represent clinically meaningful mechanisms".
- **Problem:** A null mechanism result in 90 children raises questions; it doesn't establish that a neurobiological model is wrong.
- **Suggested fix:** "...the authors argue this raises questions about whether these cognitive differences are a clinically meaningful mechanism at all."

### Study 4: Shame drives symptoms — and makes kindness hard to accept
**PMID:** 42775480 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=1,134 inpatients at Modum Bad, weekly self-reports, the reciprocal shame–distress and fear-of-compassion–distress relationships, the one-directional shame → fear of compassion finding, and the sensitivity-analysis variation are all reported accurately.

**Issue A. Causal headline on within-person observational data** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Shame drives symptoms — and makes kindness hard to accept"
- **Abstract says:** "reciprocal predictive relationships were found between shame and symptom distress... Shame also predicted fear of receiving compassion, but not vice versa" and "shame and fear of receiving compassion are dynamically involved in symptom change".
- **Problem:** "Drives" and "makes" are causal verbs applied to temporal-prediction data with no randomization — exactly what the digest's own Caveats warn against. Note also that shame and distress were *reciprocal*, so "shame drives symptoms" picks one arm of a two-way relationship.
- **Suggested fix:** "Shame and distress feed each other — and shame comes before the fear of being treated kindly".

### Study 5: People believe in therapy more than they believe in their own therapy
**PMID:** 42789775 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=270, the Expectations of Active Processes in Psychotherapy Scale completed twice (general first), the uniform general > personal gap, the largest gap on tools for cognitive control, and the two significant moderators with η² values 0.029 and 0.020 are all correct.

**Issue A. "Clinicians are measuring the wrong kind" is the digest's inference, not the study's** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Expectancy is one of the better-evidenced predictors of therapy outcome, and this suggests clinicians are measuring the wrong kind"
- **Abstract says:** "The results suggest that patients, especially those with more severe problems, may require strong, personalized efforts to instill confidence in the therapeutic process."
- **Problem:** The study collected no outcome data, so it cannot show that one type of expectancy measure predicts outcome better than another. The digest's Caveats acknowledge the missing outcome data, but the body text asserts a measurement implication the data cannot support.
- **Suggested fix:** "Expectancy is one of the better-evidenced predictors of therapy outcome; this raises the untested possibility that general endorsement of therapy masks private doubt — something the authors say warrants personalized efforts to build confidence."

### Study 6: Borderline personality disorder doesn't blunt the effect of ketamine's cousin
**PMID:** 42322927 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. The four Italian centres, N=90 (45 with comorbid BPD, 45 with no personality disorder), six-month MADRS trajectory, faster early improvement in the BPD group, higher response rates from month one, remission 48.9% vs 57.8% (p=0.398), falls in anxiety and impulsivity, no MoCA worsening and zero dropouts all match. Framing is appropriately observational and the Caveats flag the non-randomized design.

### Study 7: Depressed patients who can't stand up for themselves are more likely to be readmitted
**PMID:** 42736557 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. N=416 MDD inpatients, AHI and BDI-II at admission and discharge, mean z = 1.40, significant decreases in both during treatment, and discharge AHI associated with symptom change, 6-month depression and one-year readmission are all correct. The digest's associational language and explicit "correlation, not a prescription" instruction in the pitch match the abstract's own statement that the design does not permit causal conclusions.

### Study 8: Giving people feedback on a two-week eye-movement task cuts binge eating — briefly
**PMID:** 42698100 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=67, three arms, six sessions of a food-modified antisaccade task over two weeks, the 4- and 12-week primary outcome, all three groups improving, feedback superiority at 4 weeks washing out by 12 — all correct.

**Issue A. "Isolates the active ingredient" overstates a proof-of-principle result** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It isolates feedback — not the training itself — as the active ingredient in impulse-control training for binge eating"
- **Abstract says:** "Feedback contributes to short term reductions in binge-eating behavior and may inform development of mechanism-based interventions" — in a "proof-of-principle trial" of 67 patients where the advantage disappeared by 12 weeks.
- **Problem:** "Isolates… the active ingredient" is a mechanism claim; the authors claim only a short-term contribution, and with ~22 per arm the trial is underpowered to rule out a training effect. The digest's own headline ("briefly") is more accurate than its "Why it matters".
- **Suggested fix:** "It points to feedback, rather than the task itself, as what produced the short-term advantage — and simultaneously shows the untrained control group also binged less, which should give pause to anyone reading uncontrolled app trials."

### Study 9: Jungian therapy, long dismissed as unevidenced, shows measurable gains
**PMID:** 42779379 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=104, German training institute, pre-post design, ISR/FLZ/OPD-SFK, d = 0.555–1.174 for depression, anxiety and obsessive-compulsive symptoms, improvements in self-perception and relational functioning, and the null for eating disorder symptoms are all accurate. The no-control-group limitation is flagged in both the body and the Caveats.

**Issue A. Unverifiable comparative and historical claims in the pitch** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Carl Jung's therapy has barely been measured in a century; here is a rare attempt, done by trainees, with effect sizes in the range reported for mainstream approaches."
- **Abstract says:** Reports effect sizes of d = 0.555–1.174 within this sample; makes no comparison to other therapies and no claim about a century of non-measurement.
- **Problem:** The benchmark against "mainstream approaches" is an uncontrolled pre-post comparison to effect sizes from other literatures, which is not a like-for-like comparison, and the "barely measured in a century" line is rhetorical rather than sourced.
- **Suggested fix:** "Jungian therapy has rarely been subjected to standardized outcome measurement; here is one attempt, in a training institute, reporting moderate-to-large pre-post improvements — with no control group to compare them against."

### Study 10: A sniff of oxytocin changes how vividly people with BPD imagine compassion
**PMID:** 42372695 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=18, 24 IU intranasal oxytocin or placebo before guided compassion meditation, five weekly sessions, practice quality at T1 and T5, the imagination-dimension effect with nulls on total and somatic scores, and no adverse effects are all correct, and the Caveats are appropriately blunt.

**Issue A. Causal verb in the headline for a pilot association** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A sniff of oxytocin changes how vividly people with BPD imagine compassion"
- **Abstract says:** "oxytocin was associated with higher endpoint scores on the imagination dimension of compassion practice quality" — described throughout as a pilot, with the authors calling for "further controlled studies with larger samples".
- **Problem:** "Changes" asserts an established effect from an 18-person pilot with a single significant sub-scale; the authors use "associated with" and "may facilitate".
- **Suggested fix:** "A sniff of oxytocin was linked to more vivid compassion imagery in people with BPD — in 18 of them".

**Issue B. Affiliation for one named contact** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call Joaquim Soler or Juan Carlos Pascual at Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau in Barcelona"
- **Abstract says:** Soler is listed at Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau; Pascual is listed at CIBERSAM and the "Mental Health and Psychiatry Department, Consorci Hospitalari de Vic".
- **Problem:** Pascual's affiliation on this paper is not Sant Pau, which matters for contacting him.
- **Suggested fix:** "Call Joaquim Soler at Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau, or Juan Carlos Pascual (CIBERSAM / Consorci Hospitalari de Vic)."

### Study 11: Why people from minority ethnic backgrounds walk away from therapy
**PMID:** 42726438 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

13 studies, 286 participants, the ~50% premature-ending figure, the disproportionate rate among ethnically minoritised adults, and all six themes are reported accurately.

**Issue A. Reframing is stronger than the synthesis states** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Across studies, leaving was described not as giving up but as a reasoned response to care that did not fit" … "points to shared decision-making and flexible delivery rather than motivational interventions"
- **Abstract says:** "Discontinuation was not solely attributable to lack of motivation but was strongly influenced by structural inequalities, cultural incongruence in care, ruptures in the therapeutic relationship, and limited patient autonomy," and recommends "Culturally responsive practice, stronger therapeutic alliances, shared decision-making, and flexible models of care delivery".
- **Problem:** "Not solely attributable to lack of motivation" is weaker than "not giving up but a reasoned exit," and the abstract's recommendations are additive, not framed as a replacement for motivational approaches.
- **Suggested fix:** "Across studies, leaving was described as shaped by structural, cultural and relational factors rather than being solely a matter of motivation" … "points to culturally responsive practice, stronger alliances, shared decision-making and more flexible delivery."

### Study 12: Older people who use cannabis for pain are more careful with it than those using it for mood
**PMID:** 42341079 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

N=471 adults aged 55+, social-media recruitment, self-reported legal medical cannabis use, the PBSM/CUDIT measures, and the directions of all three associations plus the significant indirect pathways through PBS are correctly reported.

**Issue A. Pitch premise contradicts the study's own finding** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Same drug, same dose, different risk — depending on what you're treating."
- **Abstract says:** "Somatic use justifications were associated with greater PBS use, **less cannabis use**, and lower CUDIT scores. Affective use justifications were associated with less PBS use, **more cannabis use**, and higher CUDIT scores."
- **Problem:** Dose was *not* the same between the two motive groups — differing consumption was one of the three findings. The hook asserts the opposite of what the study measured.
- **Suggested fix:** "Same drug, different habits — and different risk — depending on what you're treating: older adults using cannabis for physical complaints used less of it and used more protective strategies."

**Issue B. Untested comparative claim about predictive strength** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "this suggests the reason someone reaches for it — ache versus anxiety — predicts their risk profile better than how much they take"
- **Abstract says:** Motives were associated with PBS use, cannabis use *and* CUDIT scores, with PBS mediating; no test compares the predictive strength of motive against quantity consumed.
- **Problem:** The model treats consumption as an outcome of motive, not as a competing predictor, so "better than how much they take" is a comparison the study did not make.
- **Suggested fix:** "this suggests the reason someone reaches for it — ache versus anxiety — is associated with both how much they use and how carefully they use it."

**Issue C. Unsourced background and illustrative detail** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Medical cannabis use is rising fastest among older adults" … "the protective habits (fixed limits, not using alone, avoiding mornings)"
- **Abstract says:** "Medical cannabis use is often motivated by somatic or affective concerns, and both are common among older adults"; protective strategies are measured with the PBSM but no example items are given.
- **Problem:** Both the growth claim and the specific protective behaviors are unverifiable from the abstract alone and would need sourcing before a pitch.
- **Suggested fix:** Attribute the growth statistic to a named external source, and either check the PBSM item list or write "protective habits such as limiting quantity" with a caveat that specific items need confirming.

### Study 13: The word "I" in your messages tracks how well you get on with your therapist
**PMID:** 42755118 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. The three pilot RCTs' null main effect, the pooled Ns (289 with PHQ-8 slopes, 301 in the baseline database), the platform-count moderation, the partial r = .39 for first-person singular pronoun use and alliance, the seven-intervention-patient caveat on the racial identity finding, and the authors' calls for patient-preference measurement and dashboard-engagement standardization are all correctly represented. The hypothesis-generating framing is explicit in both pitch and caveats.

### Study 14: A system for classifying group therapy sessions fails to replicate
**PMID:** 42295247 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. 98 individuals with a history of problematic alcohol use, 19 six-session groups focused on identity transitions or career development, climate rated after each session, failed k-means replication of Li et al.'s five-category taxonomy, the three exploratory types named exactly as in the abstract, and the absence of a perseveration effect or climate valence continuity all match. The "conceptual replication" caveat is correctly stated.
