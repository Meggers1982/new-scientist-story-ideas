### 1. Land acknowledgements raise awareness of Indigenous groups but can dampen interest in their culture

**Journal:** *Nature Human Behaviour* | **Published:** 25 September 2026
**PMID:** 42791336 | **DOI:** 10.1038/s41562-026-02520-6
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — large pre-registered-style randomized experiments across two countries, with a result that cuts against what both supporters and critics assume.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Two randomized experiments on non-Indigenous adults in Australia (N=1,724) and the US (N=1,773) tested what happens when people are exposed to a land acknowledgement. Exposure reliably increased awareness of which Indigenous groups are local to the area — but in some cases reduced stated interest in Indigenous culture and heritage, and moved none of the political needles, including collective guilt and support for reparations. Further experiments (N=3,638) showed that adopting acknowledgements gives institutions under public scrutiny a small reputational boost, while costlier commitments — money, and giving Indigenous people a seat in decision-making — had substantially larger effects.

**Why it matters:** This is the first solid causal evidence on a ritual that has become near-ubiquitous at conferences, sporting events and university lectures, and it suggests the main beneficiary is the institution rather than the cause.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the familiar moment before a keynote when a speaker reads a land acknowledgement from a card — then reveal that across nearly 7,000 people, the statement changed what the audience knew but not what they wanted, and in some cases made them *less* curious. The hook is the "symbolic versus costly signaling" contrast: participants rewarded institutions for the cheap gesture, but the expensive commitments were what shifted attitudes. Call Lachlan McNamee at Monash University.
- **Wider angle:** A politics/feature piece for a general-interest or current-affairs outlet on the measurement of symbolic politics more broadly — DEI statements, corporate pledges, flag-flying — and the emerging experimental literature on when symbols substitute for substance.

**Caveats:** Online survey experiments measuring stated attitudes, not behavior; short-term exposure only; effects on non-Indigenous audiences only, and the paper does not address what acknowledgements mean to Indigenous people themselves.

### 2. Angry voters pick dominant-looking leaders, frightened voters pick trustworthy-looking ones

**Journal:** *Cognition and Emotion* | **Published:** 10 October 2026
**PMID:** 42858097 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02699931.2026.2739925
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — clean emotional mechanism and an obvious election hook; limited by modest effect sizes and computer-generated faces.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** A balanced sample of US Republicans and Democrats named the real political issue that made them most angry and the one that most frightened them, then chose, from computer-generated faces varying in dominance and trustworthiness, which leader would best handle it. When angry, people picked the more dominant, less trustworthy-looking face 49.5% of the time versus 46.5% when afraid. Modeling showed the two emotions worked differently: anger increased the weight placed on dominance cues and decreased the weight on trustworthiness, while fear selectively ramped up reliance on trustworthiness.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the emotional framing of a campaign issue — whether coverage makes you furious or scared — can tilt which kind of face reads as "the right person for the job."

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the mechanism, not the headline percentage: anger and fear are both negative, both high-arousal, and yet they pull leader preference in opposite directions. Open on a voter scrolling two news stories about the same issue, one outraging, one alarming. Worth calling Mariana Von Mohr at the Centre for the Politics of Feelings, University of London.
- **Wider angle:** A longer piece for a media or political-science outlet on the "outrage economy" — if angry audiences reward dominance, what does an engagement-optimized news feed do to the kind of candidate that rises? Pair with existing work on facial inference and election outcomes.

**Caveats:** Small absolute difference between conditions (3 percentage points); hypothetical choices using synthetic faces rather than real candidates; US sample; self-selected issues.

### 3. Offered good and bad stories about a drug, people deliberately choose the bad ones — and then expect side effects

**Journal:** *Behaviour Research and Therapy* | **Published:** 6 October 2026
**PMID:** 42843138 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.brat.2026.105167
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — three studies, a genuinely new cognitive mechanism ("valenced interrogation bias"), and everyone recognizes the behavior in themselves.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Across three online studies, participants read a leaflet about a medication and were then free to choose how many positive and how many negative first-person anecdotes about side effects to read. People consistently selected more negative than positive accounts. Those who selected more negative anecdotes went on to report stronger expectations of side effects, more worry about them, and — with moderate evidence — less willingness to take the drug.

**Why it matters:** Previous nocebo research mostly pushed negative information at passive participants; this shows people actively dig toward the scary material, which may be a cognitive mechanism driving real-world side-effect expectations and treatment refusal.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on someone who has just been prescribed a statin and opens a browser tab. The surprise is that the nocebo effect may begin before any information is received — in the choice of what to read. Frame as "self-inflicted nocebo" and ask whether warning people about the bias changes their clicking. Call Tessa Rooney or Ben Colagiuri at the University of Sydney.
- **Wider angle:** A health-communication story for a medical or consumer outlet: how patient information leaflets, review sites and pharmacy apps might be redesigned around the fact that patients preferentially sample negative anecdotes — and what regulators would make of curating that balance.

**Caveats:** Online, non-clinical samples taking hypothetical medications; expectations and willingness were self-reported with no actual drug administration or symptom measurement.

### 4. Two heads don't average — they pick a winner

**Journal:** *Cognitive Psychology* | **Published:** 1 October 2026
**PMID:** 42822293 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cogpsych.2026.101849
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — directly challenges the Bayesian-averaging orthodoxy about group judgment, with model fits to back it.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Pairs of participants privately estimated a continuous quantity — the direction of a moving dot field — and then agreed a joint answer. Joint answers beat individual ones, as the "wisdom of the dyad" literature predicts, but they were usually clustered near one member's estimate rather than between the two. Model comparison favored an account in which the pair builds a combined, bimodal probability distribution and effectively samples a winner from it, with both current-trial and historical accuracy predicting whose number carried the day.

**Why it matters:** The dominant model of collaborative judgment says groups compute a confidence-weighted average; this says they more often make a categorical choice about whom to believe — which changes predictions about when groups help and when they inherit one person's error.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the deflation of "two heads are better than one" — they are, but not for the reason textbooks say. Open on a cockpit, jury room or clinical case conference where a continuous judgment (how far, how long, how much) has to be settled. The researchers also show that reputation built over past trials determines who wins, which is the second surprise. Call Simon Farrell at the University of Western Australia.
- **Wider angle:** An organizational-behavior feature for a business or management outlet: if teams resolve disagreement by deferring to a perceived expert rather than splitting the difference, meeting structures designed around consensus averaging may be solving the wrong problem.

**Caveats:** Highly abstract perceptual task; dyads only, not larger groups; conclusions rest on comparison between two candidate models rather than direct measurement of the process.

### 5. People are better at judging a food's nutritional quality than its calories — but far more confident about the calories

**Journal:** *Cognition* | **Published:** 12 October 2026
**PMID:** 42863397 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cognition.2026.106744
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a crisp accuracy–confidence dissociation that readers can test on themselves; held back by a small sample and a brand-new task.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Thirty-two volunteers completed "MetaBites," a new two-choice task comparing foods either on caloric density or on nutritional quality (scored by the Nutrient-Rich Foods 9.3 index), with difficulty adjusted on the fly and confidence rated at trial, block and whole-task level. Participants were objectively more accurate on nutritional quality, yet reported higher confidence on calorie judgments, and their global confidence sagged in the nutrition condition while holding steady for calories. Familiar foods were systematically judged more nutritious and less calorific; liking, rather than familiarity, drove trial-level confidence.

**Why it matters:** The notorious gap between knowing about nutrition and eating accordingly may partly be a metacognitive problem — people's confidence is tracking the wrong dimension of food, and is inflated by liking.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open with a supermarket shelf and two products, and the claim that you probably know which is more nutritious and probably don't believe yourself. The hook is that confidence attaches to calories — the number culture has trained us on — while accuracy sits elsewhere, and that simply liking a food makes you surer about it. Call Kelly Hoogervorst or Micah Allen at Aarhus University.
- **Wider angle:** A food-policy piece for a health or consumer-affairs outlet on front-of-pack labeling: calorie counts may be feeding confidence rather than competence, and nutrient-profile scores like Nutri-Score are competing against an entrenched metacognitive habit.

**Caveats:** Small N (32), healthy volunteers, single center; novel task with validity established only within this paper; no measurement of what participants actually ate.

### 6. We work hardest when we believe our effort can change the outcome — and depressive traits sharpen that calculus

**Journal:** *Cognitive, Affective & Behavioral Neuroscience* | **Published:** October 2026 (epub 31 August 2026)
**PMID:** 42675261 | **DOI:** 10.3758/s13415-026-01484-4
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a new task that isolates controllability from reward rate, plus a counterintuitive depression finding; modest sample.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Sixty-one participants completed the Controllability and Mental Effort Task (CoMET), designed so that how much control you have over outcomes varies independently of how much reward is available on average. People exerted more mental effort when the environment was controllable and tracked changes in controllability dynamically; in reward-poor contexts, they pushed harder when a particular offer was unusually good. Drift-diffusion modeling showed controllability both speeded value integration and raised the decision threshold. Participants with higher subclinical depression traits were *more* sensitive to controllability, becoming especially unmotivated in uncontrollable but high-reward settings.

**Why it matters:** It separates two things usually confounded in motivation research — how rich an environment is versus how much say you have in it — and hints that low mood may involve over-reading uncontrollability rather than simply blunted reward sensitivity.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead on the counterintuitive bit: people with more depressive traits were not globally less responsive, they were *more* attuned to whether effort would pay off, and it was the rich-but-uncontrollable situation that flattened them. Open on a job where the rewards are large but promotion is arbitrary. Call Bingjie Li or Eliana Vassena at Radboud University.
- **Wider angle:** A workplace-science feature for a business or psychology outlet on "learned helplessness, rebooted" — what controllability research implies for autonomy at work, algorithmic management, and gig platforms where output and reward are decoupled.

**Caveats:** Small N (61); subclinical depression traits measured by self-report in a non-clinical sample; laboratory task with monetary incentives, cross-sectional.

### 7. Steep delay discounting predicts gambling harm — but mostly through the stories gamblers tell themselves

**Journal:** *Journal of Gambling Studies* | **Published:** 1 October 2026
**PMID:** 42821202 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10899-026-10557-5
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — unusually large sample of active gamblers and a clear mediational story; cross-sectional design limits the claim.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** In 1,484 active gamblers, the researchers measured delay discounting — the tendency to prefer smaller immediate rewards over larger delayed ones — alongside gambling-related cognitive distortions, gambling frequency and problem severity. Steeper discounting predicted problem severity even after controlling for education and socioeconomic status, but the association was predominantly *indirect*: it ran through specific distortions, chiefly loss-of-control beliefs and interpretive bias about what gambling outcomes mean.

**Why it matters:** It links a laboratory impulsivity marker to real harm via the beliefs people form about their own play, which points treatment at the interpretations rather than at impulsivity per se.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is that impulsivity alone doesn't do the damage — what matters is how a near-miss or a losing streak gets interpreted. Open on a person at a slot machine explaining why the next spin is "due." N=1,484 active gamblers makes this a substantial dataset. Call Marija Stanković at the Institute for Medical Research, University of Belgrade.
- **Wider angle:** A regulation story for a policy or investigative outlet: if interpretive bias is the proximal driver of harm, then game design features that manufacture near-misses and illusory control — rather than advertising volume — become the obvious regulatory target.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and correlational (cannot establish causation or temporal order, as the authors state); self-reported gambling severity and distortions; single-country sample of active gamblers.

### 8. Teenagers' thinking gets faster and sharper with age — and puberty barely explains it

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** 2 October 2026
**PMID:** 42826555 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107883
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — 4,194 young people in the ABCD Study with longitudinal modeling; the null on puberty is the story, but it's a quieter story.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Using longitudinal data from 4,194 participants aged 9–17 in the ABCD Study, the team decomposed n-back working-memory performance into drift-diffusion parameters and modeled nonlinear development. Improvements came mainly from faster evidence accumulation (drift rate) and shorter non-decision time, with additional shifts in response caution and choice bias suggesting changing strategy. When sociodemographic variables were controlled, pubertal timing and tempo added only weak explanatory power beyond age — related to response times and drift rate in girls only.

**Why it matters:** Much popular writing attributes adolescent cognition and risk-taking to puberty; here, chronological age and processing efficiency do most of the work, with hormonal maturation contributing little extra.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the deflation: "puberty brain" is a weaker explanation of adolescent cognitive change than its cultural prominence suggests, at least for decision efficiency. Open on a 13-year-old and a 16-year-old doing the same memory task, and unpack what drift rate and decision threshold actually mean in plain language. Call Elisabeth Frogner or Lars T. Westlye at the University of Oslo.
- **Wider angle:** An education-focused feature for a parenting or schools outlet on what longitudinal cognitive modeling says about readiness — exam timing, school start ages, and the gap between "faster" and "more cautious" decision-making in teens.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort (cannot establish causation); pubertal status derived from questionnaire-based measures; n-back is a narrow probe of decision-making; US-based cohort.

### 9. The EU's new energy labels are easier to read — and change what you can remember about them

**Journal:** *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* | **Published:** 5 October 2026
**PMID:** 42832023 | **DOI:** 10.3758/s13423-026-03018-y
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — pre-registered, uses a real policy change as the experimental manipulation; the memory theory is a touch abstract for a 600-word piece.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** In a pre-registered experiment, participants rated seven refrigerators while learning their kilowatt-hour consumption, with energy values drawn from positively skewed, negatively skewed or non-skewed distributions. Ratings confirmed that the EU's old label scale (A+++ through D) is psychologically skewed in mental space, while the new A–G scale is roughly linear. After a short delay, the labels were used as retrieval cues: the linear scale produced accurate recall when the underlying values were evenly spread, but imposed excessive linearity when the real values were skewed; the old, skewed scale recalled positively skewed values best.

**Why it matters:** It proposes that retrieval cues don't just index memories, they reshape them by imposing their own distributional structure — and shows that a label redesign decided by policymakers quietly changes what consumers end up remembering.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open in an appliance showroom with the shiny new A–G sticker. The surprise is that a label is not a neutral pointer to a number; the shape of the scale distorts the numbers you can later retrieve, and the "better" label is only better if reality happens to be evenly spread. Call Patrik Sörqvist at Luleå University of Technology.
- **Wider angle:** A design-and-policy piece for a specialist consumer or sustainability outlet on rating scales in general — Nutri-Score, hotel stars, energy ratings, carbon labels — and the cognitive cost of compressing a skewed world into equal-looking categories.

**Caveats:** Laboratory memory task rather than real purchasing; single experiment with a small set of items (seven refrigerators); no measure of actual buying behavior.

### 10. Brain wiring differs by drug of choice — and in cannabis dependence it tracks impulsive choice

**Journal:** *Translational Psychiatry* | **Published:** 9 October 2026
**PMID:** 42855476 | **DOI:** 10.1038/s41398-026-04491-8
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — rare head-to-head comparison across three substances with two imaging methods; small subgroups weaken it.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** A dual-center diffusion MRI study compared white matter microstructure in 87 people with substance use disorders — 48 cannabis, 16 opioid, 23 nicotine — against 53 non-smoking controls, using both diffusion tensor imaging and NODDI. Opioid and cannabis dependence showed convergent, widespread abnormalities across fronto-temporo-parietal and limbic tracts, while nicotine dependence showed more circumscribed, mostly anterior and callosal changes. In cannabis dependence, higher orientation dispersion in the middle longitudinal fasciculus correlated with steeper delay discounting (r=0.41), an effect statistically carried by consumption and present only among daily users.

**Why it matters:** It argues against a single, generic "addicted brain" signature and offers a candidate structural correlate of impulsive choice that appears specific to heavy cannabis use.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the divergence — cannabis looked more like opioids than like nicotine, which few readers would guess. The second hook is that the brain–impulsivity link showed up only in daily cannabis users, a dose-dependent signal in an era of rising potency. Call Gabriele Ende at the Central Institute of Mental Health, Mannheim.
- **Wider angle:** A drug-policy feature for a health or science outlet on what the legalization wave means for research: cannabis neuroimaging samples are still small and cross-sectional, while product potency has moved faster than the evidence base.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional (cannot establish whether differences precede or follow use); very small opioid subgroup (n=16); correlation of r=0.41 in a subsample; controls were non-smokers, which may inflate contrasts.

### 11. Scanning doctors' brains as they decide who qualifies for assisted dying

**Journal:** *Cognitive, Affective & Behavioral Neuroscience* | **Published:** October 2026 (epub 6 May 2026)
**PMID:** 42091837 | **DOI:** 10.3758/s13415-026-01452-y
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — genuinely first-of-its-kind subject matter; fMRI reverse-inference and a student sample cap the score.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Fifty-nine Australian medical students judged hypothetical medical-assistance-in-dying requests in an fMRI scanner, some meeting legal eligibility criteria and some not. Their decisions largely tracked the law. Non-qualifying cases recruited frontoparietal regions associated with response selection and inhibition plus precuneus, temporoparietal junction and angular gyrus, consistent with harder deliberation and more mentalizing; qualifying cases engaged ventromedial prefrontal cortex and showed reduced anterior cingulate–insula connectivity, especially in high-trait-empathy participants, which the authors interpret as a compassionate rather than distressing response. Amygdala activity during qualifying cases was greater in less religious participants.

**Why it matters:** As assisted dying laws expand, this is the first attempt to characterize what happens in a clinician's brain at the moment of decision — early, but it opens a line of enquiry into the moral burden carried by practitioners.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the real scenario: a doctor with a request on the desk that falls just outside the criteria. The surprise is the asymmetry — saying yes within the law looked affectively easier and more compassion-like; saying no was cognitively effortful and mentalizing-heavy. Frame it around clinician moral distress, not around whether MAiD is right. Call Pascal Molenberghs at the Institute for Social Neuroscience, Melbourne.
- **Wider angle:** A medical-ethics feature for a health or long-read outlet on who carries the psychological cost of assisted dying laws, pairing this with workforce data on clinicians who opt out, and with the ethics-consultation literature on moral distress.

**Caveats:** Small N (59); medical students, not practicing clinicians; hypothetical vignettes; fMRI interpretation relies on reverse inference; Australian legal context only.

### 12. Playing for points instead of cash makes people try less hard — even in psychosis research

**Journal:** *Schizophrenia Research* | **Published:** October 2026 (epub 20 July 2026)
**PMID:** 42475772 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.schres.2026.07.009
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — large sample, randomized reward manipulation, and an awkward methodological implication for an entire task literature.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** 233 people at clinical high risk for psychosis and 157 healthy controls were randomized to earn either real money or points on the Effort Expenditure for Rewards Task. Across both groups, money produced more effort than points. Clinical status and negative-symptom severity did not interact with reward type; within the high-risk group, greater negative symptoms predicted less effort regardless of what was on offer. Exploratorily, better-off participants in the money condition worked *less* on the high-reward, high-probability trials.

**Why it matters:** Effort-based decision tasks are being pushed as cheap, scalable measures of motivation, and this shows the currency changes the answer — and that a participant's household income may quietly set the value of the incentive.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the income finding: the same $4 bonus is a different experiment depending on who is sitting at the keyboard, which is a problem for any psychology study that assumes money means the same thing to everyone. Open on the task itself — rapid button-mashing for small cash. Call Zachary Anderson or Vijay Mittal at Northwestern University.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-and-replication feature for a specialist science outlet on incentive design across behavioral science, covering online panels, gamified cognitive tests and the uncomfortable fact that purchasing power is an uncontrolled variable in reward research.

**Caveats:** Secondary/exploratory analyses for the income effect (p=0.046); single behavioral task; clinical high-risk status is not a diagnosis; US-based multi-site sample.

### 13. People really do compute the average of a set of numbers — they're not just eyeballing the biggest one

**Journal:** *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics* | **Published:** 13 October 2026
**PMID:** 42870117 | **DOI:** 10.3758/s13414-026-03338-0
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 5/10 — settles a real dispute with a clever design, but it's an abstract laboratory question for general readers.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** In two experiments (N=40 each), participants saw a 2×2 grid of Arabic digits and judged under time pressure whether the arithmetic mean was above or below 5. Accuracy and speed scaled with how far the true mean sat from the criterion, and mean distance beat heuristic alternatives — "use the most extreme digit," "count how many are above 5" — in item-level model comparison. In a second experiment the heuristic cues were made uninformative or actively misleading, and the mean-distance effect survived both; heuristics mattered only when uncertainty rose.

**Why it matters:** It supports the idea that the mind can genuinely average symbolic numbers, not merely fake averaging through shortcuts — relevant to anyone interpreting statistics, prices or grades at a glance.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a shopper scanning five prices or a reader skimming star ratings, and ask whether the "gist" they extract is a real average or a trick. The hook is Experiment 2, where the most extreme digit pointed to the wrong answer and people still got it right. Call Daniel Fitousi at Ariel University.
- **Wider angle:** A cognition feature for a specialist outlet on ensemble perception — the broad finding that we extract averages from crowds of faces, sizes and sounds without being able to report individual items — with this as the latest front in the debate over whether that is averaging or heuristics.

**Caveats:** Small N (40 per experiment); not pre-registered (the authors state this explicitly); highly artificial stimuli; two experiments in a single lab.

### 14. Anorexia plus suicidal thoughts shows up as choosing less food you still find tasty

### 15. A night in a sleep lab already contains a decade-long warning about your heart

**Journal:** *Sleep* | **Published:** 9 October 2026
**PMID:** 42852996 | **DOI:** 10.1093/sleep/zsag229
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — large multi-center data with external validation, but it's a risk-prediction model rather than a finding about the mind.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** A deep neural network, originally trained for arrhythmia detection, was fine-tuned on single-lead ECGs recorded during overnight polysomnography in 15,809 Massachusetts General Hospital patients, combined with sleep-stage data, to predict ten-year risk of atrial fibrillation, stroke, heart attack, heart failure and death. **Story angles:**

### 16. Pilots describe what actually saves them when something happens that no checklist covers

**Journal:** *Human Factors* | **Published:** October 2026 (epub 7 May 2026)
**PMID:** 42095443 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00187208261449578
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — vivid, quotable material on decision-making under genuine surprise, but qualitative and recommendation-heavy.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers ran 50 structured knowledge-elicitation interviews with pilots about the events they found most unexpected, how they handled them, and where training fell short. Systems malfunctions, environmental uncertainty and human-related incidents dominated. Pilots credited procedures and checklists, basic hand-flying skills and Crew Resource Management, but also emphasized cognitive strategies — chiefly sustained situational awareness — and said training covers known anomalies while leaving "judgment expertise" under-taught. ## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- *Aggregation is not what people actually do.* Last month's "People reject the utilitarian answer when asked to settle a group's disagreements" (PMID 42759227) showed that summing utilities is the rule people like least; this month's "Two heads don't average — they pick a winner" (PMID 42822293) shows that dyads resolving a continuous estimate also decline to split the difference, instead sampling one member's answer. Two different literatures — moral aggregation and perceptual collaboration — arriving independently at the same deflation of averaging models.
- *Delay discounting keeps mattering, but never on its own.* Previously, "People with more autistic traits take the money now" (PMID 42722131) located the difference in the choice rather than in valuation, and "The brains of anxious, depressed gamblers barely flinch at a loss" (PMID 42779459) tied gambling severity to a subtype-specific signal. This run, "Steep delay discounting predicts gambling harm — but mostly through the stories gamblers tell themselves" (PMID 42821202) finds the discounting–harm link is predominantly indirect, running through interpretive bias, while "Brain wiring differs by drug of choice" (PMID 42855476) attaches discounting to a structural correlate only in daily cannabis users. The marker is robust; its causal role keeps receding behind mediators.
- *Confidence detaches from accuracy.* "Being told you're wrong when you felt sure makes you doubt yourself for a long while afterward" (PMID 42734967) showed feedback recalibrating certainty rather than accuracy; "People are better at judging a food's nutritional quality than its calories — but far more confident about the calories" (PMID 42863397) shows confidence latching onto the wrong dimension entirely, and being inflated by liking.

**Advances**
- The computational decomposition thread from last run — "Time perception in autistic traits looks fine — it's the decision rule that differs" (PMID 42721950), "Autistic people use rewards to guide their decisions exactly as much as anyone else" (PMID 42721846) — now scales up to a 4,194-person developmental cohort in "Teenagers' thinking gets faster and sharper with age — and puberty barely explains it" (PMID 42826555), where drift rate and non-decision time carry the developmental story and puberty adds little.
- Motivation research continues to fractionate. "People who have attempted suicide quit faster after a setback" (PMID 42704954) separated re-engagement from stopping; "We work hardest when we believe our effort can change the outcome" (PMID 42675261) now separates controllability from reward rate, with depressive traits amplifying rather than blunting the calculus.
- Human–AI decision work has moved up a level: from whether AI teammates can be drawn into sharing information (PMID 42708610) to whether people ever check what the AI said at all, in "People rarely check whether an AI advisor is making things up — and generic warnings don't help" (PMID 42759086).

**Counterpoints**
- Last run's account of gambling harm was neural and subtype-based (PMID 42779459: blunted loss processing in emotionally vulnerable patients); this run's is cognitive and belief-based (PMID 42821202: loss-of-control and interpretive distortions). Both are plausible proximal drivers and the two framings imply different interventions — worth flagging if either is written up.
- "Letting advertising change what you care about might be a rational strategy" (PMID 42773691) argued that openness to environmental influence on values is resource-rational. Two new studies complicate that: "Offered good and bad stories about a drug, people deliberately choose the bad ones" (PMID 42843138) shows self-directed sampling pulling systematically toward harm, and "Land acknowledgements raise awareness of Indigenous groups but can dampen interest in their culture" (PMID 42791336) shows exposure moving knowledge without moving — and sometimes reducing — engagement.

**New themes**
- Political and symbolic decision-making enters the batch for the first time: PMIDs 42791336 and "Angry voters pick dominant-looking leaders, frightened voters pick trustworthy-looking ones" (PMID 42858097).
- Decision-making under legal and regulatory vacuum, with no precedent in the previous digest: assisted dying judgments in the scanner (PMID 42091837), a self-written standard of care for off-label ketamine clinics (PMID 42144140), and who carries the pregnancy in shared-motherhood IVF (PMID 42875086).
- Expertise under genuine novelty, also new: pilots on what saves them when no checklist applies (PMID 42095443) and the rule-complexity account of choking (PMID 42875363).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies here converge on a failure that is not about ignorance but about knowing where your own judgment is reliable — and about when to check. People are more accurate on nutrition than calories yet more confident about calories (PMID 42863397); they rarely verify an AI advisor and the ubiquitous "AI can make mistakes" disclaimer is the intervention with the weakest evidence behind it (PMID 42759086); given free choice, they deliberately sample negative drug anecdotes and then expect side effects (PMID 42843138); a redesigned EU energy label reshapes what consumers can later recall rather than neutrally pointing at a number (PMID 42832023); and pairs settling a joint estimate defer to whoever has a track record rather than averaging (PMID 42822293).

**Why pitch this now:** Organizations are currently betting on exactly the two fixes this evidence undercuts — generic warning text on AI interfaces and redesigned rating scales on food and appliances — while the studies suggest the binding constraint is a person's sense of when their own confidence is earned.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the metacognitive gap in everyday choices, following researchers who study not what people get wrong but whether they know to look it up — for general readers who use a chatbot, read a label and skim side-effect forums in the same week, with designers and regulators on what a targeted cue would look like compared with a blanket disclaimer.

**Potential headlines:**
- People judge nutrition better than calories, and trust the calorie number more
- The disclaimer at the bottom of the chat window is the least evidenced safeguard we have
- Knowing when to double-check turns out to be the skill that decides what you get wrong
