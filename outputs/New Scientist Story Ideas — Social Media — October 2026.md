### 1. A scale now exists for "main character syndrome" — and it looks more like identity confusion than narcissism

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** September 4, 2026
**PMID:** 42696901 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107730
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a social-media-born idea gets its first psychometric test, and the result cuts against the obvious "it's just narcissism" reading; limited by cross-sectional student samples.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers at the Lebanese American University built and validated a 10-item questionnaire for "main character syndrome" — the habit of experiencing and staging your life as a protagonist-centered story — across two independent samples of Lebanese young adults (Study 1 N=135; Study 2 N=263). The scale held together as a single dimension with good reliability, and correlated with identity diffusion (r=0.677), social media addiction (r=0.630) and narcissism (r=0.564) while remaining statistically distinct from all three. In the multivariate model, identity diffusion — an unsettled, fragmented sense of self — was the strongest correlate, not narcissism.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that performing your life as a film may be less about self-love than about scaffolding a self that hasn't settled yet, which reframes a widely mocked online behavior as something closer to identity work.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on someone filming their own walk to work with a sweeping soundtrack — then flip the assumption that this is vanity. The hook: the first instrument to measure main character syndrome finds it tracks identity diffusion more strongly than narcissism. Call Pia Tohme at the Lebanese American University, and a narrative-identity researcher such as Dan McAdams for an outside view on whether protagonist-framing is a normal developmental tool that platforms have amplified.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a culture or long-read outlet on how internet-coined folk diagnoses — main character syndrome, doomscrolling, "rejection sensitive dysphoria" — migrate into peer-reviewed psychology, and who decides when a meme becomes a construct.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional; validation samples are young adults in one country and may not generalize; Study 1 N under 100-ish threshold concerns (N=135 is above, but subsample analyses are thin); no behavioral or longitudinal outcomes yet; self-reported throughout.

### 2. Virtual influencers who overshare put people off — unless they are beautiful

**Journal:** *Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking* | **Published:** October 2026 (Epub September 21, 2026)
**PMID:** 42765465 | **DOI:** 10.1177/21522715261488664
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a clean reversal of a well-established human finding (self-disclosure builds closeness), with a decent sample; limited by survey-only design.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 540 young adults familiar with virtual human influencers — CGI personalities with social media accounts — and modeled how the influencers' self-disclosure fed into parasocial relationships and emotional attachment. With humans, disclosing personal information reliably builds intimacy. With virtual influencers it did the opposite: self-disclosure generally *hindered* parasocial bonding, unless the character was rated as physically attractive. Perceived humanness then amplified the step from parasocial relationship to genuine emotional attachment.

**Why it matters:** It marks a boundary condition on one of social psychology's most robust intimacy mechanisms — artificial agents apparently have to earn the right to confide in you, and good looks are the entry fee.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the oddity: when a computer-generated influencer tells you about her bad day, you like her less — unless she's pretty. Open on one of the big virtual influencer accounts posting a "vulnerable" caption to millions of followers. Hook into the broader question of whether disclosure from AI companions reads as confession or as marketing copy. Call Hyun Jee Oh at Hanyang University; pair with a researcher on AI companion apps for the mental health angle.
- **Wider angle:** A business/tech piece on the economics of synthetic celebrity — brands are spending real money on characters whose emotional pull depends on design choices that researchers are only now reverse-engineering. Fits a technology or marketing-focused outlet.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional self-report; moderated mediation cannot establish causal direction; sample is young adults in South Korea already familiar with virtual influencers, so may not generalize; no behavioral outcomes.

### 3. Punishing a teenager for being upset predicts worse doomscrolling months later

**Journal:** *Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being* | **Published:** October 2026
**PMID:** 42817062 | **DOI:** 10.1111/aphw.70233
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — large two-wave sample and a modern network approach linking family dynamics to a specific online habit; limited by self-report and only two time points.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Chinese researchers followed 2,873 adolescents across two measurement waves, mapping how parents respond to their children's negative emotions (dismissing, punishing, encouraging expression, problem-focused help) against components of social media doomscrolling, using cross-lagged network analysis. Conflict acted as the key bridge connecting the two domains. Punitive parental responses at wave one predicted later increases in parental distress reactions *and* in adolescents' withdrawal symptoms when they tried to stop scrolling; higher "salience" of scrolling early on predicted more tolerance and relapse later, plus less parental encouragement of emotional expression.

**Why it matters:** It treats compulsive scrolling as part of a family emotional system rather than a solo screen-time problem, and suggests the loop runs in both directions — parenting shapes scrolling, scrolling erodes parenting.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the familiar scene of a parent snapping at a teenager for sulking over their phone. The surprise: the telling-off itself is statistically upstream of the withdrawal symptoms that make the phone harder to put down. Call Dan Tan at Tianjin Normal University; get an outside read from an emotion-socialization researcher such as Amanda Sheffield Morris, and be explicit that this is observational, not a parenting prescription.
- **Wider angle:** A piece for a parenting or education outlet on why screen-time rules keep failing while emotion coaching is barely discussed — with a wider look at network analysis as a way of modeling households rather than individuals.

**Caveats:** Observational — cannot establish causation; all measures self-reported by adolescents; two waves only, so temporal inference is limited; single-country sample (China) may not generalize; doomscrolling measured with addiction-style symptom items whose clinical status is contested.

### 4. Liking a vaping post is a stronger marker of heavy tobacco use than just seeing one

**Journal:** *Addictive Behaviors* | **Published:** October 2026 (Epub May 29, 2026)
**PMID:** 42229276 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.addbeh.2026.108755
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — nationally representative N=70,773 and a genuinely useful distinction between algorithmic delivery and user engagement; still cross-sectional.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using three years of the U.S. National Youth Tobacco Survey (2021–2023; N=70,773), researchers at the University of Queensland separated *passive* exposure to e-cigarette content (simply seeing it) from *active* engagement (liking, commenting, sharing), then mapped both onto latent classes of tobacco use. Three classes emerged: minimal/non-users (~96%), predominantly e-cigarette users (~3%), and poly-tobacco users (~1%). Passive exposure was associated with predominantly e-cigarette use; active engagement was associated with both e-cigarette and poly-tobacco use, with larger effect sizes, and associations grew with exposure frequency.

**Why it matters:** Most "social media causes vaping" research lumps scrolling past an ad together with tapping the heart button; separating them shows which signal is doing the work, and tells regulators whether the target is content delivery or interaction design.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is methodological but lands hard: it's not what the algorithm shows teenagers, it's what they touch. Open on a 15-year-old's feed and the single tap that marks them out statistically. Call Carmen Lim at the National Centre for Youth Substance Use Research. Be careful: these are associations, and engagement may simply be a marker of existing interest rather than a cause.
- **Wider angle:** A policy story for a public health or current-affairs outlet on whether vaping content rules should regulate engagement features — follows, shares, comment sections — rather than just advertising, as Australia's age-limit legislation and ongoing platform litigation reshape the debate.

**Caveats:** Observational and cross-sectional — reverse causation (users seeking out vaping content) is entirely plausible; self-reported exposure and use; survey years span pandemic-era shifts in both vaping and social media use; U.S. adolescents only.

### 5. Personal trainers say their own bodies are the product — and it's wrecking their body image

**Journal:** *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* | **Published:** September 24, 2026
**PMID:** 42785604 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychsport.2026.103266
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — first qualitative look at the people *producing* fitness content rather than consuming it, with vivid material; small qualitative sample caps the score.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers at the University of Edinburgh ran semi-structured interviews with 17 online personal trainers (mean age 33.5; 58.8% women), asking them about social media as a workplace and about content they had chosen themselves, then analyzed the transcripts using reflexive thematic analysis. Trainers described maintaining a visible presence as non-negotiable for business, and felt obliged to display a lean physique while performing "authenticity" — which meant actively hiding their own body image struggles from clients. Some rejected these norms; others deliberately monetized their appearance to signal competence.

**Why it matters:** Almost all research on fitness content and body image studies the audience; this flips the lens onto the creators, who turn out to be caught in the same machinery they run.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the title quote — "To be a personal trainer, you have to look like 4% body fat" — and the trainer who posts a vulnerability video while concealing an actual eating problem. The surprise is the authenticity trap: being paid to look real while being unable to show the real thing. Call Helen Sharpe or Emily Newman at the University of Edinburgh, and note the lead author's own declared work as a trainer/nutritionist.
- **Wider angle:** A labor story for a general-interest or business outlet: what happens when your body is a business asset with a depreciation curve — covering the occupational health gap for self-employed creators across fitness, beauty and lifestyle content.

**Caveats:** Small N (17), qualitative — no prevalence or causal claims; self-selected participants likely more reflective about body image than average; lead author declares income from personal training and nutrition services; UK/Ireland-based sample.

### 6. Men preoccupied with getting bigger report more sexual problems

**Journal:** *Body Image* | **Published:** September 29, 2026
**PMID:** 42809933 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bodyim.2026.102201
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a well-powered first look at an unstudied link in a group rarely asked about either topic; cross-sectional, online-recruited.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Drawing on The Study of Boys and Men — an online survey of 1,116 sexually active males aged 15–35 recruited through social media advertisements in 2024 — researchers tested whether muscle dysmorphia symptoms (measured with the Muscle Dysmorphic Disorder Inventory) tracked eight domains of sexual dysfunction, including anxiety, lack of arousal, pain and erectile difficulties. Higher overall symptoms, and especially the "Appearance Intolerance" subscale (distress and avoidance around one's own body), were consistently associated with a broader range of sexual dysfunctions. The "Drive for Size" subscale — the gym-obvious part — showed weaker and fewer associations.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the harmful core of muscle dysmorphia may be shame about how the body looks rather than ambition about how big it gets, which matters for how clinicians screen young men.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the counterintuitive split: wanting to be huge isn't the part that predicts trouble in bed — hating how you look is. Open on a young man who trains six days a week and avoids being seen undressed. Call Kyle Ganson at the University of Toronto or Jason Nagata at UCSF. Avoid implying causation or clinical action: this is a single cross-sectional survey.
- **Wider angle:** A men's health feature on why sexual function is almost never asked about in eating disorder and body image services for men, and how anabolic steroid use sits in the middle of the picture — fits a health or men's-interest specialist outlet.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional — cannot establish direction (sexual difficulties could worsen body image); recruited via social media advertisements, so the sample is self-selected and skewed toward engaged, body-focused respondents; all outcomes self-reported; steroid use and mental health comorbidities not disentangled in the abstract.

### 7. Most bereaved partners have sexual memories of the person who died — and mostly find them comforting

**Journal:** *The Journal of Sex Research* | **Published:** October 2026 (Epub April 10, 2025)
**PMID:** 40208217 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00224499.2025.2484199
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — genuinely untouched territory and highly relatable, but a modest convenience sample and no clinical outcomes.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 165 Canadians bereaved of a romantic partner, recruited through Leger panels, social media and online grief forums, about whether they reminisced sexually about the person who died. It was near-universal: 80.5% did so, 31.8% at least weekly, and the experiences were generally positive in tone. More frequent sexual reminiscing was associated with stronger continuing bonds, higher past sexual satisfaction with the deceased, and higher post-traumatic growth.

**Why it matters:** Grief research and grief therapy have essentially ignored the erotic dimension of continuing bonds; this is the first evidence that it is both common and, for most people, not distressing.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is a near-universal private experience that nobody studies or admits to. Open on a widow who has never mentioned this to her grief counsellor. Frame it through continuing bonds theory — the idea that healthy grief maintains rather than severs the relationship — and ask whether sexual memory is part of that. Call Christopher Quinn-Nilas at Memorial University of Newfoundland.
- **Wider angle:** A longer essay for a general-interest magazine on the taboos that survive bereavement: what therapists are trained not to ask about, and how widowed people navigate new relationships while an old intimacy persists.

**Caveats:** Small-to-moderate N (165); self-selected sample recruited partly from grief forums and social media, likely biased toward those comfortable discussing the topic; cross-sectional and self-reported; Canadian sample, mostly heterosexual presumably — generalization unclear; correlational associations only.

### 8. Compulsive internet users may be easier to phish

**Journal:** *Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking* | **Published:** September 29, 2026
**PMID:** 42808340 | **DOI:** 10.1177/21522715261494036
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — an unusual and useful cross-domain hypothesis linking addiction neuroscience to security behavior, but it's a narrative review with no new data.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** A clinician and a cybersecurity practitioner synthesize evidence from 2017–2026 across addiction neuroscience, clinical psychology and security research to argue that internet addiction disorder — still not a formal DSM diagnosis — involves dopaminergic reward and prefrontal disruptions resembling those in substance use disorders. They propose impaired prefrontal inhibitory control as the central mediator linking compulsive use to real-world security failures: poorer compliance with protocols, greater susceptibility to social engineering, more risky online behavior. The authors situate this against Australia's social media minimum-age law and a 2026 U.S. civil verdict holding platforms liable for addictive design.

**Why it matters:** If the same impulsivity that keeps people scrolling also makes them click the malicious link, then phishing resistance becomes partly a mental health variable rather than a training problem — an idea neither field has tested properly.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the practical twist: your organization's weakest security link may be the person who cannot stop refreshing their feed. Open on a security awareness training session that works for everyone except the people who need it most. The authors — one at Queen's Medical Centre, one at NCC Group — are an unusual pairing worth calling together. Flag hard that the underlying evidence is cross-sectional and directionality is unresolved.
- **Wider angle:** A piece for a technology or business-security outlet on the ethics of the proposal: should employers be allowed to profile staff by impulsivity or digital habits to assess breach risk, and what happens when behavioral health data enters corporate security?

**Caveats:** Narrative review, not systematic — selection of evidence is at the authors' discretion; underlying studies are predominantly cross-sectional, so causal direction cannot be established; internet addiction disorder lacks formal diagnostic status; one author works for a commercial cybersecurity firm (NCC Group plc).

### 9. Fear of being cancelled is showing up in therapy rooms as a distinct anxiety

**Journal:** *Current Opinion in Psychology* | **Published:** October 2026 (Epub June 5, 2026)
**PMID:** 42296638 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.copsyc.2026.102333
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a timely and vivid clinical observation, but non-empirical and politically loaded, which caps both the score and the framing options.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Two Fordham University psychologists argue that "fear of cancellation" has become a recognizable clinical presentation that cuts across generalized anxiety disorder, OCD and social anxiety disorder. They describe characteristic behaviors — compulsively re-reading years of old posts for anything potentially offensive, checking and deleting, or withdrawing from social media entirely — and note these map onto existing anxiety and compulsive-checking mechanisms while being a product of the permanent, searchable archive the internet created. They also raise the second-order problem of psychologists self-censoring in their own field.

**Why it matters:** Not much is established yet — this is an agenda-setting piece rather than data — but it names a pattern that clinicians report seeing and that maps cleanly onto ostracism research, opening an empirical line of enquiry.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on someone scrolling back through a decade of their own posts at 2am, deleting. The hook is the clinical reframing: this looks less like political commentary and more like compulsive checking, with the internet supplying the permanent record that makes it possible. Call Dean McKay at Fordham; balance with an ostracism researcher such as Kipling Williams on how ancient the underlying fear is. Note explicitly that no prevalence data exist.
- **Wider angle:** A sociology-of-science feature on self-censorship within psychology itself — what happens to a research field when its members report avoiding certain questions — for a specialist research or higher-education outlet.

**Caveats:** Non-empirical opinion/review article with no new data and no prevalence estimates; contested political framing; one author is a guest editor of the issue (recused from peer review, per the journal's statement); clinical claims rest on case observation rather than systematic study.

### 10. Social media and AI are quietly rewriting what psychiatric terms mean

**Journal:** *American Psychologist* | **Published:** October 2026 (Epub March 2, 2026)
**PMID:** 41770174 | **DOI:** 10.1037/amp0001673
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — important and very relatable trend, but a conceptual/ethics article rather than a study.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Psychologists at Mercer University describe a feedback loop in which psychological terminology circulates through social media and AI systems, is reshaped by engagement-optimizing algorithms, and then flows back into public — and eventually clinical — understanding. They argue this has genuinely reduced stigma and increased mental health awareness while also driving overpathologization, drift away from diagnostic criteria, and the spread of inaccuracies, and they weigh these against each other in a utilitarian ethical framework. They propose concrete practice recommendations and new ethical guidance for the APA.

**Why it matters:** It frames a familiar irritation — everyone online now has "trauma responses" and "executive dysfunction" — as a measurable systems problem with a loop that includes AI models trained on the same distorted discourse.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a clinician whose new clients arrive already diagnosed by TikTok, then ask the sharper question: when a chatbot is trained on that same discourse, does the loop close? Lead with the fact that the benefit side — stigma reduction — is real, which makes this harder than a "misinformation bad" story. Call Jody Finch at Mercer University, and pair with someone working on concept creep, such as Nick Haslam.
- **Wider angle:** A reported feature for a technology or media outlet tracing a single term — "gaslighting", say, or "burnout" — from the clinical literature through platform virality into chatbot output and back into a doctor's office, with linguists and corpus data.

**Caveats:** Conceptual/ethics article with no new empirical data; recommendations are untested; US-centric and framed around APA ethical standards specifically.

### 11. The more you understand how targeted ads work, the harder you work to avoid them

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** September 22, 2026
**PMID:** 42790213 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107848
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — large sample and a clear mediation chain from knowledge to skepticism to avoidance, but it confirms a plausible theory in a single cross-sectional survey.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 977 social media users in Pakistan and used structural equation modeling to test how "persuasion knowledge" — awareness of advertisers' tactics and intent — relates to resistance against personalized ads. Greater persuasion knowledge was associated with more ad skepticism (β=0.228), more perceived intrusiveness (β=0.437) and more ad avoidance (β=0.150), with skepticism and intrusiveness sequentially mediating the path. Perceived intrusiveness, rather than skepticism, carried the strongest association.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the industry's main defense of personalization — that better targeting means more relevant, less annoying ads — may backfire among exactly the users who understand what targeting is: it feels like surveillance, not service.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is that transparency has a cost for advertisers: teaching people how personalization works makes them avoid it, and the dominant feeling is intrusion rather than disbelief. Open on the uncanny moment everyone recognizes — an ad for the thing you only said out loud. Call Fatima Maqsood at Huazhong University of Science and Technology; contrast with an advertising-industry researcher on whether "autonomy-respecting personalization" is a real option.
- **Wider angle:** A piece for a technology or policy outlet on ad literacy as a regulatory tool: if knowing how the machine works makes people opt out, what does that imply for transparency mandates under privacy regulation — and for platforms whose revenue depends on the opposite?

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional self-report — avoidance was reported, not measured behaviorally; cannot establish causation; single-country sample (Pakistan) in a distinctive digital market may not generalize; no manipulation of persuasion knowledge.

### 12. Labeling a robot's fact-check as coming from a regulator doesn't make people believe it more

**Journal:** *Ergonomics* | **Published:** September 21, 2026
**PMID:** 42765662 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00140139.2026.2734930
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a largely null result against a widely assumed effect, with brain imaging attached; small N and exploratory connectivity analyses limit confidence.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Seventy adults judged food-safety rumors before and after the same Pepper robot delivered identical corrections, attributed variously to social media users, local news outlets or national regulators, while prefrontal activity was recorded using fNIRS. Corrections did shift judgments in the corrective direction overall — but the attributed source made no significant difference to post-correction accuracy or to whether the correction worked. Between-condition differences in prefrontal activation did not survive statistical correction. Source cues did move *perceived* AI capability and trust, and orbitofrontal activation tracked those subjective ratings rather than accuracy.

**Why it matters:** It separates two things usually conflated in misinformation design — how much people trust an AI advisor and how much they actually update their beliefs — and suggests authority badging buys the former without the latter.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the null: slapping a government logo on an AI fact-check made people rate the AI as smarter without making them any more likely to change their minds. Open in the lab with the Pepper robot debunking a food scare three times over. Call Yi Ding at Anhui Polytechnic University. Treat the neuroimaging cautiously — the headline result here is behavioral.
- **Wider angle:** A story for a technology or design outlet on the rise of "source transparency" features in AI assistants — citations, provenance badges, confidence scores — and the thin evidence that any of them change what users actually do.

**Caveats:** Small N (70) for an imaging study; key between-condition effects did not survive correction, so this is a null rather than a demonstrated absence; functional connectivity analyses are explicitly exploratory; single-center lab study with one robot platform and one topic domain (food safety); findings may not transfer to politically charged misinformation.

### 13. In online arguments about returning wolves, people blame the wolf

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** September 28, 2026
**PMID:** 42804846 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107887
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a quirky and quotable observation about how moral agency gets assigned to animals online, but qualitative discourse analysis with no quantification.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Dutch researchers applied discursive psychology to social media interactions about the return of wolves and beavers to the Netherlands, analyzing how participants construct and trade blame. Rather than confining responsibility to humans — farmers, conservationists, government — commenters routinely attributed intent and responsibility to the animals themselves, through personification ("the wolf defended itself instinctively"), categorization, contradiction and counter-assertion. These attributions functioned as covert declarations of where the speaker stood on rewilding, hardening the divide between camps.

**Why it matters:** It shows that a conservation argument people think is about facts is largely conducted through implicit moral framing of animals as agents — a mechanism that could be used to defuse, rather than inflame, polarized environmental debate.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a comment thread after a wolf attack on livestock, where the argument turns on whether the wolf "chose" to attack. The psychological hook is folk attribution of intent to non-human agents, and how it smuggles political positions into apparently factual claims. Call Anne Kessels at Wageningen University; pair with a theory-of-mind or anthropomorphism researcher for the cognitive layer.
- **Wider angle:** An environment feature on rewilding communication across Europe as wolves recolonize farmland, examining whether conservation bodies' messaging inadvertently fuels the agency framing that makes compromise harder. Fits a nature or environmental policy outlet.

**Caveats:** Qualitative discourse analysis — no sample size, prevalence or effect estimates reported in the abstract; single-country (Netherlands), two-species focus may not generalize; social media commenters are not representative of public opinion; the lead author reports equipment/supplies provided by a social media monitoring company (Spotler Engage) and writing assistance from an external contributor.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Virtual influencers keep behaving unlike their human counterparts in instructive ways.** Last month's randomized exposure study found that CGI influencers damage women's body image exactly as much as real ones ("AI influencers dent women's body image just as much as real ones", PMID 42379075). The new survey work ("Virtual influencers who overshare put people off — unless they are beautiful", PMID 42765465) adds the mirror image on the relationship side: the standard human intimacy mechanism of self-disclosure *reverses* for synthetic personalities unless they are rated attractive. Together the two suggest the harm transfers from human to synthetic bodies while the warmth does not.
- **Non-screen upstream stressors keep being pulled into models of problematic use, and both from the same institution.** "Punishing a teenager for being upset predicts worse doomscrolling months later" (PMID 42817062, Tianjin Normal University) follows directly on "Burnt-out teenagers scroll more" (PMID 41580900, also Tianjin Normal University): in both, the explanatory work is done by something outside the phone — school exhaustion in one, parental emotion socialization in the other — with compulsive use as the downstream node. The new study improves on the old one by adding a second wave and modeling the loop as bidirectional.
- **Composite constructs keep dissolving into informative sub-parts.** "A scale now exists for 'main character syndrome'" (PMID 42696901) finds identity diffusion, not narcissism, as the dominant correlate; "Men preoccupied with getting bigger report more sexual problems" (PMID 42809933) finds Appearance Intolerance, not Drive for Size, carries the associations. That is the same analytic move as last month's FOMO-is-brooding-not-reflection result (PMID 41622812) and the distress-not-attachment finding on post-breakup monitoring (PMID 42388174).
- **Quantity of exposure continues to lose to the specific behavior.** Separating passive exposure from active engagement with vaping content across 70,773 US teenagers (PMID 42229276) extends the previous digest's pattern in which problematic use drops out once more specific variables enter the model (PMID 42714373) and in which identical phone hours conceal two opposite attentional states (PMID 42492199).

**Advances**

- The algorithm-and-AI social learning synthesis from last month (PMID 42715802) argued that engagement optimization inflates the tails of what people see while generative AI collapses them. "Social media and AI are quietly rewriting what psychiatric terms mean" (PMID 41770174) applies that same loop to a specific vocabulary — clinical terminology — and closes it by pointing out that models are trained on the discourse they helped distort.
- Body image research moves from audience to producer. Previous runs studied viewers (PMIDs 42379075, 41580900); "Personal trainers say their own bodies are the product" (PMID 42785604) is the first look at the people making the content, who describe concealing their own struggles while performing authenticity.

**Counterpoints**

- Last month's IUD experiment (PMID 42641053) showed that a brief dose of negative content reliably shifts attitudes through identifiable cognitive routes, implying that message design matters a great deal. "Labeling a robot's fact-check as coming from a regulator doesn't make people believe it more" (PMID 42765662) pushes the other way on the corrective side: source authority moved perceived trust and AI capability ratings without moving belief updating at all. Persuasion in and persuasion out are not symmetric.

**New themes**

- **Internet-coined folk diagnoses are being formalized.** A validated scale for main character syndrome (PMID 42696901), a clinical case for "fear of cancellation" as a cross-diagnostic anxiety presentation (PMID 42296638), and an ethics paper on psychiatric terms reshaped by platforms and AI (PMID 41770174) are all new; nothing comparable appeared last month.
- **Cross-domain spillover beyond mental health.** "Compulsive internet users may be easier to phish" (PMID 42808340) exports internet addiction into corporate security, and "The more you understand how targeted ads work, the harder you work to avoid them" (PMID 42790213) exports it into advertising regulation.
- **FOMO, the hub construct of the September batch (PMIDs 42722509, 41622812, 42594777), does not appear anywhere in this one** — worth watching whether it was a one-month cluster.
- **Social media as data source and recruitment channel rather than subject matter**: the wolf-blame discourse analysis (PMID 42804846) and the social-media-recruited surveys on bereavement (PMID 40208217) and muscle dysmorphia (PMID 42809933).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Five studies in this batch show vernacular, internet-born categories of distress hardening into research objects. "Main character syndrome" now has a validated 10-item scale (PMID 42696901); "fear of cancellation" is described as a recognizable clinical presentation spanning GAD, OCD and social anxiety (PMID 42296638); doomscrolling is measured with addiction-style salience, withdrawal, tolerance and relapse items in a 2,873-adolescent cohort (PMID 42817062); internet addiction disorder, still not in the DSM, is proposed as a predictor of real-world security failures (PMID 42808340); and a conceptual paper maps the feedback loop by which psychological terminology is reshaped by engagement algorithms and AI systems before returning to clinicians (PMID 41770174).

**Why pitch this now:** The traffic is running backwards — labels are travelling from feeds into instruments, journals and, in the cybersecurity and litigation examples cited in these papers, into policy and liability arguments — and it is happening faster than anyone is checking whether the constructs hold up.

**Angle:** Follow two or three terms from meme to measurement instrument to consulting room and ask what the quality control looks like: who validates these scales, on whom, and what happens when a construct with no diagnostic status starts carrying regulatory or commercial weight. For curious general readers who already use this vocabulary about themselves.

**Potential headlines:**
- Psychologists are now building questionnaires for conditions the internet invented
- Main character syndrome turns out to measure a shaky sense of self, not vanity
- The label you found online may reach the consulting room before you do

**Potential outlets:**
- **New Scientist Mind** — the section already handles construct-level reappraisals of familiar online worries, and the main character syndrome scale is a concrete, quotable entry point.
- **The Atlantic** — strong track record on self-diagnosis culture and concept creep; the stigma-reduction-versus-overpathologization tension in PMID 41770174 is exactly its kind of trade-off.
- **Undark** — suited to the sceptical reporting job here: sample sizes, single-country validation, and the commercial and editorial conflicts declared in several of these papers.
- **STAT** — covers nosology and the DSM's boundaries closely, and would take the clinical end seriously, including what clinicians do when patients arrive pre-labelled.
