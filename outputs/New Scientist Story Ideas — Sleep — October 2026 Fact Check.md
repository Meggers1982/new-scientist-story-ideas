### Study 1: Bad nights and next-day eating disorder symptoms feed each other in a daily loop
**PMID:** 42834479 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Core facts check out: N=128, ANSD with current or past suicidal ideation, daily EMA, pre-registered hypotheses and analyses, multilevel models, bidirectional lagged associations across restriction, binge eating, compensatory behaviors, body checking and SI. Framing is appropriately non-causal and the caveats are honest.

**Issue A. Institutional attribution is incomplete** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Researchers at the University of Louisville recruited 128 people"
- **Abstract says:** Authors are affiliated with the University of Louisville (Buseman, Cash, Levinson) and Auburn University (Smith AR, Department of Psychological Sciences).
- **Problem:** The study is a multi-institution collaboration; Auburn is dropped.
- **Suggested fix:** "Researchers at the University of Louisville, with a collaborator at Auburn University, recruited 128 people"

**Issue B. "Smartphone surveys" is not stated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "had them complete repeated daily smartphone surveys (ecological momentary assessment)"
- **Abstract says:** "completed daily ecological momentary assessment (EMA) surveys"
- **Problem:** The delivery method (smartphone) is plausible but not specified. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "had them complete repeated daily ecological momentary assessment surveys"

**Issue C. NS fit score sits slightly high for all-self-report EMA data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10"
- **Abstract says:** All constructs — sleep disturbance, ED symptoms, SI — are self-reported via EMA; no objective sleep measure, no comparison group.
- **Problem:** A 7 rests on data that are entirely self-reported and uncontrolled, even if pre-registered and intensively sampled. The pre-registration and clinical-population access are real strengths, but the evidence does not support a 7.
- **Suggested fix:** "**NS fit:** 6/10 — pre-registered, intensively sampled real-world data on a hard-to-study clinical group; limited by self-report throughout, absence of objective sleep measurement and no comparison group."

*Note:* "The effects ran in both directions within the same person" — the abstract tested both within- and between-person effects but does not specify at which level each significant effect emerged. Unverifiable from abstract only; worth checking the full text before the pitch leans on "within the same person."

### Study 2: Sleep helps the brain file away where things were, in both directions at once
**PMID:** 42844862 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Method description (intentionally learned pictures, incidentally learned horizontal and vertical screen positions, natural night sleep vs daytime wakefulness, multinomial modelling, bound source memory benefit), institution and journal details all match. The caveat correctly notes sample size is absent from the abstract.

**Issue A. "First-in-class" novelty label conflicts with the abstract's own framing** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** First-in-class"
- **Abstract says:** "Extending previous research on sleep benefits in item-context associative memory, we investigated whether sleep also improves bound source memory for cross-dimensional context associations."
- **Problem:** The authors position this as an extension of an established literature and a confirmation of the active systems consolidation hypothesis, not a first-of-its-kind result. The NS fit note already concedes this ("extends an established hypothesis rather than overturning it"), so the novelty tag contradicts the digest's own assessment.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** Incremental extension — first test of cross-dimensional context binding"

### Study 3: Anxiety about keeping up with AI tracks with worse sleep
**PMID:** 42830665 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=364, ages 18–60, 71.98% female, mean age 28.43, Yildiz Technical University, two-step SEM with bootstrapped indirect effects, controls for gender, age and AI tool usage frequency — all accurate. Headline is correctly non-causal and the caveats are strong.

**Issue A. Pitch claims a null direct effect the abstract never reports** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The surprise is that the worry doesn't appear to keep people awake on its own — it does so, statistically, only after it has been converted into rumination and then dread about the future"
- **Abstract says:** "The results indicated significant indirect associations between artificial intelligence competence anxiety and sleep disturbance through RNT and future anxiety."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports significant *indirect* effects; it says nothing about whether the direct path was non-significant. "Doesn't keep people awake on its own" and "only after" assert full mediation, which cannot be established from the abstract. This is the hook of the pitch, so it needs to hold.
- **Suggested fix:** "The hook is the route: the link between AI competence anxiety and disturbed sleep runs statistically through repetitive negative thinking and then anxiety about the future — the same chain existing insomnia therapies already target. (Check the full text for whether a direct effect survived.)"

**Issue B. "Rumination" substituted for repetitive negative thinking** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "linked to disturbed sleep indirectly, via rumination and then future anxiety in sequence"
- **Abstract says:** "repetitive negative thinking (RNT)"
- **Problem:** RNT is a broader construct than rumination (it includes worry and post-event processing); swapping the term narrows the finding and may misdescribe the instrument used.
- **Suggested fix:** "linked to disturbed sleep indirectly, via repetitive negative thinking and then future anxiety in sequence"

**Issue C. Author's first name not verifiable** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call Begüm Ceren Kara's group at Yildiz Technical University"
- **Abstract says:** "Kara BC"
- **Problem:** The expanded given name is an inference from initials. Unverifiable from abstract only — confirm before the pitch goes out.
- **Suggested fix:** "Call B. C. Kara's group at Yildiz Technical University" (pending name confirmation)

### Study 4: Religious practice goes with lighter sleep problems in older women, but not men — and the link mostly dissolves on adjustment
**PMID:** 42838779 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Dataset (2018 LSAHV), n=4901, age ≥60, Chulalongkorn University, six self-reported indicators, three latent classes at 29.4 / 45.6 / 25.0 percent, the moderate–severe contrast, the 6.0 percentage-point female association and the null result in men are all reported correctly. Non-causal language throughout; caveats accurate.

**Issue A. "Mostly dissolves" overstates the attenuation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the link mostly dissolves on adjustment"
- **Abstract says:** "This association was attenuated by 2.0 percentage points after further adjustment for psychosocial and behavioral factors and was no longer statistically significant."
- **Problem:** The point estimate fell from 6.0 to roughly 4.0 percentage points — a reduction of about a third, not "mostly." What vanished was statistical significance, not most of the effect size. Readers will take "mostly dissolves" to mean the estimate collapsed.
- **Suggested fix:** "...and the link loses statistical significance on fuller adjustment"

**Issue B. Sequential adjustment described as a mediation analysis** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Lead with the mediation that undoes the headline — the apparent benefit of religious engagement for older women's sleep largely disappears once you account for social and behavioral life, hinting that what's doing the work is company and structure, not faith."
- **Abstract says:** Sex-stratified multinomial logistic regression with staged adjustment; attenuation of 2.0 percentage points after adding psychosocial and behavioral covariates. No mediation analysis is reported.
- **Problem:** Attenuation on covariate adjustment is suggestive of confounding or mediation, but the study did not test a mediation pathway, and a cross-sectional design could not separate the two. Calling it "the mediation" and concluding what is "doing the work" asserts a mechanism the analysis cannot deliver — and "largely disappears" repeats the Issue A overstatement.
- **Suggested fix:** "Lead with what happens on adjustment: the association between religious engagement and milder sleep problems in older women shrinks and loses significance once psychosocial and behavioral factors are in the model — raising the question of whether company and routine, rather than faith itself, are carrying the signal. Note the design cannot separate confounding from mediation."

*Note:* "diverged sharply on perceived sleep quality, restfulness and medication use" — the abstract says only "differed in." Not flagged separately, but "sharply" is the digest's word, not the authors'.

### Study 5: One in four drug users in a Spanish survey say they take something to help them sleep
**PMID:** 42802253 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Headline and pitch generalize a cannabis/heroin-specific figure to all drug users** — Severity: Major

- **As written:** "One in four drug users in a Spanish survey say they take something to help them sleep" / "a quarter of respondents said they used to get to sleep"
- **Abstract says:** "Cannabis and heroin were frequently used to relax or manage stress (49.73% and 30.14%, respectively), improve sleep (26.66%), and cope with anxiety and/or depression (26.35%)."
- **Problem:** The 26.66 percent figure sits inside a sentence specifically about cannabis and heroin, and the abstract does not state that it refers to all 6,399 respondents or to all substances. The headline ("drug users... take something") and the pitch ("a quarter of respondents") both convert a substance-specific reason-for-use figure into a survey-wide prevalence. The body text handles this correctly ("using cannabis or heroin... to improve sleep (26.7 percent)"), which makes the headline and pitch internally inconsistent with the digest's own study description. This is the entire hook of the pitch.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "A quarter of cannabis and heroin use in a Spanish survey was reported as a sleep aid." Pitch: "The surprise is how much cannabis and heroin use is reported as self-medication: roughly a quarter of it to get to sleep, a quarter to cope with anxiety or depression." Confirm the denominator from the full text before pitching.

**Issue B. "Population-level signal" contradicts the digest's own caveat** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It also provides one of the first population-level signals that chemsex is no longer an exclusively MSM phenomenon."
- **Abstract says:** "We analyzed data from 6,399 valid participants residing in Spain who completed the European Web Survey on Drugs... Though historically associated with MSM, chemsex use is increasingly reported among women."
- **Problem:** The European Web Survey on Drugs is a self-selected web survey, as the caveats correctly state ("not population-representative"). The body text asserts exactly the opposite. Separately, "one of the first" is unverifiable from the abstract, and the abstract's own conclusion still frames chemsex as "particularly among MSM."
- **Suggested fix:** "It also adds a large-survey signal — though from a self-selected sample — that chemsex is increasingly reported by women as well as men who have sex with men."

**Issue C. "Rather than as recreation" reverses the abstract's headline conclusion** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It documents how much illicit drug use in a European country is functioning as unsupervised self-medication for insomnia and distress rather than as recreation"
- **Abstract says:** "Recreational use was the most common reason for drug consumption (78.01%)... Illicit drug use in Spain is predominantly recreational and typically occurs at home, festivals, or nightlife venues."
- **Problem:** The authors' explicit conclusion is that use is *predominantly recreational*. "Rather than as recreation" inverts that. The digest reports the 78 percent figure in the study description but then argues against it in "Why it matters."
- **Suggested fix:** "It documents a substantial minority of illicit drug use functioning as unsupervised self-medication for insomnia and distress, alongside the predominantly recreational use the authors report"

*Cleared:* The industry conflict of interest (Blasco-Fontecilla; Takeda, BIAL, Rubio, Rovi lecture fees) is correctly disclosed in the caveats. Journal, date, N and the 78.01 / 13.66 / 3.31 percent figures are accurate. NS fit of 4/10 is defensible.
