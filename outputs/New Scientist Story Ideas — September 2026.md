### 1. Postpartum depression is strongly heritable, Danish twin study finds

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 26 August 2026 (issue 1 December 2026)
**PMID:** 42648551 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122432
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — first twin-design heritability estimate for postpartum depression in a whole-population register, though it rests on a small number of concordant pairs.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers linked the Danish Twin Register to national health registers, identifying 10,418 female twins (5209 pairs) born 1949–2000 who had given birth. Postpartum depression was defined by hospital diagnosis or filled antidepressant prescription in the first year after delivery. Identical twins were roughly twice as concordant as fraternal twins (0.22 versus 0.11), giving a heritability estimate of 0.60, with the remaining 40 percent attributed to non-shared environment.

**Why it matters:** Postpartum depression has long been framed either as a hormonal accident or as a failure of coping; a genetic contribution on a par with that of major depression reframes it as a condition women are predisposed to, and the authors explicitly pitch this as an anti-stigma finding.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the number — well over half of the variation in who gets postpartum depression tracks with genes, from the country with the world's best birth and prescription registers. Open on a woman whose sister or mother had it. Call Trine Munk-Olsen or Mette Bliddal (University of Southern Denmark), who have built much of the population-scale perinatal psychiatry evidence base, and ask what a heritability figure can and cannot tell an individual mother.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a health or parenting outlet on how far perinatal mental health screening has moved from symptom checklists toward family history and, eventually, polygenic risk — and the ethical unease of telling a pregnant woman she is "at risk" when no preventive treatment is guaranteed.

**Caveats:** Observational and register-based (no interviews); only 15 concordant pairs in total, so confidence intervals are wide; antidepressant prescriptions are an imperfect proxy for depression; Danish-only population may not generalize; author funding from the Novo Nordisk and Lundbeck Foundations, with Lundbeck lecture fees declared.

### 2. Insomnia travels with PTSD and depression but does not seem to cause them

**Journal:** *Journal of Sleep Research* | **Published:** 16 September 2026
**PMID:** 42750113 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jsr.70450
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a 10-year, six-wave military cohort directly contradicts the widely repeated claim that poor sleep drives later mental illness; limited by self-report subscales.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** The Dutch PRISMO cohort followed 846 deployed military personnel from before deployment to 1 month, 6 months, 1 year, 2 years and 10 years afterwards, measuring insomnia, depression and PTSD symptoms. Using a random-intercept cross-lagged panel model, which separates within-person change from stable individual differences, insomnia symptoms did not predict later PTSD or depression. Instead, all three rose and fell together at every time point, while PTSD and depression showed small reciprocal effects on each other.

**Why it matters:** "Fix the sleep to prevent the PTSD" has become a standard assumption in military and civilian mental health; this suggests insomnia is a co-traveler reflecting shared vulnerability rather than an upstream cause — which matters for how prevention trials are designed and sold.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is a negative: ten years of data on soldiers before and after deployment find that bad sleep does not come first. Open on the standard advice given to returning personnel. Call Elbert Geuze or Eric Vermetten (PRISMO cohort), and ask how this squares with insomnia-treatment trials that do improve PTSD symptoms — the within-person modeling is the crux of the argument.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-led feature for a science or psychology outlet on how cross-lagged panel models have been quietly demolishing a generation of "X predicts Y" findings once between-person differences are stripped out — sleep, screen time, exercise and mood all in the firing line.

**Caveats:** Observational (cannot establish causation in either direction); insomnia and depression measured only as SCL-90 subscales, not clinical insomnia diagnoses; military sample is mostly young and male and may not generalize; long gap between the 2-year and 10-year waves.

### 3. Twelve minutes of vigorous exercise a week matches 150 minutes of moderate activity

**Journal:** *International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity* | **Published:** 15 September 2026
**PMID:** 42745313 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12966-026-01980-3
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — the largest pooled device-measured activity dataset yet, with a strikingly small vigorous-activity threshold; still observational, and short follow-up for mortality.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers pooled wrist accelerometer data from 147,414 adults aged 40–74 in the German National Cohort and UK Biobank and followed them for a mean 7.9 years, during which 5545 died. Compared with no activity, 150 minutes a week of moderate activity was associated with a 30 percent lower mortality risk — and so was about 510 minutes of light activity or roughly 12 minutes of vigorous activity. Statistically swapping just 15–60 minutes a week of light or moderate activity for vigorous activity was associated with 18–30 percent lower risk.

**Why it matters:** Guidelines are written in units of moderate activity; these numbers suggest intensity buys disproportionate returns at very low volumes, and that "not enough time" is a weaker excuse than assumed.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Twelve minutes. That is the headline, and it comes from accelerometers rather than the notoriously flattering activity questionnaires. Open on someone who walks a lot but never gets out of breath. Call Michael Stein or Michael Leitzmann (University of Regensburg / Helmholtz Munich) and press them on reverse causation — people who can sprint are people who are already well.
- **Wider angle:** For a specialist sport-science or policy outlet: are public health guidelines still using the wrong currency? A piece on the push to rewrite activity recommendations around intensity and "exercise snacks," and why moderate-intensity minutes became the unit in the first place.

**Caveats:** Observational (cannot establish causation); baseline activity measured once, over about a week; reverse causality from undiagnosed illness is a real risk despite adjustment; UK Biobank and NAKO participants are healthier and more affluent than the general population; one co-author declares Bayer and Siemens Healthineers funding.

### 4. "Food noise" is quieter in people six months into GLP-1 drugs — and sweet tastes register less

**Journal:** *Physiology & Behavior* | **Published:** 25 August 2026 (issue 1 November 2026)
**PMID:** 42641845 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.physbeh.2026.115486
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — puts laboratory numbers on the much-discussed "food noise" effect, but cross-sectional with fewer than 30 people per group.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Seventy-nine adults with obesity were tested in three groups: eligible for but not yet taking a GLP-1 receptor agonist (n=27), one month into treatment (n=25), and six months in (n=27). They completed laboratory measures of taste thresholds, taste intensity and pleasantness, fat taste, chemesthesis, smell and salivary responses, plus questionnaires on food noise, cue responsivity, craving and food reward. Sweet detection thresholds were higher in both treated groups — they needed more sugar to notice it — while most other chemosensory measures were essentially unchanged. Six-month users reported markedly less food noise, craving, cue responsivity and food reward, and ate less.

**Why it matters:** It suggests these drugs work mainly on the wanting and the mental chatter around food rather than by making food taste different — with one specific exception for sweetness.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The lead is the dissociation: on GLP-1 drugs, food does not taste worse, it just stops shouting. Open on the now-familiar patient description of the "quiet" in their head. Call Richard Mattes (Purdue), a long-standing chemosensory researcher, and ask whether the blunted sweet threshold is cause or consequence of eating less sugar.
- **Wider angle:** A features piece for a general-interest or food outlet on the science of "food noise" as a newly legitimized psychological construct — where the questionnaire came from, what it measures, and what it implies for people who have it without ever taking a drug.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional group comparison, not a randomized trial — no one was followed through treatment, so selection effects are plausible; small N per group; dietary intake and food noise self-reported; senior author declares extensive food-industry consulting and funding (Grain Foods Foundation, Almond Board of California, General Mills, Mars Food and others).

### 5. Boosting a language region of the brain makes metaphors harder to understand

**Journal:** *Brain and Cognition* | **Published:** 29 August 2026 (issue October 2026)
**PMID:** 42667874 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bandc.2026.106476
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a clean causal manipulation with a backwards-seeming result; small, single-session, between-subjects design.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Forty participants received either high-definition transcranial direct current stimulation over the left inferior frontal gyrus (n=20) or sham stimulation (n=20), then rated how comprehensible they found metaphorical sentences ("Passion is a storm"), literal ones ("A gorilla is an ape") and nonsense ones ("Depression is a party"). Anodal stimulation, which raises neuronal excitability, made metaphors seem *less* comprehensible. Literal and nonsensical sentences were unaffected.

**Why it matters:** It implicates the brain's semantic control hub causally in figurative language — and shows that turning the volume up on a brain region can degrade, not improve, the behavior it supports.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Sell the paradox: stimulate the brain's meaning-control center and poetry stops making sense. Open on the metaphor itself — what has to happen in the head for "passion is a storm" to land. Call Hamad Al-Azary (Lawrence Technological University) and ask what selective excitation of semantic control actually does, and why the right hemisphere's traditional starring role in metaphor now looks overstated.
- **Wider angle:** For a specialist or long-read outlet: the persistent credibility problem of tDCS. Small samples, sham blinding, effects that flip direction between studies — a piece examining whether brain stimulation findings like this one survive replication, and what the field is doing about it.

**Caveats:** Small N (40 total, 20 per arm); between-subjects rather than within-subjects design; single stimulation session; subjective comprehensibility ratings rather than objective comprehension measures; no active control site.

### 6. Survivors of the Nova festival attack are being studied from weeks after the trauma to two years on

**Journal:** *European Journal of Psychotraumatology* | **Published:** 14 September 2026
**PMID:** 42735067 | **DOI:** 10.1080/20008066.2026.2721729
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — an extraordinary and probably unrepeatable cohort design, including people who were on psychoactive drugs during the trauma; but it is a protocol with no results yet.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (1 hits)

**The study:** The Nova Protocol describes a multimodal longitudinal study of civilian survivors of the 7 October 2023 attack on the Nova music festival, plus a sociocultural comparison group, running from the first weeks after the event to roughly 24 months. Assessments combine repeated clinical questionnaires (PCL-5, K6), wearable-sensor monitoring, ecological momentary assessment, saliva endocrine and inflammatory markers, structural and functional MRI, cardiac interoception paradigms, reinforcement-learning tasks and qualitative interviews. Crucially, many survivors were acutely under the influence of psychoactive substances during and after the attack, creating a naturalistic — not randomized — window on how drug states shape trauma encoding.

**Why it matters:** Prospective data starting weeks after a mass trauma with a narrowly defined shared exposure is almost never obtainable; if the cohort holds, it could test which early markers predict who develops PTSD, and whether being on MDMA or LSD during a trauma alters the outcome.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** A cohort that should not exist: hundreds of people exposed to the same catastrophic event within hours of each other, many of them on psychedelics at the time, now wired to sensors and scanners for two years. The hook is the uncomfortable question the researchers themselves flag — does a drug state at the moment of trauma protect or amplify? Call Roee Admon or Roy Salomon (University of Haifa). Frame strictly as a study to watch, with no results yet.
- **Wider angle:** For a magazine or specialist psychotrauma outlet: the ethics of studying survivors while they are still acutely traumatized. Who consents, who declines, how researchers handle the risk of re-traumatization through repeated assessment, and what the PRISMO and 9/11 cohorts learned about long-term participant burden.

**Caveats:** Protocol paper — no findings reported; substance exposure was self-selected and self-reported, so any drug-related association is confounded; survivors are self-selecting volunteers; the comparison group is sociocultural, not exposure-matched; findings will be correlational only, as the authors state.

### 7. Anxious people don't get the usual relief of bad imagined futures fading

**Journal:** *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology* | **Published:** 16 September 2026
**PMID:** 42750110 | **DOI:** 10.1177/17470218261490745
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a genuinely interesting asymmetry in how anxiety handles the imagined future, but reported across four linked experiments with unstated sample sizes.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Across four experiments, researchers compared ways of getting people to imagine specific future events, then tracked how the emotion attached to those imagined events decayed over one week or one month. Sentence completion and open association produced more vivid, more plausible, more emotionally charged future events than the standard recombination paradigm. With the more realistic method and a one-week follow-up, people low in anxiety showed the classic "fading affect bias" — negative imagined futures lost their emotional sting faster than positive ones — whereas highly anxious participants showed no such asymmetry.

**Why it matters:** The fading affect bias is usually described as a property of memory; extending it to the imagined future, and finding it absent in anxiety, suggests one reason dreaded scenarios stay vivid for some people and dissolve for others.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Most of us are quietly protected by a bias that lets imagined disasters lose their charge within a week — and anxious people appear to lack it. Open on the 3am rehearsal of a feared conversation. Call Lei Mo's group at South China Normal University and ask whether the effect is about the imagining or the remembering of the imagining.
- **Wider angle:** For a psychology or wellbeing outlet: a piece on episodic future thinking as a therapeutic target — how clinicians are already using imagined futures in treatment for depression and anxiety, and how methodological choices (sentence completion versus recombination) change what participants actually feel.

**Caveats:** Sample sizes not reported in the abstract; anxiety measured by questionnaire grouping rather than clinical diagnosis; emotion ratings entirely self-reported; short follow-up (one week for the key result); the one-month experiment found nothing, which the authors attribute to method rather than absence of effect.

### 8. Your baseline cortisol decides whether stress wrecks your memory

**Journal:** *Neurobiology of Learning and Memory* | **Published:** 16 September 2026
**PMID:** 42749210 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.nlm.2026.108219
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — offers a plausible explanation for years of contradictory stress-and-memory results; two modest lab experiments.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Participants learned pairs of unrelated words with negative or neutral meaning and later completed an associative recognition test. In Experiment 1 (n=63) stress was induced before learning; in Experiment 2 (n=56) before recall. Stress before retrieval clearly impaired associative memory. Across both experiments, a larger cortisol surge predicted more false alarms — confidently "recognizing" pairs that never occurred — whereas a higher resting cortisol level predicted fewer. Baseline cortisol moderated the effect: only people with low baseline levels were hurt by a big stress response.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the reason stress-memory studies keep disagreeing is that the same stress response does different things depending on where a person starts, and that stress mainly damages the ability to tell a real memory from a plausible lure.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Same stress, opposite memory outcome, depending on your resting hormone level — and the damage shows up as confident false memories rather than blanks. Open on an exam or a police interview. Call Jiongjiong Yang (Peking University) and ask whether baseline cortisol could explain the wider replication mess in stress research.
- **Wider angle:** For a legal or criminal-justice-focused outlet: what this means for eyewitness testimony, where stressed witnesses are questioned precisely when their false alarm rate may be highest. A reported piece with memory researchers and interviewing specialists.

**Caveats:** Small N per experiment (63 and 56); single-center laboratory paradigm with word pairs, far from real-world memory; between-groups stress manipulation; salivary cortisol is a noisy single-sample measure; population is likely young university students.

### 9. Sending clowns into dementia wards changes how families experience visiting

**Journal:** *Dementia (London)* | **Published:** 16 September 2026
**PMID:** 42749305 | **DOI:** 10.1177/14713012261487540
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a warm, vivid, genuinely unexamined question (the effect on relatives, not patients), but qualitative and small.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 11 family members and 20 staff across four long-term care homes in eastern Australia about "elder clowning" — trained performers who improvise individualized, person-centered interactions with residents who have dementia. Two themes emerged: the emotional experience of visiting was transformed, easing dread before visits and distress afterwards; and shared moments of laughter supported reminiscence, memory-making and family connection. Relatives also picked up dementia-specific communication techniques from watching the clowns and used them independently.

**Why it matters:** Interventions for dementia are evaluated on the person with dementia; this is early evidence that the benefit spills over to the relatives who do most of the emotional work — and that the spillover includes transferable skills.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The image sells it: a professional clown gets a response from a mother who has not responded to her own daughter in months. Lead on the quoted moment — "someone's acknowledged my mum". Call Sarah Roberts and Michelle DiGiacomo at UTS's IMPACCT group, and ask what exactly the clowns do that relatives can copy.
- **Wider angle:** For an arts-and-health or general-interest outlet: the professionalization of medical clowning, from pediatric wards to dementia care — how performers are trained, what the trial evidence actually shows, and why some families decline to take part at all.

**Caveats:** Small N (11 family members, 20 staff); qualitative interviews with self-selected participants likely to view the program favorably; four homes in one region; no measurement of outcomes in the residents themselves; no control condition.

### 10. What is actually happening in the brain when blindness brings hallucinations

**Journal:** *Brain* | **Published:** 16 September 2026
**PMID:** 42747922 | **DOI:** 10.1093/brain/awag317
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — an under-covered condition that readers find arresting, but this is a review rather than new data.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (1 hits)

**The study:** This review synthesizes neuroimaging, neurophysiological, structural and biochemical evidence on Charles Bonnet syndrome, in which people losing their sight experience vivid visual hallucinations without psychiatric illness. The authors conclude the evidence points to cortical hyperexcitability and compensatory reorganization following visual deprivation, with brain-wide changes in connectivity extending beyond visual cortex into attentional networks, and probable involvement of GABAergic and cholinergic signaling. Structural changes specific to the syndrome, as opposed to blindness generally, remain elusive.

**Why it matters:** Charles Bonnet syndrome is common in sight loss, badly underrecognized, and frequently misread as psychosis by patients and clinicians alike; it is also a natural experiment in what a visual cortex does when starved of input.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** People going blind who see faces, patterns and figures in impossible detail — and who often keep silent for fear of being thought mad. The hook is the mechanism: a visual system so starved of input that it generates its own. Open on a patient who took years to tell an ophthalmologist. Call Geraint Plant (UCL) or D. Samuel Schwarzkopf (Auckland), and ask about the proposal to model the syndrome with deep neural networks deprived of input.
- **Wider angle:** For a general-interest or long-read outlet: hallucination as a normal property of predictive brains. A feature threading Charles Bonnet syndrome together with musical hallucinations in deafness, phantom limbs, and sensory-deprivation experiments — what they collectively say about how much of ordinary perception is invented.

**Caveats:** Narrative review, not primary research; the underlying literature is methodologically heterogeneous and dominated by small samples and case studies; mechanistic claims are inferential.

### 11. Exercise gives a small but real lift to memory in people with depression

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 25 August 2026 (issue 1 December 2026)
**PMID:** 42641961 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122418
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — first meta-analysis pooling RCTs on this specific outcome, with moderate-certainty evidence; the effect is small and no exercise variable moderated it.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** A three-level meta-analysis of 13 randomized controlled trials (1098 participants) tested whether exercise interventions improve episodic memory — remembering specific events — in people with depression. The pooled effect was small to moderate and statistically robust (Hedges' g = 0.21), with similar gains for immediate and delayed recall. No exercise characteristic — type, intensity, duration, frequency — moderated the effect, and certainty of evidence was rated moderate.

**Why it matters:** Memory complaints are one of the most disabling residual features of depression and are poorly addressed by antidepressants; this suggests exercise helps modestly, and that the specific prescription may matter less than doing something.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The useful nuance is the absence of a dose-response: across 13 trials, nothing about *how* people exercised predicted how much their memory improved. Open on someone in recovery from depression who can function at work but cannot remember last week. Call Xue Xia (University of Health and Rehabilitation Sciences, Qingdao) and ask what "no moderators" really means when the trials are this heterogeneous.
- **Wider angle:** For a health or clinical outlet: cognitive symptoms as the neglected half of depression. Why trials measure mood and not memory, why regulators do not require cognitive endpoints, and what patients say about which symptom bothers them most.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of existing trials; only 13 studies with inconsistent memory instruments; small effect size; short follow-up in component trials; risk of unblinded outcome assessment in exercise RCTs; publication bias hard to exclude at this number of studies.

### 12. Aggression, insomnia and loneliness explain much of the link between childhood trauma and suicide risk

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 25 August 2026 (issue 1 December 2026)
**PMID:** 42637149 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122414
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — large clinical cohort and a careful mediation model, but cross-sectional, which undercuts the whole idea of mediation.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using 1572 people with a 12-month depressive or anxiety disorder from the Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety, researchers tested which psychosocial, personality, lifestyle and biological variables statistically account for the link between childhood trauma severity and either suicidal ideation alone or ideation with an attempt. Childhood trauma raised the odds of ideation (OR 1.51) and, far more, of ideation with an attempt (OR 2.68). Multiple-mediator models cut the direct association by 66 percent and 39 percent respectively, with aggression, insomnia and low social support surviving for both outcomes; interleukin-6 accounted for only a small slice of the attempt pathway.

**Why it matters:** It picks out a handful of potentially modifiable links in a chain that is otherwise fixed in the past — though the cross-sectional design means these are statistical, not demonstrated, pathways.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Childhood trauma nearly triples the odds of a suicide attempt in people with depression or anxiety — and two-thirds of that statistical link runs through things that can, in principle, be treated now: sleep, aggression, isolation. Call Jasper Wiebenga or Brenda Penninx (Amsterdam UMC / NESARC-scale NESDA cohort). Be scrupulous: no clinical claims from cross-sectional mediation.
- **Wider angle:** For a specialist psychiatry or policy outlet: the inflammation hypothesis of suicide gets a lukewarm result here, with IL-6 explaining very little. A piece on how immune markers have fared as predictors of suicidal behavior once psychosocial variables are in the model.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design — the authors themselves note causal inference is not possible, and mediation models require temporal ordering they do not have; childhood trauma is retrospectively recalled; suicide attempts self-reported; clinical sample of people with depressive or anxiety disorders, so it does not generalize to the general population.

### 13. Cannabis use disorder tracks with suicide attempts — except in people with bipolar disorder, where it barely moves quality of life

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychiatric Research* | **Published:** 27 August 2026 (issue November 2026)
**PMID:** 42664567 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2026.08.026
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a nationally representative sample and a genuinely odd dissociation, but secondary analysis of cross-sectional survey data.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Drawing on NESARC-III, researchers compared 753 adults with lifetime bipolar I disorder against 35,556 without, across levels of DSM-5 cannabis use disorder severity. Lifetime suicide attempts were reported by 34 percent of people with both bipolar I and severe cannabis use disorder, against 17 percent with severe CUD and no bipolar diagnosis. But while mental health quality of life fell steadily with CUD severity in people without bipolar disorder, in people with bipolar disorder it did not vary with CUD severity at all. Physical health quality of life differed by neither.

**Why it matters:** It suggests bipolar disorder sets a floor on self-reported mental wellbeing that heavy cannabis use does not lower further, even as the association with lifetime suicide attempts is much stronger — a reminder that quality-of-life scores and risk of harm can come apart.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead on the dissociation: in bipolar disorder, escalating cannabis problems go with double the rate of suicide attempts but no measurable drop in reported quality of life. Open on why that makes cannabis problems easy for a clinician to miss. Call Ty Schepis (Texas State University) and ask whether it is a ceiling effect in the instrument or something real.
- **Wider angle:** For a drug-policy or health outlet: as cannabis legalizes and potency climbs, what happens to the subgroup for whom it is riskiest? A reported piece on how legalization frameworks handle — or ignore — people with serious mental illness.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of cross-sectional national survey data (cannot establish causation or direction); lifetime diagnoses and lifetime suicide attempts both retrospectively self-reported; NESARC-III data now over a decade old and predates high-potency product markets; bipolar subgroup with severe CUD is small, giving wide confidence intervals.

### 14. Detained teenagers reach psychiatric emergency rooms with insomnia and suicide attempts, not aggression

**Journal:** *Criminal Behaviour and Mental Health* | **Published:** 16 September 2026
**PMID:** 42748415 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cbm.70051
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — directly contradicts the "disruptive behavior" stereotype using a full year of consecutive presentations; single-center retrospective record review.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers reviewed all 1206 adolescent presentations to a public child and adolescent psychiatric emergency service in Brazil over one year, of which 160 (13 percent) involved adolescents in custodial or semi-custodial detention. Compared with community-living peers, detained adolescents were more likely to present with insomnia (43 versus 19 percent) and suicide attempts (26 versus 18 percent), and carried more stress-related and adjustment disorder, conduct disorder and substance use diagnoses. **Why it matters:** Detained young people are widely assumed to reach mental health services because of disruptive behavior; here they arrive in acute distress, which implies a different kind of screening inside detention facilities.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The stereotype is aggression; the data say sleeplessness and self-harm. Open on the emergency clinician who expected one and found the other. Call Débora Marques de Miranda (Federal University of Minas Gerais) and ask what a 43 percent insomnia rate inside detention actually reflects — the environment, withdrawal, or trauma.
- **Wider angle:** For a social-affairs or investigative outlet: sleep as a neglected instrument of harm in youth custody. A reported piece on lights, noise, cell checks and night-time distress across juvenile detention systems, and the small but growing literature linking custodial sleep conditions to self-harm.

**Caveats:** Retrospective review of clinical records, single center, one city in Brazil; diagnoses made in emergency conditions; 160 detained adolescents is a modest subgroup; no data on adolescents in detention who never reached the service; findings may not generalize to other justice systems.

### 15. Boys and girls with autism turn out to look developmentally alike — with one exception

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** 16 September 2026
**PMID:** 42748595 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107803
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a mostly null result in a large clinical sample, which is useful and rarely published; limited by uncertainty about whether the tool could detect differences anyway.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers analyzed C-PEP-3 developmental profiles from 1050 children with autism (834 boys, 216 girls; aged 2–10) assessed in Beijing between 2021 and 2024. After controlling for age and correcting for multiple comparisons, only one of seven developmental domains differed by sex, and only in the youngest group: girls aged 2–3 had smaller delays in cognitive performance than boys (Cohen's d = −0.36). Nothing differed at ages 4–6 or 7–10.

**Why it matters:** Sex differences in autism are widely assumed to be pervasive and are increasingly used to explain late diagnosis in girls; in a large Chinese clinical sample they are almost entirely absent on this standard developmental profile — although the authors concede the instrument may be too coarse to detect them.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** A large sample, a careful analysis, and essentially nothing: the story is the null. Open on a clinician looking for the "female autism profile" and not finding it. ## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Insomnia keeps turning up as a marker rather than a mechanism.** The previous digest's harmonized Australian–Swedish cohorts found that broken sleep, not oxygen desaturation, carried the depression signal (PMID 42663413). The new batch pushes that further in two directions: the 10-year PRISMO military cohort finds insomnia rises and falls *with* PTSD and depression but never precedes them (PMID 42750113), and insomnia appears as one of three surviving statistical mediators between childhood trauma and suicide attempt (PMID 42637149), plus the dominant presenting complaint among detained adolescents in a Brazilian psychiatric emergency service (PMID 42748415). Across two runs, insomnia is everywhere and causal in nothing.
- **Nulls that puncture policy assumptions.** The MH17 "closure" null (PMID 42635331) and the precision psychiatry readiness audit (PMID 42657684) in the previous run are matched by the new digest's finding that state parity laws and larger mental health budgets shift only cost-related barriers, not treatment receipt, unmet need, overdose or suicide mortality (PMID 42750086), and by the near-total absence of sex differences in developmental profiles of 1050 autistic children (PMID 42748595). Both batches are dominated by well-powered results that decline to confirm the expected story.
- **Exercise as a modest, non-specific lever.** The previous run's dissociation between mental and physical fatigue in predicting activity (PMID 42543096) sits next to two new items: 12 minutes of weekly vigorous activity matching 150 minutes of moderate activity for mortality in 147,414 accelerometer-tracked adults (PMID 42745313), and a small but robust memory benefit of exercise in depression with no moderation by type, intensity, duration or frequency (PMID 42641961). The recurring message is that intensity and specifics matter less than the units guidelines are written in imply.

**Advances**

- **Trauma cohort science gets more ambitious.** The previous digest's post-trauma intervention work was analogue-lab (psychological first aid, PMID 42561812) or retrospective-longitudinal (MH17, PMID 42635331). The new Nova Protocol (PMID 42735067) is prospective from weeks after a mass-casualty event, with wearables, MRI, endocrine markers and a naturalistic drug-state exposure — the design that PFA-style questions actually need. The PRISMO cohort (PMID 42750113) supplies the decade-long comparator.
- **PTSD's framing shifts from fear to morality.** Following the previous run's grief-structure work (PMIDs 42635331, 42635333), the new validation and latent profile analysis of moral injury in 455 Dutch veterans (PMID 42734648) extends the same move — decomposing a broad distress category into distinguishable constructs with their own correlates, here poorer family functioning.
- **From correlational brain markers to causal manipulation.** Where the previous batch offered observational signatures (post-stroke EEG, PMID 42663449; DTI-ALPS and antipsychotic polypharmacy, PMID 42551238), the new digest includes a direct causal intervention: anodal stimulation of the left inferior frontal gyrus making metaphors *less* comprehensible (PMID 42667874) — with the same replication-fragility caveat attached.

**Counterpoints**

- **Interventions that work, but not on what was measured.** The previous run's storyline was psychological help failing or backfiring (PMIDs 42546730, 42561812). The new batch complicates it: elder clowning transformed the experience of *relatives* rather than being evaluated on residents (PMID 42749305), and the Arabic PTSD Coach app reduced sadness while leaving happiness untouched (PMID 42747789). Benefit is real but lands off the target the trial was pointed at.
- **Sleep as a treatment target.** The previous digest implied insomnia was the causally relevant sleep variable for mood (PMID 42663413); the new within-person modeling of PRISMO (PMID 42750113) argues it is a co-traveler that does not predict later PTSD or depression at all. These two need reconciling, and the tension is itself a story.

**New themes**

- **Heritability and genetics return.** The Danish twin estimate of 0.60 for postpartum depression (PMID 42648551) opens a genetic strand entirely absent from the previous digest.
- **Service-level inequity.** People with psychotic disorders are markedly less likely to have planned medication for opioid use disorder than those with depression or anxiety (PMID 42743214), joining the parity-law null (PMID 42750086) to make access and delivery, rather than mechanism, a live theme.
- **Appetite, taste and GLP-1 drugs.** Laboratory measures putting numbers on "food noise" and finding blunted sweet detection but otherwise intact chemosensation (PMID 42641845) is new territory for this digest.
- **Hallucination and deprived cortex.** The Charles Bonnet syndrome review (PMID 42747922) restores a perception-science strand that in the previous run was represented by featureless object tracking (PMID 42537447).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Study after study in this batch finds that a treatment or policy moves one dial and leaves the obvious neighboring dial untouched — and that which dial you chose to measure decides whether anything "worked." GLP-1 drugs quiet food noise, craving and reward while food itself tastes much the same (PMID 42641845); an Arabic self-help app lowered sadness with no model fitting happiness at all (PMID 42747789); in bipolar I disorder, escalating cannabis use disorder goes with double the rate of lifetime suicide attempts yet no change in self-rated mental health quality of life (PMID 42664567); elder clowning's documented benefit falls on visiting relatives, who were never the intended outcome (PMID 42749305); exercise in depression lifts episodic memory with no dose-response (PMID 42641961); and mental health parity laws remove the price barrier while treatment receipt and unmet need stay flat (PMID 42750086).

**Why pitch this now:** Regulators, insurers and app developers are all currently deciding which endpoints count as evidence of benefit in mental health, and this batch shows the standard composite measures — quality-of-life scores, mood scales, "treatment receipt" — systematically missing effects that patients and families describe clearly.

**Angle:** A reported feature on outcome measurement as the hidden decision in mental health research: who picks the endpoint, why negative and positive affect were ever assumed to be one scale, and what gets classified as ineffective simply because nobody measured the thing that changed. Aimed at readers who have filled in a PHQ-9 or been told a treatment "didn't show a benefit."

**Potential headlines:**
- The treatments are working, just not on the things being measured
- An app made people less sad and no happier, and that broke the scoring sheet
- Your treatment may be helping in a way the questionnaire cannot see

- **New Scientist Features** — the Mind section can carry the GLP-1 food-noise and sadness-versus-happiness findings as concrete anchors for a measurement story readers will recognize from their own forms.
- **STAT** — endpoint selection is squarely its beat, and the parity-law null plus the opioid-medication disparity give it a regulatory and payer spine.
