### 1. A progesterone pill cuts cannabis cravings in women — but does nothing for men

**Journal:** *Psychopharmacology* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 10
**PMID:** 42429926 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s00213-026-07128-2
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Strong RCT design with a clear sex-specific counterintuitive finding (a reproductive hormone cutting drug cravings) gives it high counterintuitive appeal and a compelling societal hook, with modest penalty for short follow-up and single-center design.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at the Medical University of South Carolina ran a randomised, placebo-controlled trial in which 148 adults with cannabis use disorder (82 male, 66 female) took progesterone 200 mg twice daily or placebo for one week while abstaining from cannabis. At the end of treatment, participants underwent a laboratory stress task (Trier Social Stress Test) followed by a two-week medication-free follow-up. Progesterone reduced stress-induced cannabis craving in women but not men, and normalised the relationship between subjective stress and cortisol in women and between stress and craving in both sexes. Higher stress-induced cortisol responses were also associated with longer time to first cannabis use during follow-up across the whole sample.

**Why it matters:** Cannabis use disorder lacks approved pharmacotherapies, and stress-triggered relapse is a leading obstacle to recovery; these findings suggest that sex-hormone-based treatments could open a personalised medicine avenue specifically for women.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "A progesterone pill cuts cannabis cravings in women — but does nothing for men" A hormone usually associated with pregnancy may be the key to helping women quit cannabis — but only women. In the first randomised trial of its kind, progesterone — a naturally occurring sex hormone — slashed stress-triggered cannabis cravings in women with cannabis use disorder but had no equivalent effect in men. The finding reframes relapse not just as a behavioural problem but as one shaped by biology and hormonal state, and points toward a future where addiction treatment is tailored by sex. With cannabis use disorder on the rise globally and zero approved medications, the stakes are high. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are fascinated by sex differences in the brain and by novel, surprising treatments for addiction.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** short follow-up, single-center, non-treatment-seeking sample, industry funding [Achieve Life Sciences, Indivior, Jazz Pharmaceuticals, Aelis Farma, PleoPharma — disclosed by authors]. Only one week of treatment; participants were not seeking treatment (may not represent typical patients); single US centre; some authors have industry ties; follow-up was only two weeks, so long-term relapse prevention is unknown

### 2. Being shamed by family for your parenting predicts burnout — but social support only helps if you're not already comparing yourself to others

**Journal:** *Journal of Family Psychology* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 16
**PMID:** 42461606 | **DOI:** 10.1037/fam0001495
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — Strong societal hook around parental burnout and family shaming, decent cross-cultural sample (N=1,373), and counterintuitive moderation finding; docked for cross-sectional self-report design and the exploratory moderation being Vietnam-specific.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** A cross-sectional, cross-cultural study surveyed 1,373 parents from the UK and Vietnam (mean age ~38) about perceived social support, personal social comparison, critical comparison and shaming by family members, and parental burnout. Perceived social support was negatively associated with burnout, while social comparison and family shaming were positively associated with it — patterns consistent across both countries. However, an unexpected moderation emerged: in the Vietnamese sample only, social support was a stronger buffer against burnout for parents who compared themselves more to others, a pattern not found in the UK.

**Why it matters:** The finding that family shaming — not just lack of support — independently predicts parental burnout highlights a toxic group dynamic that prevention programmes have largely overlooked.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Being judged by your family for your parenting is a bigger burnout risk than getting no support at all" A study of 1,373 parents across two countries finds that critical shaming by family members is independently linked to parental burnout — even after accounting for how much support parents receive. We're often told that social support protects parents from burning out — and it does. But this cross-cultural study adds a darker twist: being criticised or shamed by family members predicts burnout on top of and independent of whatever support parents receive. Comparing yourself unfavourably to other parents also raises risk. Together these findings suggest that the social world around parents can simultaneously help and harm, and that prevention programmes ignoring the 'comparison and shame' dimension may be missing a key driver of the global parental burnout epidemic. Why it fits: Parental burnout, social comparison, and the hidden harms of family judgment are topics NS Mind readers encounter in their own lives and find compellingly counter to the 'more support = better' narrative.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** observational design, cross-sectional, self-reported outcomes. Cross-sectional design means causation cannot be established — burned-out parents may perceive more shaming. Self-reported measures throughout. The social-support moderation finding was exploratory and emerged only in Vietnam, possibly reflecting collectivist cultural norms around family criticism that don't translate directly to Western contexts; journalists should not present it as a universal finding *Editor's fact-check note: The Vietnam-specific moderation finding is noted as exploratory in the abstract; this is flagged in caveats_to_flag. Sample size N=1,373 is well above threshold so no small-sample flag. Cross-cultural comparison across only two countries (UK and Vietnam) is a limitation; the Vietnam-specific moderation may not generalise globally.*

### 3. It's not just bad neighbourhoods that raise ADHD risk — it's the unpredictability of parenting they produce

**Journal:** *Journal of Family Psychology* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 13
**PMID:** 42441638 | **DOI:** 10.1037/fam0001518
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — Counterintuitive focus on parenting unpredictability (not just quality) as the mechanism linking poverty to ADHD is a strong NS Mind hook, with a decent longitudinal sample and clear societal relevance; docked for observational design and single-center limitations.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at the University of Rochester followed 235 families with young children (average age ~3 at baseline) across three annual waves, measuring neighbourhood deprivation via census data, maternal parenting behaviour through observed mother-child interactions, and child ADHD symptoms. They found that neighbourhood risk was associated with greater 'parenting entropy' — unpredictability in how mothers responded — and that this inconsistency, rather than average parenting quality alone, was linked to higher child ADHD symptoms over time. Crucially, the effect was specific to discipline contexts involving emotional arousal ('hot') rather than calm rule-setting ('cool') contexts.

**Why it matters:** The finding suggests that interventions targeting neighbourhood stress may reduce ADHD risk by helping parents become more consistent, not just warmer or stricter — a potentially important reframe for public health programmes.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "It's not a rough neighbourhood that raises ADHD risk — it's the parenting chaos it creates" Growing up in a deprived area is linked to higher ADHD symptoms in children — but a new study suggests the real culprit is the unpredictability of parenting that neighbourhood stress produces, not deprivation itself. Researchers tracked 235 families from toddlerhood across three years and found that neighbourhood deprivation predicted ADHD symptoms not through average parenting quality but through parenting 'entropy' — erratic swings in how mothers responded to their children. Strikingly, the effect only appeared in emotionally charged discipline moments, not calm instructional ones. This suggests that poverty's effect on children's attention may be partly mediated by the stress-induced inconsistency it creates in parents — a finding that reframes both the cause and the potential fix. Why it fits: NS Mind readers care deeply about child development, mental health inequalities, and the hidden mechanisms linking social disadvantage to brain and behaviour.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** observational design, single-center, self-reported outcomes (partially), small-to-moderate sample. Observational longitudinal design cannot prove causation; single university-based US sample limits generalisability; some measures relied on parental report *Editor's fact-check note: Sample size is N=235 families, which is above the N<100 small-sample threshold; removed 'small sample' flag. ADHD symptoms were assessed via multi-informant methods (not solely self-report), but some measures involved parental report, so 'self-reported outcomes (partially)' is retained as a caveat.*

### 4. Your brain partially repairs itself after an all-nighter — but not all functions recover equally

**Journal:** *Sleep* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 13
**PMID:** 41757508 | **DOI:** 10.1093/sleep/zsag054
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — Counterintuitive dissociation between feeling sleepy and actually performing well is a strong NS Mind hook, with decent methodology, though the small lab sample and short protocol limit generalisability.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers kept 54 healthy adults awake for 35 consecutive hours while repeatedly testing their reaction speed, cognitive processing, and subjective sleepiness. Using a two-process model of sleep regulation plus a meta-analysis of published data, they found that objective cognitive performance partially bounced back in the evening — a 'circadian rescue' of 33–52% for reaction time and 46% for processing speed — but subjective feelings of sleepiness recovered only 23.5%, suggesting the body's internal clock rescues mental performance more than it rescues how tired you feel.

**Why it matters:** Understanding which cognitive abilities recover during the evening 'second wind' after sleep loss — and which don't — has implications for safety-critical industries like medicine, aviation, and long-haul transport where workers must judge their own fitness to perform.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "You can feel exhausted but still think clearly — your body clock is overriding your tiredness" After a full night without sleep, your brain quietly stages a partial comeback in the evening — even while you still feel wrecked. A new study kept 54 people awake for 35 hours straight and found their reaction times and processing speed partially recovered in the early evening, driven by the circadian clock — yet their subjective sleepiness barely budged. The mismatch is striking: your internal clock can rescue your performance even when you're convinced you're too tired to function. This has real consequences for anyone who uses tiredness as their gauge for whether they're safe to drive, fly, or make high-stakes decisions. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are fascinated by the gap between how we feel and how we actually perform — this finding reframes the popular idea that exhaustion and cognitive impairment go hand in hand.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** small sample (N<100), single-center, short follow-up. Small laboratory sample (N=54), all-nighter protocol may not reflect real-world shift patterns, and recovery rates varied by task type so the finding should not be over-generalised

### 5. Terror attack survivors spend years imagining how they could have saved others — or killed the gunman

**Journal:** *Scandinavian Journal of Psychology* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 11
**PMID:** 42435482 | **DOI:** 10.1111/sjop.70131
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — Strong topic fit (trauma, emotion, cognition), decent sample for a qualitative study, counterintuitive emphasis on other-directed rather than self-directed imagined outcomes, but caveats around single event/single country and self-report keep the score from going higher.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers conducted thematic analysis of counterfactual thoughts ('what if' thinking) reported by 220 survivors of the 2011 Utøya terrorist attack, approximately 8.5 years after the event. Participants (52.3% female, mean age 27.5) described their most common 'what if' scenarios in rich detail. Most antecedents involved self-referent actions during the attack — such as helping others, being in a different location, or overpowering the attacker — while the majority of imagined outcomes focused on how others who died could have survived. Many survivors also described counterfactual thoughts involving self-blame.

**Why it matters:** Understanding the specific structure and content of post-trauma 'what if' thinking — including its strong other-focused and self-blaming character — could help clinicians better identify and address this form of distress in trauma survivors, without implying any change in current treatment practice.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Terror attack survivors spend years imagining how they could have saved others — not themselves" Nearly a decade after surviving a massacre, most Utøya attack survivors' 'what if' thoughts weren't about saving themselves — they were about saving the people who died. Researchers analysed the 'what if' thinking of 220 survivors of the 2011 Utøya shooting, 8.5 years on. Most imagined taking actions during the attack — helping, moving, or even overpowering the gunman — with the hoped-for outcome being that someone else would have survived. The finding upends a common assumption that post-trauma counterfactual thinking is primarily self-protective, and raises important questions about guilt, altruism, and how we mentally rewrite catastrophe. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are drawn to counterintuitive findings about how the mind processes extreme experience; this challenges the assumption that trauma survivors are primarily self-focused in their regret.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** observational design, self-reported outcomes, single-center, single-event sample (Utøya attack survivors in Norway). Qualitative thematic analysis means findings describe patterns rather than frequencies in a statistically rigorous sense; all participants survived a single, specific attack in Norway, so generalisability to other trauma types or cultures is uncertain; self-reported, retrospective accounts may be shaped by years of memory consolidation and prior therapy *Editor's fact-check note: N=220 confirmed against abstract. Design is qualitative/thematic analysis — noted as observational. The study population is Norwegian and specific to a single mass-casualty event; generalisability to other trauma populations or non-Scandinavian contexts should be treated with caution, though the psychological mechanisms discussed (counterfactual thinking, self-blame) are broadly studied cross-culturally.*

### 6. People can accurately sense how hard a mental switch was — even when they never actually responded

**Journal:** *Consciousness and Cognition* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 11
**PMID:** 42435727 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.concog.2026.104095
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — Counterintuitive finding about introspection and metacognition earns points for topic fit and novelty, but the likely small and unreported sample size, highly controlled lab paradigm, and relatively technical subject matter limit broad NS Mind appeal.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at the University of Regensburg ran two laboratory experiments examining how people form introspective judgements about their own mental performance during task-switching — where participants rapidly alternate between different cognitive tasks. In Experiment 1, participants (n not specified in abstract) reliably recognised whether they had just switched tasks or repeated one, even within a short 1-second window. In Experiment 2, when participants were told not to respond on certain trials (NoGo trials), they still showed 'introspective switch costs' — correctly sensing that switching tasks felt harder — even though they had produced no overt response to reflect on, suggesting that theory-based mental knowledge, not just physical feedback, underlies self-assessment of cognitive effort.

**Why it matters:** The finding suggests that humans have a surprisingly sophisticated internal model of their own cognitive processes that operates independently of direct experience, with implications for understanding metacognition, decision-making, and how reliably people can self-assess mental effort.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Your brain knows a mental switch was hard — even when you did absolutely nothing" When people are stopped from responding in a cognitive task, they can still accurately sense how difficult the mental switch was — with no physical action to go on. A new study finds that people don't need to actually do something to know how hard it was — they can accurately judge the difficulty of a mental switch even on trials where they were instructed not to respond at all. This suggests the brain holds a working theory of its own operations, separate from physical feedback. The finding has wider implications for understanding how reliable our self-assessments of mental effort really are — relevant to everything from exam performance to workplace decisions. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are fascinated by the inner workings of the mind; this finding challenges the intuition that self-knowledge requires direct experience.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** small sample (N not reported in abstract — likely <100 based on lab paradigm), single-center, short follow-up. Sample size is not reported in the abstract and is likely small for a lab-based cognitive study; the artificial task-switching paradigm may not map cleanly onto real-world cognitive switching; findings are preliminary and replication in larger, more diverse samples is needed *Editor's fact-check note: The abstract does not report a specific N for either experiment; the summary therefore omits a precise sample size and flags this uncertainty. ### 7. Popular sleep drug causes nightmares far more often in younger patients than in older ones

**Journal:** *Journal of Clinical Psychopharmacology* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 17
**PMID:** 42165432 | **DOI:** 10.1097/JCP.0000000000002194
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — Clinically relevant sleep finding with a counterintuitive age gradient, but significantly limited by retrospective single-center design, a Japan-specific prescribing context (tandospirone is rarely used in the US/UK), and a small nightmare subgroup, keeping the score moderate.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at the University of Miyazaki Hospital in Japan retrospectively examined records of 327 psychiatric patients prescribed lemborexant (a dual orexin receptor antagonist) for insomnia between January 2020 and September 2024. The overall incidence of nightmares was 8.0%, higher than rates seen in previous clinical trials. Multivariate analysis found that younger age (10–29 years, with an ROC cutoff of ≤49 years), concurrent use of the anxiolytic tandospirone, and switching from benzodiazepines were all independently associated with a significantly greater risk of nightmares and earlier nightmare onset.

**Why it matters:** Lemborexant is increasingly prescribed for insomnia in people with psychiatric disorders — populations already vulnerable to disturbed sleep — so identifying who is at greatest risk of nightmares could help clinicians make better prescribing decisions.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Popular sleep drug causes nightmares far more often in younger patients than in older ones" A widely used insomnia drug is triggering nightmares at twice the rate previously reported in clinical trials — and younger patients are most at risk. Lemborexant, one of the newer generation of sleep drugs that works by blocking the brain's wake-promoting orexin system, is now widely prescribed — including to people with existing psychiatric conditions. But a real-world study found nightmares occurring in 8% of patients, significantly more than in controlled trials, with people under 50 at substantially higher risk. The finding raises questions about how well clinical-trial populations represent everyday patients, and whether the same brain pathways that regulate wakefulness also govern dream intensity. Why it fits: Sleep disorders and the side-effects of psychiatric medications are perennial NS Mind topics, and an unexpected age-reversal effect (older patients doing better) is inherently counterintuitive.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** retrospective design, single-center, observational design, self-reported outcomes, small nightmare subgroup (N=26 of 327), Japan-specific clinical context (tandospirone use pattern), short follow-up. Retrospective chart review from a single Japanese hospital; tandospirone (a co-prescribed drug linked to nightmare risk) is rarely used outside Japan, limiting direct generalisability; nightmare subgroup is small; causality cannot be established from observational data *Editor's fact-check note: Abstract does not specify that outcomes were self-reported; nightmare identification was based on clinical records in a retrospective chart review, so 'self-reported outcomes' flag is partially applicable but not fully confirmed — noted for transparency. **The study:** Researchers in Spain tested the psychometric properties of the Impact of Illness Scale (IIS) in 141 adults (mean age 35.7 years, 51.8% female) with a first episode of psychosis. The authors conclude the IIS is a valid and reliable tool for capturing patients' subjective experience of how psychosis has changed their life.

**Why it matters:** Measuring how psychosis affects daily life from the patient's own perspective — not just clinician-rated symptoms — could improve how researchers and services evaluate recovery and quality of life in early psychosis.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "How psychosis reshapes your life matters more than your symptom score — and now there's a validated way to measure it" A patient's own sense of how psychosis has derailed their life turns out to be measurable, reliable, and distinct from what clinicians capture in symptom checklists. Spanish researchers have validated a scale that captures the subjective life impact of a first psychotic episode — measuring disruption to personal, social, and vocational domains as the patient perceives it. The tool showed strong reliability and validity in 141 patients and correlated meaningfully with stigma and negative beliefs but not just symptom severity. The finding highlights that recovery from psychosis is about more than reducing hallucinations — and that services may be missing a crucial dimension of patient experience. Why it fits: NS Mind readers care about the gap between clinical measurement and lived experience, especially in conditions as stigmatised as psychosis.
