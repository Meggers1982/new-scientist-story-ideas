### 1. Online gamblers return faster after winning than after losing — the opposite of what harm models assume

**Journal:** *Journal of Behavioral Addictions* | **Published:** 2026 Aug 21
**PMID:** 42627694 | **DOI:** 10.1556/2006.2025.00454
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 9/10 — Large pre-registered dataset, strongly counterintuitive finding that directly challenges existing harm models, clear societal hook around problem gambling policy — one of the strongest submissions in this batch.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Using a large-scale behavioural dataset of 11,156 online casino gamblers and approximately 170 million bets, researchers conducted pre-registered analyses to examine how quickly gamblers returned for their next session depending on whether their previous session ended in a win or a loss. Contrary to the assumption that loss-chasing drives rapid return, gamblers came back faster after winning sessions than losing sessions. Return speed also increased with larger prior outcomes (both wins and losses). Slot machine players and high-involvement gamblers showed less sensitivity to prior outcomes overall, suggesting habituation.

**Why it matters:** The finding suggests that winning — not just losing — may be a trigger for potentially harmful return gambling, which could require a rethink of how harm-detection algorithms and responsible gambling tools are designed.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Gamblers chase wins just as much as losses — and return to the casino faster after winning" Conventional wisdom says problem gamblers are driven back by losses they want to recover — but a study of 170 million bets finds they return even faster after winning. Researchers analysed the return patterns of over 11,000 real online casino players and found that a winning session, not a losing one, was the stronger pull drawing gamblers back sooner. The effect was amplified by larger wins and losses alike. Heavy, high-involvement gamblers and slot players showed the least sensitivity to prior outcomes — a pattern the authors attribute to habituation. The pre-registered finding challenges the dominant 'loss-chasing' narrative and suggests that responsible gambling tools focused only on loss sequences may be missing a major part of the picture. Why it fits: NS Mind readers care about the psychology of decision-making, risk, and addiction; this finding upends a widely held model and has direct implications for how online platforms should protect users.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** observational design. Observational data from a single online gambling platform — causal direction cannot be established; findings may not generalise across all gambling products or regulatory environments; high-involvement vs low-involvement was defined by monetary and time expenditure, which may not map cleanly onto problem gambling severity *Editor's fact-check note: N=11,156 confirmed; ~170 million bets confirmed; pre-registered design noted as strength. **The study:** In a randomised crossover study, 11 healthy adults (mean age 23.3 years) were exposed to normobaric hypoxia (12% oxygen for 2 hours, equivalent to high-altitude air) and compared to a combined luminotherapy-plus-melatonin condition, both designed to simulate a 4-hour circadian phase advance. A small but cleverly designed randomised trial in Canada tested whether two hours of low-oxygen air (mimicking altitude) could shift participants' circadian rhythms, comparing it head-to-head with the gold-standard combination of morning light therapy and melatonin. The hypoxia nudged the body clock by about 35 minutes on average — less than the 78 minutes achieved by light-plus-melatonin, and not statistically significant. Yet the finding opens an entirely new line of inquiry: molecular clock pathways are sensitive to oxygen levels, and if the effect is real, it could eventually benefit people who cannot use light therapy. Why it fits: Sleep, jet lag and circadian biology are perennial NS Mind topics, and 'breathing thin air instead of popping melatonin' is exactly the kind of counterintuitive finding readers love.

**Why it matters:** The finding shifts focus from average emotional state to emotional instability as a key mechanism connecting impulsivity to substance use and sexual risk, suggesting interventions targeting mood regulation skills in volatile emotional moments may be more effective than those addressing general negative mood.

**Story angles:**

Conventional wisdom says people act recklessly when they feel bad.
