### Study 1: Your earliest memory dates from the same age whatever culture you grew up in
**PMID:** 42757868 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Headline generalizes a three-group New Zealand adolescent null to all cultures** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Your earliest memory dates from the same age whatever culture you grew up in"
- **Abstract says:** "This study included 268 New Zealand youth (12-21 years) who identified primarily as New Zealand Māori (NZM; n = 91), New Zealand European (NZE; n = 90), or New Zealand Chinese (NZC; n = 88)... The main finding was that of no cultural, gender, or age differences in the age of earliest memory"
- **Problem:** The null holds across three ethnic groups within a single country, all adolescents. "Whatever culture you grew up in" claims a universal the data cannot support, and a within-sample null is not evidence of equivalence across cultures generally. The abstract itself opens by affirming that "Cultural differences exist in early autobiographical memories."
- **Suggested fix:** "Three cultures, one age of earliest memory — at least among New Zealand teenagers"

**Issue B. "Carefully matched" sample is not supported by the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "in a carefully matched three-culture adolescent sample, it didn't"
- **Abstract says:** Reports group sizes only (n = 91 / 90 / 88); no matching procedure described.
- **Problem:** Similar group sizes are not the same as matching on age, sex, SES or education. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "in a three-culture adolescent sample with near-equal group sizes, it didn't"

**Issue C. NS fit score of 7 on self-dated, single-country null** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — a clean cross-cultural null that contradicts a textbook claim, though it rests on self-reported dating of memories."
- **Problem:** The central outcome (age at earliest memory) is entirely self-dated and unverifiable, the sample is one country and adolescent-only, and the claim being "overturned" is not directly tested against the original cross-cultural adult literature. A null of this kind is interesting but not strong enough to carry a 7.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10, with the same one-line justification.

### Study 2: Intrusive memories may not be the simple conditioned reflexes we assumed
**PMID:** 42759257 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. The cue-elicited increase in intrusions was specific to the Day 3 group** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The odor cue did trigger more intrusions than a control scent — but the intrusions were not specific to the film that odor had been paired with"
- **Abstract says:** "Although exposure to the odour cue on Day 3 produced more intrusive memories than a control odour, these were not selective for the associated film type."
- **Problem:** The digest drops the timing qualifier. The abstract reports this effect for the Day 3 exposure condition (n = 46), not for the odour cue overall, and the digest has already told readers that half the sample sprayed immediately.
- **Suggested fix:** "Among those who sprayed the odor three days after leaving the lab, the cue did trigger more intrusions than a control scent — but the intrusions were not specific to the film that odor had been paired with"

**Issue B. NS fit score of 7 on an analogue paradigm in 91 students** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — a deliberate, well-designed attempt to break a dominant clinical theory, and it partly succeeded; limited by an analogue trauma design in students."
- **Problem:** The headline claim ("a dominant clinical theory may be wrong") is large; the evidence is a single within-subject trauma-film study in 91 undergraduates with diary-based self-report and mixed results (partial cue-specificity in the lab). The abstract's own conclusion is hedged ("may be context dependent").
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10, noting the mixed rather than clean result.

### Study 3: A third of sexual abuse survivors with PTSD report genital arousal when the memory returns
**PMID:** 42704048 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline says "arousal" where the study measured self-reported "genital responses"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A third of sexual abuse survivors with PTSD report genital arousal when the memory returns"
- **Abstract says:** "Genital responses related to sexual abuse memories were reported by 34.4% (n = 364)."
- **Problem:** "Arousal" implies a subjective state; the construct here is a self-reported bodily response, and the digest's own wider angle rests on arousal non-concordance — the very distinction the headline collapses. The body text correctly uses "genital responses."
- **Suggested fix:** "A third of sexual abuse survivors with PTSD report genital responses when the memory returns"

Study facts otherwise check out: N = 1057, 84.7% female, mean age 38.81, 34.4% / 91.8% / 87.6% / 70.6% / 38.2%, disgust and self-hatred but not shame correlated with PTSD severity, younger age at abuse and more repeated abuse in the responder group. The 7/10 score is defensible given the sample size and clinician-administered PTSD severity ratings, with self-report limits already in Caveats.

### Study 4: Typing the first few digits helps you hold more in working memory
**PMID:** 42754727 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "A substantial part" of capacity overstates an unquantified effect** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It suggests a substantial part of what we call working memory capacity is a coordination problem rather than a hard storage limit"
- **Abstract says:** "Maxispan procedures were developed to test whether conventional span tasks underestimate working memory (WM) performance as people may not optimally coordinate distinct maintenance mechanisms... typing-based maxispan improved immediate serial recall relative to a simple-span control"
- **Problem:** No effect size is given in the abstract, so "a substantial part" is unverifiable and reads as a quantified claim. The supported statement is directional: conventional span tasks underestimate capacity.
- **Suggested fix:** "It suggests that some of what we measure as working memory capacity reflects how people coordinate maintenance strategies rather than a hard storage limit"

Design details (nine digits in Experiments 1–2, seven in Experiment 3; first four typed cumulatively; benefit for both typed and unrehearsed items; effect survives articulatory suppression; none preregistered) are accurately reported.

### Study 5: People who recall their lives in more detail tell their stories more often
**PMID:** 42742196 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Pitch extends the finding to SDAM, which the study did not assess** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Jun Hashimoto's group at Hiroshima University can speak to what this means for people at the low end of autobiographical memory — including those with severely deficient autobiographical memory (SDAM)."
- **Abstract says:** "This study used the experience sampling method to examine the relationship between individual differences in autobiographical memory detail and the frequency of memory sharing. Participants (n = 116)..." — a continuous individual-differences sample, no clinical or SDAM subgroup.
- **Problem:** As phrased, the pitch could lead a writer to present this as evidence about SDAM. The study measured normal-range variation in recall detail.
- **Suggested fix:** Add "— noting the study sampled normal-range variation and included no SDAM participants, so any extension to SDAM is the researchers' speculation."

N (116), eight signals a day for seven days, the detail–frequency association and the closeness-modulation dissociation are all accurately reported.

### Study 6: Choosing to act makes you remember better — and the brain signal shows it
**PMID:** 42753975 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Hommel described as senior author; Ma is the last/corresponding author** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Ke Ma at Southwest University, with Bernhard Hommel as senior author"
- **Abstract says:** Author order "Zhu R, Ma H, Hommel B, Ma K"; Ma K (Southwest University) is listed last and as a corresponding author; Hommel is at Shandong Normal University.
- **Problem:** Hommel is the third of four authors and at a different institution; Ma K occupies the senior-author position. A writer approaching "the senior author" would go to the wrong person.
- **Suggested fix:** "Ke Ma at Southwest University is the senior and corresponding author; Bernhard Hommel (Shandong Normal University) is a co-author and can situate this against decades of work on the self-reference effect."

The four-experiment structure, the volition-versus-consistency dissociation, the recollection/FN400/LPC results, and the failure in Experiment 2 with a post hoc explanation are all accurately conveyed.

### Study 7: Years on the football field damage memory directly; military service does it through PTSD
**PMID:** 42753419 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Causal language in the headline for a cross-sectional observational cohort** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Years on the football field damage memory directly; military service does it through PTSD"
- **Abstract says:** "Years of football play were directly associated with cognitive and neuropsychiatric outcomes, whereas history of military service contributed primarily through indirect pathways involving PTSD."
- **Problem:** "Damage" asserts causation from an observational, self-enrolled online cohort; "directly" in the statistical sense (a direct path in a simultaneous equation model) is not the same as "directly" in the everyday causal sense, and the headline exploits the ambiguity. The digest's own Caveats state "no causal inference possible," so the headline contradicts the caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "Years of football track with worse memory directly; military service only via PTSD"

**Issue B. Football also showed indirect effects via PTSD, and all effect sizes were very small** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "More years of football were directly associated with worse self-reported cognition, worse mood and worse performance on a computerized paired-associates memory test. Military service, by contrast, was associated with worse outcomes only indirectly, through PTSD."
- **Abstract says:** "Greater years of football showed direct (ECog: β = 0.08... BRIEF-A BRI: β = 0.09) and indirect (ECog: β = 0.01; p = 0.025...) associations with self-reported outcomes, and direct associations with worse computerized PAL First Attempt Memory scores (β = -0.07; p = 0.025)."
- **Problem:** Football had both direct *and* indirect (PTSD-mediated) associations; the digest's framing implies a clean either/or between the two exposures. All standardized betas are between 0.01 and 0.09 — statistically significant in a 3055-person sample but tiny, which a pitch built on "damage" should state.
- **Suggested fix:** "More years of football were associated with worse self-reported cognition, mood and computerized paired-associates memory both directly and, more weakly, via PTSD (all standardized βs 0.05–0.09). Military service was associated with worse self-reported outcomes only indirectly, through PTSD."

### Study 8: Talking yourself down after good news makes you read social situations more rigidly
**PMID:** 42727265 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. "Why it matters" hardens a hedged mechanism claim and converts a single induction into a habit** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It suggests the habit of deflating your own good feelings is not merely a symptom of low mood but a causal step that stiffens negative beliefs — a specific, trainable target for therapy."
- **Abstract says:** "These findings suggest that dampening may operate as a proximal cognitive-affective mechanism that reduces the uptake of positive belief-disconfirming evidence... Targeting dampening responses to positive emotion may strengthen belief updating and cognitive flexibility in interventions for depression and anxiety."
- **Problem:** Two leaps. First, the experiment induced dampening once in a laboratory session; it says nothing about the effect of a long-standing *habit* of dampening. Second, "is... a causal step" drops the authors' "may," and "trainable target" asserts an intervention benefit the study did not test (the sample was non-clinical and there was no follow-up).
- **Suggested fix:** "It suggests that dampening a positive feeling may be more than a symptom of low mood — in this single-session experiment it made people slower to revise negative readings of ambiguous social situations, which the authors argue makes it worth testing as a therapy target."

**Issue B. "After good news" misdescribes the manipulation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Talking yourself down after good news makes you read social situations more rigidly"
- **Abstract says:** "Participants completed a positive autobiographical memory induction and were randomly assigned to a dampening, amplifying, or control condition."
- **Problem:** The positive affect came from recalling a positive memory, not from receiving news. The pitch angle then opens on a promotion, compounding the mismatch.
- **Suggested fix:** "Talking yourself down after a happy memory makes you read social situations more rigidly"

N (268 Dutch adults), preregistration, randomization, the inflexibility result relative to both other conditions and the null on positive interpretation bias are all accurate.

### Study 9: Event boundaries matter less to your memory than what happens next
**PMID:** 42760449 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Headline sets up a contrast with event boundaries that the study did not make — and that its secondary result contradicts** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Event boundaries matter less to your memory than what happens next"
- **Abstract says:** "memory was much more strongly influenced by the number of causal consequences than the number of causal antecedents or serial position. ... Of secondary interest, for one of these reanalyses, we also found that memory was better at event boundaries, particularly as the number of event dimension shifts increased."
- **Problem:** The tested comparison was consequences versus antecedents versus *serial position* — not versus event boundaries. The abstract's one statement about boundaries is that memory was *better* at them. As written, the headline implies a boundary-versus-consequence horse race that was never run, and points the reader in the opposite direction from the boundary result.
- **Suggested fix:** "What an event goes on to cause predicts memory better than what caused it"

The body text ("antecedents... consequences... than its causes or its position in the story"), the reactivation interpretation, the preregistered-reanalysis status and the Notre Dame contact are all accurate.

### Study 10: Interrupting a film scrambles your memory of it but not how you carve it into events
**PMID:** 42758465 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. A non-significant result reported as "unchanged"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Segmentation was remarkably robust — overall rates, patterns and hierarchical structure were unchanged"
- **Abstract says:** "Interrupting or jumbling videos did not significantly change overall segmentation rates, patterns, or the tendency to hierarchically organize events."
- **Problem:** "Unchanged" states equivalence; the abstract reports only a failure to detect a significant change, and (per Caveats) the sample is likely modest and the study was not preregistered, so absence of evidence is doing the work here.
- **Suggested fix:** "Segmentation was remarkably robust — overall rates, patterns and hierarchical structure showed no significant change"

The three viewing conditions, the brief post-jump spike settling after 5 s, and the reduced quantity and organization of recall in the jumbled condition are accurately reported, as is the "not preregistered" caveat.

### Study 11: Even fluent bilinguals falter when instructions come in their second language
**PMID:** 42717310 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Phonological short-term memory null is narrower than "performance"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "phonological short-term memory scores, which were uniformly high, did not predict performance"
- **Abstract says:** "Phonological short-term memory scores (L2 pseudoword repetition accuracy) were overall high and did not significantly predict accuracy of instruction enactment."
- **Problem:** The null applies specifically to enactment accuracy, not to task performance in general; the abstract separately reports that L1 operation span showed a non-significant trend toward predicting enactment memory.
- **Suggested fix:** "phonological short-term memory scores (L2 pseudoword repetition), which were uniformly high, did not significantly predict enactment accuracy"

N = 36, Persian–English bilinguals, five-instruction sequences in both languages, verbal versus enacted recall, and the L2 cost confined to enactment are all accurate.

### Study 12: Raised gamma brainwaves in autistic children track memory for faces over four years
**PMID:** 42721928 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Pitch implies individual-level tracking that the caveat rules out** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The hook is the four-year window — a single resting EEG measure that keeps tracking how well a child remembers faces."
- **Abstract says:** "This elevation was longitudinally associated with lower non-verbal IQ and memory for faces. ... The study demonstrates that resting-state gamma power is related to cognitive ability across ages and timepoints of our study in autistic youth."
- **Problem:** "A single resting EEG measure that keeps tracking how well a child remembers faces" reads as individual prediction; the finding is a group-level longitudinal association. This sits awkwardly against the digest's own caveat that "no diagnostic or prognostic use is supported," and a writer could build the pitch on the stronger reading.
- **Suggested fix:** "The hook is the four-year window — a resting EEG measure that stays associated, at group level, with how well autistic children remember faces."

Study facts (1597 EEGs, 399 children, four timepoints at baseline / +6 weeks / +6 months / +4 years, elevated gamma in autistic children, sex difference, decline with age) are accurate, and the pharmaceutical consulting disclosures are already in Caveats.

### Study 13: Nighttime seizure activity may be stealing the memory benefit of sleep
**PMID:** 42748517 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline calls interictal discharges "seizure activity"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Nighttime seizure activity may be stealing the memory benefit of sleep"
- **Abstract says:** "Another finding shared by six studies was the negative effect of nocturnal interictal epileptic discharges (IEDs) on cognition."
- **Problem:** Interictal discharges are by definition the activity occurring *between* seizures; calling them "seizure activity" in the headline misdescribes the phenomenon, and the pitch itself hinges on patients whose seizures are controlled. The body text gets this right.
- **Suggested fix:** "Nighttime epileptic discharges may be stealing the memory benefit of sleep"

Review scope (2000–2025, 13 studies, 581 adults), the nine / four / six study counts, and the authors' hedged mechanism argument are accurately reported, and the digest is explicit that this is a hypothesis.

### Study 14: A depressive bias doesn't stop your brain getting the usual memory boost from a sudden target
**PMID:** 42706587 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Cue effects on valence are compressed and given an unsupported "in both groups"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Adding a warning cue before the target improved detection and shifted the memory advantage toward positive images in both groups."
- **Abstract says:** "(2) During encoding, a positivity advantage emerged under the No-Cue condition, whereas alerting cues significantly improved overall detection efficiency and attenuated valence-related differences; (3) During recognition, alerting cues modulated the ABE magnitude for emotional stimuli, with a particularly pronounced enhancement for positive information."
- **Problem:** Two separate results are merged. At encoding the positivity advantage appeared *without* the cue and cues *attenuated* valence differences; the positive enhancement appears at recognition. The abstract does not report the cue-by-valence effects as equivalent across groups, so "in both groups" is unverifiable.
- **Suggested fix:** "Alerting cues improved overall detection and flattened valence differences at encoding, while at recognition they enhanced the attentional boost for emotional images, most strongly for positive ones."

N = 81 (42 / 39), BDI-II ≥ 14 with structured interview confirming sub-clinical status, and the Bayesian moderate evidence for no group difference are accurate. The 4/10 score is well judged.

### Study 15: Decades after epilepsy surgery, most people say life is good — even those still having seizures
**PMID:** 42727324 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Strongest predictor" is a step beyond "remains central"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Seizure freedom remained the strongest predictor, but 55–72% of those still having seizures also reported good quality of life"
- **Abstract says:** "Seizure freedom, absence of anxiety, and ability to work predicted better QoL-T, while seizure freedom and figural memory predicted QoL-PS. ... While seizure freedom remains central, factors linked to cognitive compensation and reduced stress, including disability pension, appear increasingly relevant."
- **Problem:** The abstract names seizure freedom as a predictor of both measures and describes it as "central," but does not rank predictors by strength. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Seizure freedom predicted both quality-of-life measures, but 55–72% of those still having seizures also reported good quality of life"

N = 86, 10–44 years post-surgery, QOLIE-89 plus Beck Anxiety Inventory, subscale scores of 60–88/100, and the anxiety / work / figural memory predictors are accurately reported, as is the survivorship caveat.
