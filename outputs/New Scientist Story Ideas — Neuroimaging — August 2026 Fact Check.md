### Study 1: Only one type of "psychosis risk" leaves a brain signature that resembles schizophrenia
**PMID:** 41823413 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Sample sizes (1,425 patients / 907 controls), balanced accuracy (69%), similarity to schizophrenia (r = 0.60), fronto-parieto-perisylvian reductions, UHR enlargements, UHR's absent biopsychosocial predictability and better longitudinal outcomes all match the abstract exactly.

**Issue A. Study compared four signatures, not two** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "They compared brain signatures of the two main high-risk definitions: 'ultra high-risk' (UHR) attenuated psychotic symptoms, and cognitive basic symptoms (COGDIS)"
- **Abstract says:** "to derive and compare diagnostic brain signatures: cognitive disturbances (COGDIS), UHR, their overlap (MIXED), and schizophrenia." MIXED showed "a mixed pattern of reductions and enlargements" and *was* predictable from biopsychosocial data (21% in patients vs 3% in controls), with immune/blood–brain-barrier polygenic enrichment.
- **Problem:** The digest drops the MIXED group entirely. That matters for the pitch, because MIXED — not UHR — is the group the authors describe as a dimensional, transdiagnostic signature; a writer working from the digest would misdescribe the paper's taxonomy.
- **Suggested fix:** "They compared brain signatures for three risk definitions — 'ultra high-risk' (UHR) attenuated psychotic symptoms, cognitive basic symptoms (COGDIS), and their overlap (MIXED) — against the schizophrenia signature. Only COGDIS separated patients from controls (69% balanced accuracy) and aligned with schizophrenia (r = 0.60); MIXED produced a transdiagnostic pattern, and UHR a weak, diagnostically unspecific one."

**NS fit 8/10:** Defensible — 2,332 participants, multi-site consortium, pre-specified comparison, and the digest's caveats correctly limit the accuracy claim.

### Study 2: Childhood trauma raises bipolar risk almost entirely through routes the brain scan cannot see
**PMID:** 42234441 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one Moderate)

N (1,031 BD / 2,221 controls), 19 cohorts, mean ages, 75 morphology measures, "<1% mediated", and the three significant mediators (hippocampal volume, medial orbitofrontal thickness, superior frontal thickness) are all accurate.

**Issue A. Causal verb in headline for a cross-sectional case-control study** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Childhood trauma **raises** bipolar risk almost entirely through routes the brain scan cannot see"
- **Abstract says:** "This case-control study conducted a **cross-sectional analysis**… Severity of childhood trauma was **directly associated with** higher likelihood of having a bipolar disorder diagnosis."
- **Problem:** "Raises" asserts causation. The digest's own Caveats say "cannot establish causation," so the headline contradicts the caveat — exactly the mismatch that survives into a pitch and then into a standfirst.
- **Suggested fix:** "Childhood trauma is linked to bipolar disorder almost entirely through routes the brain scan cannot see"

**Issue B. "The mechanism must lie elsewhere" overreaches the null** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the mechanism must lie elsewhere (function, connectivity, immune/metabolic pathways, or psychosocial routes entirely)."
- **Abstract says:** The authors conclude the opposite in tone — "severity of childhood trauma exposure was associated with bipolar disorder diagnosis **in part through** a smaller hippocampus, thinner cortex…" and that "identification of this mechanistic pathway improves understanding of the disorder."
- **Problem:** A near-null mediation through *structural* measures does not establish that the mechanism lies elsewhere; it only shows morphology is not the route. Also worth signalling to the writer that the authors themselves frame the result positively, so the deflationary angle will need to be put to them.
- **Suggested fix:** "…which means grey-matter structure is not the route — leaving function, connectivity, immune/metabolic or psychosocial pathways as untested possibilities. Note that the authors themselves frame the significant mediators as a meaningful mechanistic pathway."

Industry disclosures are present and the digest flags them in Caveats. ✓
**NS fit 8/10:** Defensible.

### Study 3: People carrying dementia genes hold their brain networks together for decades while the tissue shrinks
**PMID:** 41277710 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

289 presymptomatic carriers / 271 non-carrier family members, preserved functional integration across age in carriers vs decline in non-carriers, strongest in cognitive control networks, behaviourally relevant, independent of atrophy severity, glial (astrocyte/microglia/oligodendrocyte) transcriptomic overlap — all match. The Rowe COI list in the digest matches the abstract's declaration.

**Issue A. "For decades"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "hold their brain networks together **for decades**"
- **Abstract says:** "brain structural changes are evident **a decade or more** before the expected onset"; resilience "protecting against clinical conversion **for many years**."
- **Problem:** Plural "decades" inflates the abstract's "a decade or more"/"many years."
- **Suggested fix:** "hold their brain networks together for a decade or more while the tissue shrinks"

**Issue B. Specific variants named** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a GENFI participant at 40 who knows they carry a *C9orf72* or *MAPT* variant"
- **Abstract says:** "carriers of pathogenic variants associated with FTD" — no genes specified.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only. GENFI does recruit C9orf72/GRN/MAPT carriers, but the writer should confirm rather than assume from the digest.
- **Suggested fix:** Add "(confirm variant breakdown with authors — not specified in abstract)".

**NS fit 7/10:** Defensible — n = 560, multi-site, and the digest correctly labels the transcriptomic layer as speculative.

### Study 4: A brain-training therapy for early schizophrenia fails to protect grey matter the second time round
**PMID:** 42623965 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Arm sizes (CET n = 56, EST n = 43), assessment schedule (baseline/9/18 months), the single right rostral anterior cingulate group × time effect, its lack of relationship to cognitive/social change, and the null secondary ROIs all match.

**Issue A. "Randomised" vs "completed"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Ninety-nine people with early-course schizophrenia **were randomised** to 18 months of Cognitive Enhancement Therapy"
- **Abstract says:** "Ninety-nine participants with early course schizophrenia **completed** MRI, cognitive, and social adjustment assessments at baseline, 9… and 18… months."
- **Problem:** 99 is the completer figure, not the randomised figure (which the abstract does not give). The digest's own Caveats correctly say "99 completers," so the body contradicts the caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "Ninety-nine people with early-course schizophrenia completed 18 months of assessments after randomisation to Cognitive Enhancement Therapy (n=56) or Enriched Supportive Therapy (n=43)…"

**NS fit 7/10:** Defensible for a pre-specified confirmatory null; digest flags power limits.

### Study 5: People who often become lucid in dreams have distinctive grey and white matter architecture
**PMID:** 41677447 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

mCCA + jICA method, one joint GM–WM component (frontal, temporal, parietal, cerebellar; metacognition/imagery/volitional control), one GM-specific component including cuneus, and two WM-only components for ordinary dream recall with no overlap — all accurate. Dates (epub 12 Feb 2026, Aug issue) correct.

**Issue A. Author affiliation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "De Pisapia and colleagues **at Trento**"
- **Abstract says:** Four authors at University of Trento; Grecucci A is at the University of Bari.
- **Suggested fix:** "De Pisapia and colleagues at Trento and Bari…"

**Issue B. NS fit score too high for the evidence** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "NS fit: 7/10"
- **Problem:** The paper is self-described as exploratory, uses a fully self-reported trait measure, reports no independent replication, and the digest itself notes the sample size is not stated and is "likely modest." Under the rule on 7+ scores resting on small or self-reported data, 7 is not defensible on evidence quality alone — it is being carried by subject appeal.
- **Suggested fix:** Score **6/10**, with the note "irresistible subject, but exploratory data-driven design, self-reported trait measure and unstated N cap the confidence."

### Study 6: An SSRI taken only in the second half of the cycle calms brain responses to provocation in PMDD
**PMID:** 42515970 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Design (randomised, placebo-controlled, intermittent escitalopram 20 mg/day luteal phase), DRSP prospective ratings, Point Subtraction Aggression Paradigm, irritability/anger d = −0.93, aggression d = −0.42, mediation, and "nominally" lower anterior insula reactivity with a positive relationship to irritability/anger — all match.

**Issue A. "Largest effect on irritability" / "bigger than the overall mood effect"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Escitalopram substantially reduced PMDD symptoms, with the **largest effect** on irritability/anger (d = −0.93)" and, in the pitch, "Lead with the irritability effect size — **bigger than the overall mood effect**."
- **Abstract says:** "significantly reduced PMDD symptoms compared with placebo, **in particular** irritability or anger (Cohen's d = −0.93…)" — no effect size is given for the overall symptom score.
- **Problem:** The comparative claim can't be checked against the abstract; "in particular" implies prominence, not a formally largest effect.
- **Suggested fix:** "…with the irritability/anger effect singled out by the authors (d = −0.93). Check the overall symptom effect size in the full text before pitching it as the *largest*."

**Issue B. The aggression effect is borderline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "and reduced self-rated aggressiveness (d = −0.42)"
- **Abstract says:** "diminished following treatment (Cohen's d = −0.42, 95% CI [1.4 × 10⁻⁴, 0.84])" — the interval effectively abuts zero.
- **Problem:** Presented flatly as a reduction, the reader will assume a solid secondary effect. The CI as printed is barely distinguishable from null.
- **Suggested fix:** "…and reduced self-rated aggressiveness, though only marginally (d = −0.42, CI running essentially to zero)."

**Issue C. NS fit** — Severity: Minor
7/10 rests on an RCT (a point in its favour) but with an unstated N, a self-reported primary outcome and a neuroimaging result the authors label "nominal" — i.e. the brain hook, which is what earns it a place in a neuroimaging digest, is the weakest part. **Suggested: 6/10**, unless the full text confirms a reasonable trial size.

### Study 7: Brain surgery for depression works — but only when the brain's valuation machinery comes back online
**PMID:** 42533701 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one Moderate)

N = 62 across three groups, task, hierarchical Bayesian RL-DDM modelling, blunted reward prediction errors, increased loss responses, slowed dynamics, responders' efficient evidence accumulation and near-normal value representation, dACC prediction-error and lateral OFC value signals — all match. The digest's caveat that patients were scanned post-surgery only is correct and important.

**Issue A. Headline and rationale assert causation and temporal order the design cannot support** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Brain surgery for depression works — but **only when** the brain's valuation machinery **comes back online**" and "whether lesion surgery helps **depends on** whether valuation circuitry renormalises"
- **Abstract says:** "clinical improvement following neurosurgical treatment **is associated with** relative normalisation of valuation-related neurophysiology" — and the study is a post-surgical cross-sectional comparison of responders vs non-responders.
- **Problem:** "Comes back online" and "depends on" imply a before/after change and a causal dependency. Nobody was scanned before surgery, so the responders' profile may have preceded the operation — which is what the digest's own caveat says. Also, "works" as an unqualified verb overstates "can produce sustained clinical response in carefully selected patients."
- **Suggested fix:** Headline — "Brain surgery for depression: the patients it helps are the ones whose valuation circuitry looks near-normal afterwards." Body — "…suggests that clinical response after lesion surgery goes together with more normal valuation signalling, though the study cannot say whether that difference followed the operation or preceded it."

**NS fit 6/10:** Defensible.

### Study 8: Night-shift nurses show altered amygdala activity that tracks both blood metabolites and low mood
**PMID:** 42624217 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

121 female nurses (62 night, 59 day), ReHo decreases in left superior temporal cortex and left anterior hippocampus, increases in right medial amygdala and right superior parietal lobule, D1 receptor spatial correspondence, gene-expression enrichment for stress response and mitochondrion organisation, and the metabolite–amygdala–BDI indirect path all match.

**Issue A. The indirect association was observed within the night-shift group only** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "right medial amygdala activity linked serum metabolic variation to Beck Depression Inventory scores"
- **Abstract says:** "**In NSN**, metabolite PC1 was positively associated with right medial amygdala ReHo, and an **exploratory** indirect association between metabolite PC1 and BDI scores through right medial amygdala ReHo was observed."
- **Problem:** Stated unrestricted, it reads as a whole-sample chain. It is a within-night-shift, exploratory path. (The digest does flag "exploratory" in Caveats, so this is only about the missing group restriction.)
- **Suggested fix:** "…and within the night-shift group, an exploratory analysis linked serum metabolic variation to Beck Depression Inventory scores via right medial amygdala ReHo."

Note also that "amygdala activity" in the headline is regional homogeneity — local synchronisation, not activation level. The body explains this, so it is acceptable shorthand, but the writer should not let it drift into "overactive amygdala."

**NS fit 6/10:** Defensible.

### Study 9: A neural network can identify you from two minutes of brain activity — and then predict things about you
**PMID:** 42527301 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one Moderate)

All four accuracy figures (93% MPI-Leipzig, 94% NKI-Rockland, 73% OASIS-3, 99% HCP), the sub-two-minute truncation result, cross-dataset generalisation and partial transfer to cognitive/trait prediction match the abstract. The bioRxiv preprint note is correct.

**Issue A. Pitch angle merges two separate results into one claim** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "brain scans shared in open repositories… are now **identifiable to 99% accuracy from under two minutes of data, by a model that has never seen the dataset**"
- **Abstract says:** The 93/94/73/99% figures are for "individual recognition from **held out scan sessions**" across the training datasets. Generalisation to "completely new datasets and participants… not used in training" is reported separately and without a headline accuracy figure. The sub-two-minute result is reported without dataset-specific accuracies.
- **Problem:** The pitch stacks the best-case number (99%, HCP, held-out sessions), the truncation result, and the never-seen-dataset result into a single sentence. That is the more exciting result a reader would assume, not what the paper reports. It would not survive a check by the authors.
- **Suggested fix:** "Lead with the privacy inversion: a model recognises individuals from held-out sessions with up to 99% accuracy (HCP; 73–94% elsewhere), holds up on under two minutes of data, and — separately — generalises well enough to recognise people in datasets and scanners it was never trained on."

**NS fit 6/10:** Defensible.

### Study 10: Coal miners' resting brain networks look nothing like the undergraduates neuroscience is built on
**PMID:** 42518290 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

52 male miners (36–45), 55 male students (≤25), fNIRS, effect sizes (global efficiency d = −1.34, node strength d = −0.93, clustering d = −0.81), the 9th-percentile figure, the FDR-uncorrected emotion-evoked null with power adequate only for large effects, and the behavioural valence/arousal differences all match. The age-confound caveat is well made.

**Issue A. Headline overstates the scope of difference** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Coal miners' resting brain networks **look nothing like** the undergraduates neuroscience is built on"
- **Abstract says:** Differences were found in graph-theoretic resting-state network efficiency; emotion-evoked activation differences did **not** survive correction, and the authors' conclusion is that differences "preferentially manifest in baseline network organisation rather than emotion-evoked responses."
- **Problem:** "Look nothing like" implies global dissimilarity when the finding is specific to resting-state prefrontal network efficiency measured by fNIRS — and the task-state comparison was, if anything, comparable (as the paper's own title says).
- **Suggested fix:** "Coal miners' resting prefrontal networks look very different from the undergraduates neuroscience is built on — but their emotional responses don't"

**NS fit 6/10:** Defensible.

### Study 11: The hunt for brain-based subtypes of childhood attention problems mostly comes up empty
**PMID:** 41989408 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

ABCD cohort, k-means and latent profile analysis, randomisation approach for cluster number, within-sample replication testing, Oregon ADHD-1000 with >50% ADHD, and the finding that computational reaction-time parameters yielded reproducible groups with some clinical validity — all match.

**Issue A. "Only" DTI FA** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "among brain measures, **only** diffusion-imaging fractional anisotropy yielded reproducible clusters"
- **Abstract says:** "Diffusion tensor imaging-measured fractional anisotropy features yielded the **strongest evidence** of reproducible groups **among structural neuroimaging features**."
- **Problem:** "Strongest among" is a ranking; "only" is an exclusion. The abstract does not say other imaging features produced nothing.
- **Suggested fix:** "…among brain measures, diffusion-imaging fractional anisotropy gave the strongest evidence of reproducible clusters, though generalisation and clinical validation were weaker."

**NS fit 6/10:** Defensible.

### Study 12: Adults with ADHD fall into three emotion-regulation types — and they respond differently to therapy
**PMID:** 41962667 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one Moderate)

N = 497, eight ER dimensions from the ERQ and DERS, three profiles at 29.4/49.7/20.9% (rounded correctly), LP3 worst symptoms and executive function, and the non-linear postcentral gyrus–amygdala connectivity result (LP2 weaker than both LP1 and LP3) all match. The digest's caveat about the circular 97.8% SVM figure is exactly right and should be kept.

**Issue A. "Gained the most from CBT" is a comparative claim the abstract does not make** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The severe group… **gained the most from** cognitive behavioural therapy" and, in the pitch, "a fifth of the sample were severely dysregulated, and **they were the ones CBT helped most**."
- **Abstract says:** "In addition to significantly improving executive functions (particularly inhibition, shifting, and initiation) **in LP3**, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) also led to marked improvements in emotion regulation strategy use." Treatment responses across profiles were "analyzed," but no between-profile comparison of gains is reported.
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that CBT improved things *within* the severe group; it does not state that the severe group improved *more than* the other groups. The pitch hook rests entirely on the comparative version.
- **Suggested fix:** "The severe group had the worst symptoms and executive function, and showed significant CBT-related gains in inhibition, shifting, initiation and regulation strategy use. Confirm with the authors whether these gains exceeded those in the other two profiles — the abstract does not report a between-profile comparison, and the treatment analysis was not randomised across profiles."

**Issue B. Date presentation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Published: 15 August 2026"
- **Abstract says:** J Affect Disord 2026 Aug 15;407:121748, **Epub 2026 Apr 9**.
- **Problem:** The 15 August date is the print issue; the paper has been public since April. Worth noting for the "last 30 days" coverage claim and for any competitor check.
- **Suggested fix:** "Published: 15 August 2026 (issue); online 9 April 2026."

**NS fit 5/10:** Defensible and appropriately cautious.

### Study 13: Elite footballers read the game with the thinking brain, not the visual brain
**PMID:** 42518012 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

20 elite / 20 novice, fMRI with realistic video decision paradigm, higher expert accuracy and faster RTs, greater frontal/parietal engagement, reduced occipital reliance, and the precuneus/angular gyrus/middle cingulate correlations within experts — all match. The caveats about selection vs training and eye movements are appropriate.

**Issue A. Headline dichotomy erases a stated finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Elite footballers read the game with the thinking brain, **not the visual brain**" — and in the pitch, "the best players are **not looking harder, they are looking less**."
- **Abstract says:** "**Both groups showed widespread cortical and subcortical activation**, yet experts exhibited greater engagement of frontal and parietal cortices and **reduced reliance on** occipital visual areas."
- **Problem:** Experts still recruited visual cortex; the difference is relative. "Not the visual brain" and "looking less" invite a reader to conclude experts barely use vision, which the abstract contradicts.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline — "Elite footballers lean on the thinking brain more, and the visual brain less." Pitch — "the best players are not extracting more from the image; they are doing more with what they extract."

**NS fit 5/10:** Defensible for a 40-person expertise comparison.

### Study 14: Sad music reliably lights up the brain's memory hub, not just its emotion circuits
**PMID:** 42617440 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Nine studies, 155 participants, 25 foci, GingerALE random-effects with cluster-level FWE, the right medial temporal lobe cluster spanning parahippocampal gyrus and hippocampus, and the BA28/entorhinal peak all match. The digest's caveat about the ~17-experiment convention and the author's own MTL caution is well judged.

**Issue A. "Reliably" and "largely" overstate an exploratory nine-study ALE** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** Headline "Sad music **reliably** lights up the brain's memory hub"; Why it matters — "sad music moves us **largely** by pulling on autobiographical memory."
- **Abstract says:** "In this **exploratory** study…"; "**Although** these findings are consistent with a memory-mediated account… the involvement of these regions should be interpreted **cautiously** because the medial temporal lobe also supports other cognitive functions beyond episodic memory."
- **Problem:** "Reliably" implies robustness the author explicitly declines to claim, and "largely" quantifies a contribution the meta-analysis never estimated. The Caveats correctly hedge, so the headline and rationale contradict them.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline — "Sad music converges on the brain's memory hub, not its emotion circuits." Why it matters — "It is consistent with the idea that sad music moves us partly by engaging autobiographical memory…"

**NS fit 5/10:** Defensible.

### Study 15: A computational model splits sentence-building into two stages with separate brain addresses
**PMID:** 42527808 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The MPT model, its status as "the first computational instantiation of a two-stage model of syntactic production," the aphasia discourse-sample fitting, and the three localisations (hierarchical encoding → posterior STS and parietal cortex; linearisation → posterior inferior frontal; omissions → dorsal frontal regions and underlying white matter) are all accurate. Non-preregistration is confirmed by the open practices statement.

**Issue A. Recruitment site is inferred** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "single-centre recruitment (University of South Carolina)"
- **Abstract says:** Only that "all procedures were approved by the internal review board at the University of South Carolina." Sample size is not given.
- **Problem:** IRB of record is not the same as single-site recruitment. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "IRB of record was the University of South Carolina; confirm recruitment sites and sample size with the authors — neither is in the abstract."

**NS fit 5/10:** Defensible.

### Study 16: Counting things you touch and counting things you see compete for the same mental slot
**PMID:** 42551095 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. The two-experiment design (tactile WM load + visual enumeration; visual WM load + tactile enumeration), the increased error rates and reduced subitizing capacity under high load, the specificity to the subitizing range and the explicit ruling-out of general dual-task cost all match the abstract. The digest correctly notes that the frontoparietal interpretation is inferred from prior imaging rather than measured here, and correctly identifies non-industry funding (STI2030 / NSFC). Framing is consistent with the abstract; the NS fit of 4/10 is appropriately modest for a behavioural paper in a neuroimaging-focused digest.

### Study 17: One night without sleep reorganises how brain structure and function line up
**PMID:** 41851418 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. N = 50 healthy adult males, 1-back spatial working memory task, multimodal MRI before and after acute total sleep deprivation, the three hierarchical levels of SC-FC coupling, the **trend-level** negative correlation between left limbic network coupling and omission rate, and the **moderate** discriminative performance of the subcortical lateralization index are all reported accurately. Crucially, the digest does not upgrade "trend-level" to significant or "moderate" to "usable biomarker" — the Why it matters section ("Not much yet") and the pitch angle both state the limitation explicitly. Dates (epub 18 Mar 2026, August issue) are correct.

### Study 18: Brain chemistry at admission predicts who will halve their psychosis symptoms in 12 weeks
**PMID:** 42061209 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 40, mean age 22.68, 1.5 T MRS and DTI at baseline, 12-week follow-up, ≥50% PANSS reduction endpoint, and the correlates of improvement (lower childhood trauma, fewer prior episodes, lower baseline depression, lower inflammatory markers) all match.

**Issue A. Direction of the NAA/Cr predictor is dropped** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "glucose and NAA/Cr for negative symptoms"
- **Abstract says:** "higher glucose (OR = 4.90) and **lower** NAA/Cr (OR = 0.07) in the left FL for the Negative scale."
- **Problem:** Listing the two together implies both point the same way; they don't. A writer using this line could reverse the finding.
- **Suggested fix:** "…higher glucose but *lower* NAA/Cr in the left frontal lobe for negative symptoms, and left anterior cingulate fractional anisotropy (alongside frontal glucose and NAA) for total symptoms."

**Issue B. "Predicts" in the headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Brain chemistry at admission **predicts** who will halve their psychosis symptoms"
- **Abstract says:** "Baseline neurochemical and structural parameters in the left hemisphere serve as **isolated predictors**" — from a 40-patient multinomial regression with multiple outcome scales and no validation sample.
- **Problem:** The body and Caveats correctly call this hypothesis-generating; the headline promises a working prediction. Contradicts the caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "Brain chemistry at admission is linked to who will halve their psychosis symptoms in 12 weeks — in 40 patients"

**NS fit 4/10:** Defensible and appropriately low given the overfitting risk the digest flags.

**Editor's note on the pattern:** Nothing here is factually wrong in a way that would mislead a clinician, and the Caveats sections are unusually good — several correctly identify circular validation, trend-level results and exploratory mediation before I got to them. The recurring weakness is that headlines and pitch angles are written to a stronger claim than the body and caveats support (Studies 2, 7, 9, 12, 14, 18). Tightening the headline verbs would clear most of this digest in one pass.
