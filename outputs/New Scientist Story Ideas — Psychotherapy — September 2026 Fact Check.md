### Study 1: Exposure therapy for spider phobia works less well when progesterone is high
**PMID:** 42700422 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline states an association as a settled effect** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Exposure therapy for spider phobia works less well when progesterone is high"
- **Abstract says:** "higher progesterone levels on the day of exposure therapy were associated with less improvement in behavioral avoidance" and "provide preliminary evidence that ovarian hormones ... *may* affect exposure-based outcomes"
- **Problem:** The headline converts a secondary, correlational, explicitly preliminary finding on one outcome (behavioral avoidance) into a general statement about how well exposure therapy works. The body text and Caveats are appropriately hedged; the headline is not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Higher progesterone on the day of exposure therapy is linked to less improvement in spider-phobia avoidance"

**Issue B. "Everyone improved" overstates a group-level result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Everyone improved."
- **Abstract says:** "Both phobic fear and avoidance behavior improved significantly over time."
- **Problem:** Significant mean improvement over time is not the same as every participant improving; the abstract reports no individual-level response rates.
- **Suggested fix:** "Fear and avoidance improved significantly across the group over time."

**Issue C. NS fit score of 7 rests on a small, uncontrolled sample with a null primary comparison** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — genuinely new question ('when does therapy work?') with a clean biological hook, held back by a small sample and a null result on the headline comparison"
- **Abstract says:** "N=45 women"; "improvement did not differ between menstrual cycle groups"; "preliminary evidence"; "More research in larger samples is needed"
- **Problem:** The rationale text already concedes the two problems that should cap the score: N=45, no control group, no randomization to cycle phase, and the only positive result is a secondary hormone–outcome correlation. A 7 implies near-certain commissioning strength.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10, with the same rationale text.

*Note:* Author affiliations ("Susanne Fischer and Ulrike Ehlert at the University of Zurich") are not listed in the abstract record supplied — **unverifiable from abstract only.**

### Study 2: People want their psilocybin natural, even when the synthetic version is chemically identical
**PMID:** 42678038 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Significance claim for Study 2 not stated in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "in the second study, people were significantly more willing to take psilocybin 'isolated from mushrooms' than an identical synthetic version"
- **Abstract says:** "In Study 2, we found that participants had stronger intentions to use a natural version of psilocybin isolated from mushrooms as compared to an identical synthetic version..."
- **Problem:** The abstract flags significance explicitly for Study 1 ("significantly lower intentions") but not for the Study 2 comparison. The digest adds "significantly" where the abstract does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "people reported stronger intentions to take psilocybin 'isolated from mushrooms' than an identical synthetic version"

**Issue B. Caveats contain details not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "online convenience samples of unclear representativeness"; "population may not generalize beyond the US"
- **Abstract says:** "two studies involving independent samples (total N = 1,102)" — no recruitment mode, sampling frame or country stated.
- **Problem:** The mode of administration and national composition of the samples are **unverifiable from abstract only**. The inference is plausible (all authors are US-based) but should not be stated as fact.
- **Suggested fix:** "Sampling frame and recruitment method not specified in the abstract; likely US-based samples, so generalisability is unclear"

**Issue C. NS fit of 7 rests on hypothetical, self-reported intentions** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 ... limited by the fact that intentions are not behavior"
- **Abstract says:** "we examined the intentions to use psilocybin in a mental health context"
- **Problem:** All outcomes are hypothetical willingness ratings plus self-reported depression, mindfulness and narcissism scales. The large N supports precision, not validity. A 7 overstates the strength of the evidence base for the claim being made about real-world uptake.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10.

### Study 3: Psychotherapy use in the US jumped 41 per cent in four years — and worsening mental health explains only a third of it
**PMID:** 42723007 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline attaches the decomposition figure to the wrong outcome** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Psychotherapy use in the US jumped 41 per cent in four years — and worsening mental health explains only a third of it"
- **Abstract says:** "Decomposition analysis suggested that 36.7% of the increase in **mental health care use** was associated with changes in depression and anxiety"
- **Problem:** The 36.7% figure decomposes the rise in *any* mental health care use (19.2% → 23.8%), not the psychotherapy-specific rise. The digest body gets this right ("36.7 per cent of the overall rise"), but the headline welds the 41% psychotherapy figure to the 36.7% overall-care decomposition, implying a decomposition of psychotherapy use that the abstract does not report.
- **Suggested fix:** "US psychotherapy use jumped 41 per cent in four years — and symptoms explain only a third of the wider rise in mental health care"

**Issue B. Decomposition language edges toward causal attribution** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Decomposition analysis attributed 36.7 per cent of the overall rise to changes in anxiety and depression symptoms and 11.3 per cent to insurance coverage"
- **Abstract says:** "36.7% of the increase in mental health care use was **associated with** changes in depression and anxiety, whereas 11.3% was associated with changes in health insurance coverage"
- **Problem:** "Attributed to" reads as causal apportionment; the abstract keeps the language associational throughout. The Caveats correctly note causation cannot be established, so the body text should match.
- **Suggested fix:** "Decomposition analysis associated 36.7 per cent of the overall rise with changes in anxiety and depression symptoms and 11.3 per cent with insurance coverage"

### Study 4: Children's therapy works better when the therapist unconsciously mirrors them
**PMID:** 42721331 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Active ingredient ... rather than anything said out loud" is not supported** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "suggests the active ingredient in play therapy may be bodily synchrony rather than anything said out loud"
- **Abstract says:** "therapists' nonverbal coordination supports therapeutic change in Mentalization-Based Treatment for Children by enhancing children's affect regulatory capacities within play"
- **Problem:** The study coded nonverbal coordination and in-session affect regulation; it did not measure or model verbal interventions, so it cannot rank bodily synchrony against speech. The digest sets up a comparison the study never ran.
- **Suggested fix:** "suggests that therapists' bodily coordination with a child is an active contributor to change in play therapy, alongside what is said"

**Issue B. "Unconsciously" is an addition** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Children's therapy works better when the therapist unconsciously mirrors them"
- **Abstract says:** "therapists' nonverbal coordination"
- **Problem:** Nothing in the abstract addresses whether the coordination was conscious, deliberate or automatic. The word is doing rhetorical work the data don't support.
- **Suggested fix:** "Children's therapy works better when the therapist's movements sync with theirs"

**Issue C. "Entirely" overstates the mediation result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The effect of nonverbal coordination on outcomes ran entirely through in-session regulation; the direct path was not significant."
- **Abstract says:** "Indirect effects ... were significant, whereas direct effects were not, indicating that nonverbal coordination was associated with outcomes **primarily** through in-session affect regulation"
- **Problem:** A non-significant direct path is not a zero direct path; the authors say "primarily", not "entirely".
- **Suggested fix:** "The effect of nonverbal coordination on outcomes ran primarily through in-session regulation; the direct path was not significant."

**Issue D. Behavioural specifics not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "more nonverbal coordination — matching posture, rhythm and movement during play"
- **Abstract says:** "coded for therapists' nonverbal coordination"
- **Problem:** The specific coded channels (posture, rhythm, movement) are **unverifiable from abstract only**.
- **Suggested fix:** "more nonverbal coordination with the child during play (as rated by trained coders)"

### Study 5: Social life doesn't improve during therapy — it improves for years afterward
**PMID:** 42695247 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Pitch attributes post-treatment growth to therapy, contradicting the digest's own caveat** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "**Pitch angle:** Therapy's social payoff arrives late — sometimes years after the last session."
- **Abstract says:** "There was significant growth in QQSR across long-term follow-up ... There was no change from pre- to posttreatment"; design is naturalistic with no control group
- **Problem:** The study observed growth *after* treatment in an uncontrolled sample; it cannot show that therapy produced it (maturation, regression to the mean and life events are unmodelled alternatives). The Caveats say "cannot establish causation", but the pitch line asserts a therapy "payoff". Body and caveat contradict each other.
- **Suggested fix:** "Social connection keeps improving in the years after therapy ends — even though it didn't shift during treatment"

**Issue B. Self-affiliation glossed as self-compassion** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It also offers evidence for the long-suspected loop between self-compassion and friendship running in both directions."
- **Abstract says:** "the Structural Analysis of Social Behavior Introject **self-affiliation**"; "A friendly relationship to oneself"
- **Problem:** SASB introject self-affiliation is a specific construct, not the self-compassion scale readers will assume. Also worth noting the authors' own caution, omitted from the digest: "The QQSR might measure relatively stable characteristics."
- **Suggested fix:** "It also offers evidence for a two-way loop between social connection and a warm relationship to oneself (measured as SASB self-affiliation)."

### Study 6: AI writes usable therapy notes — but misses what clinicians notice
**PMID:** 42715612 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Rater identity claim not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "evaluation by expert raters, not by the clinicians or clients involved"
- **Abstract says:** "Blinded-to-source judges evaluated the summaries on factual consistency, salient meaning preservation, and clinical value."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not state the judges' expertise or their relationship to the sessions. **Unverifiable from abstract only.**
- **Suggested fix:** "evaluation by blinded judges whose expertise and independence from the sessions are not specified in the abstract"

Everything else — 1,008 summaries (504/504), 108 five-minute segments, 36 sessions, clinicians rated higher overall, LLM superior on central themes, no difference on clinical value for therapeutic relationship and intrapersonal functioning, "first direct comparison", draft-and-review conclusion — matches the abstract.

### Study 7: Automated sentiment analysis of therapy sessions barely matches what human coders see
**PMID:** 42708250 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Predicted" overstates a dyadic association** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Both tools nonetheless predicted therapist- and client-rated early alliance."
- **Abstract says:** "Automated sentiment was dyadically associated with therapist- and client-reported early alliance."
- **Problem:** Two changes: "associated with" becomes "predicted" (directional/causal lean), and the abstract does not break the alliance result out by tool, so "both tools" is not established.
- **Suggested fix:** "Automated sentiment was nonetheless associated with therapist- and client-rated early alliance."

The turn-level null convergence, the weak session-level convergence, the client-versus-therapist negativity finding, the 108 sessions, LIWC and XLM-T, and Peluso's Florida State affiliation all check out.

### Study 8: Whether a patient had a "sudden gain" in therapy depends on which questionnaire you used
**PMID:** 40955789 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Overturns prior research" overstates the novelty category** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** "These results seem to raise important questions about the detection of sudden gains, and the comparability of findings across studies using different measures."
- **Problem:** The paper raises a measurement-comparability caution; it does not test or refute prior substantive findings about sudden gains. The authors' own framing is "a reason for caution".
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** Methodological challenge to an established literature"

**Issue B. "Artifact of the questionnaire" goes beyond the finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the week everything suddenly shifts — may be partly an artifact of the questionnaire"
- **Abstract says:** "the standard and ultra-brief measures identified different individuals as having experienced a sudden gain"
- **Problem:** Measure-dependent *identification* is a detection problem; it does not establish that the phenomenon itself is an artifact. Also worth noting the abstract's directional detail the digest smooths over: more sudden gains by PHQ-9 than PHQ-2, but more by GAD-2 than GAD-7.
- **Suggested fix:** "the week everything suddenly shifts — may be harder to pin down than the literature assumes, because who counts as having had one depends on the scale"

### Study 9: Trauma-focused therapy for PTSD plus addiction helps veterans more than civilians
**PMID:** 41114957 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Safety claim the study did not test** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "it addresses the persistent clinical worry that trauma-focused therapy destabilizes people who are drinking. It did not; it helped."
- **Abstract says:** "trauma-focused psychotherapy targeting PTSD and integrated trauma-focused psychotherapy ... were both superior to treatment as usual for alcohol use severity outcomes"
- **Problem:** Superiority on a group-mean alcohol severity outcome is not a test of destabilisation, dropout or adverse events, none of which the abstract reports. "It did not" asserts a safety finding not in evidence, and a reader could take it as clearance to proceed clinically.
- **Suggested fix:** "it speaks to the persistent clinical worry about trauma-focused therapy in people who are drinking: on alcohol severity outcomes, these therapies outperformed treatment as usual. The abstract reports no data on adverse events or dropout."

**Issue B. Headline generalises a single-domain effect** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Trauma-focused therapy for PTSD plus addiction helps veterans more than civilians"
- **Abstract says:** "this effect was stronger for veterans than civilians" (alcohol use severity only); "Analyses did not provide evidence for differential response ... on PTSD or drug use severity outcomes as a function of veteran status."
- **Problem:** The veteran advantage exists on one of three outcome domains. The body text and Caveats say so; the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Trauma-focused therapy for PTSD plus addiction cuts drinking more in veterans than civilians"

Funding (NIAAA) and the COPE-manual/industry conflicts are both disclosed in the Caveats — correctly handled.

### Study 10: Muslim clients say they censor their faith in the therapy room
**PMID:** 42068016 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Visible discomfort" is not what the abstract reports** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "barriers — therapists' visible discomfort and limited knowledge, secular service frameworks, and clients' resulting self-censorship"
- **Abstract says:** "perceived barriers to discussing religion in therapy reflected therapists' **limited confidence and knowledge**, secular service frameworks and participants' resulting self-censorship"
- **Problem:** "Limited confidence" becomes "visible discomfort", a stronger and more behavioural claim. The pitch angle then compounds it ("Clients are managing their therapists' discomfort about religion").
- **Suggested fix:** "barriers — therapists' limited confidence and knowledge, secular service frameworks, and clients' resulting self-censorship"; and in the pitch, "Clients are working around their therapists' uncertainty about religion".

Sample (25 UK Muslim adults, NHS and private), reflexive thematic analysis, the two overarching themes, and the Bath affiliation all check out.

### Study 11: In psilocybin trials, giving the active metabolite may be gentler than the drug itself
**PMID:** 42668404 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Specific side effects named that the abstract does not name** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "suggests the active metabolite gives you the same trip with less nausea and cardiovascular load"
- **Abstract says:** "Oral psilocin produced a similar temporal and subjective profile to oral psilocybin, with a **lower burden of adverse effects**."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports adverse effect burden in aggregate and mentions vital signs as an assessment method, but does not specify nausea or cardiovascular outcomes. **Unverifiable from abstract only.**
- **Suggested fix:** "suggests the active metabolite gives a comparable experience with a lower overall burden of adverse effects"

Design (randomized within-subject crossover, N=20, 10 men/10 women, mean age 40.1), doses, therapeutic support, and the sublingual exposure caveat are all accurate, and the Filament Health Corp. involvement is disclosed in the Caveats.

### Study 12: European psychiatrists learn to handle suicide risk mostly by living through a patient's suicide
**PMID:** 42622500 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Mostly" / "rather than" overstate the authors' own hedge** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "European psychiatrists learn to handle suicide risk mostly by living through a patient's suicide" and "a core clinical skill is being acquired reactively, through adverse events, rather than through structured teaching"
- **Abstract says:** "Exposure to patient suicide is associated with higher knowledge levels, suggesting that learning may rely **partly** on experiential rather than structured training pathways."
- **Problem:** The finding is a cross-sectional association between exposure and higher knowledge scores, which the authors interpret as a *partial* reliance on experiential learning. The digest upgrades "partly" to "mostly" and to an exclusive "rather than". The Caveats correctly note the association could run in either direction, but the headline and Why-it-matters do not reflect that.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "European psychiatrists who have lost a patient to suicide score higher on suicide knowledge — and get less support afterwards." Why it matters: "It suggests a core clinical skill may be acquired partly through adverse events rather than structured teaching — and that clinicians who go through the event are left comparatively unsupported."

Mean SKSQ score (76.7%), N=259 across 36 countries, the psychotherapy-training and comfort associations, and the Volpe affiliation all check out.

### Study 13: Indigenous Australians reach mental health services more often but get fewer therapy sessions
**PMID:** 42592892 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline generalises a single urban region to a national population** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Indigenous Australians reach mental health services more often but get fewer therapy sessions"
- **Abstract says:** "an urban Indigenous population"; "2,494,381 individuals (2.98% Indigenous) in Southeast Queensland residents"; title: "A case study from Southeast Queensland"
- **Problem:** The authors explicitly frame this as a single-region urban case study. The Caveats flag it, but the headline reads as a national finding.
- **Suggested fix:** "In urban Southeast Queensland, Indigenous residents reach mental health services more often but get fewer therapy sessions"

Minor wording note: the abstract says these subgroups were "less likely to use any services"; the digest says "least likely", a small strengthening. Everything else — linked 2021 Census/MBS data, higher any-use, fewer total and psychotherapy sessions, longer waits between care plan and follow-up, subgroup predictors, and the QCMHR affiliations — is accurate.

### Study 14: When hypnosis makes facial pain worse, it may be revealing where the pain came from
**PMID:** 42685357 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. Framing is consistent with the abstract: three patients, tertiary orofacial pain clinic, standardized hypnotic sessions, symptom exacerbation interpreted as diagnostically meaningful rather than a treatment failure, and subsequent engagement with previously resisted psychotherapy. The hedging ("may be revealing", "Not much yet in terms of hard evidence") is appropriate for a three-case series, the Caveats are complete, and the NS fit of 4/10 is well calibrated to the evidence.
