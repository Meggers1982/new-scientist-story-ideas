### 1. Telling people prisons are cruel doesn't make them want reform — but changing how they see prisoners does

**Journal:** *Law and Human Behavior* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 9
**PMID:** 42423676 | **DOI:** 10.1037/lhb0000673
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Counterintuitive null result overturning a common advocacy assumption, large US sample (N=1,110), clean randomised design, and strong societal hook around criminal justice and racial resentment — docking one point because the Prolific quota sample may not fully represent public opinion.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers recruited a US quota sample of 1,110 adults via Prolific and randomly assigned them to one of seven conditions in a factorial experiment, varying information about prisons (rehabilitation programmes, cruel conditions, or recidivism statistics) and how that information was framed (justice-focused vs utilitarian). Contrary to the researchers' hypothesis — and to much advocacy messaging — neither the content nor the framing of brief pro-reform messages meaningfully shifted support for prison reform. Instead, the strongest predictors of reform support were pre-existing beliefs about prisoners' capacity for rehabilitation and levels of racial resentment, with perceptions of prisoners' moral character also playing a key mediating role.

**Why it matters:** The findings suggest that short-term information campaigns about prison conditions or public safety are unlikely to move the needle on criminal justice reform, pointing instead to the deeper psychological and attitudinal roots of public opinion on punishment.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Showing people that prisons are cruel makes no difference to whether they want reform" A large randomised experiment finds that telling Americans about prison cruelty or rising recidivism does almost nothing to increase their support for prison reform. Researchers tested seven different messaging strategies on over 1,100 US adults and found that none of them — including harrowing accounts of prison conditions or stark recidivism statistics — meaningfully changed support for prison reform. What did predict support was something far harder to shift: whether people believed prisoners were capable of change, and how much racial resentment they harboured. The societal hook is sobering — if brief informational campaigns can't move public opinion on criminal justice, reform advocates may need to rethink their entire communications strategy. Why it fits: NS Mind readers will be struck by the implication that decades of facts-based criminal justice advocacy may be targeting the wrong psychological lever entirely.

### 2. All-nighters can now be run from home — and the science holds up just as well

**Journal:** *Sleep* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 13
**PMID:** 41947369 | **DOI:** 10.1093/sleep/zsag082
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Counterintuitive methodological finding with a clear societal hook (sleep science democratized via webcam), human subjects, RCT design, and direct relevance to cognition and sleep — docked one point for modest sample size and college-only population.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at Baylor University randomly assigned 77 young adults (mean age ~20) to either a normal night's sleep or total sleep deprivation, with the sleep-deprived group continuously monitored via webcam from their own homes. Adherence was remarkably high — deprived participants averaged just 0.04 hours of sleep — and dropout rates were very low (4% withdrawal, 6% dismissal). Sleep deprivation impaired science concept learning and dampened perceived physiological arousal but did not significantly increase reported stress; recovery sleep restored learning performance to baseline levels. The study suggests that rigorous total sleep deprivation experiments can be conducted outside a laboratory with high fidelity.

**Why it matters:** Remote sleep deprivation protocols could dramatically lower the cost and logistical burden of sleep research, enabling larger, more diverse studies and international collaborations that were previously impractical.

The twist: recovery sleep fully erased those learning deficits. This could open sleep research to far larger, more diverse samples, and even cross-continental overnight studies.
