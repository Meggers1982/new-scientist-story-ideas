### 1. When you pick a card, any card, you're probably just reaching for the one closest to your right hand

**Journal:** *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 17
**PMID:** 42466586 | **DOI:** 10.1177/17470218261472690
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Highly counterintuitive finding that overturns attentional and cognitive heuristic accounts of choice bias, with a magician's trick as the experimental hook — exactly the kind of surprising, accessible cognitive science NS Mind excels at; penalised slightly for single lab experiment with a narrow stimulus set.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at the Universities of Plymouth and Essex recruited 137 participants to select one of four face-down playing cards in a classic magician's 'force' setup, while their eye movements were tracked. Despite spending more time looking at the two central cards, participants' gaze patterns did not predict which card they ultimately chose. More reflective thinkers and those who deliberated longer were no less susceptible to the bias. Instead, the overwhelming predictor was physical reach: participants favoured the card requiring the least motor effort, supporting what the authors call the 'reachability hypothesis' over attentional or cognitive heuristic explanations.

**Why it matters:** The finding suggests that many everyday choices — from selecting items on a supermarket shelf to picking options in a user interface — may be driven less by attention or deliberation than by the simple physical ease of reaching for something. This has broad implications for how choice environments are designed.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "When you pick a card, any card, you're probably just reaching for the one closest to your right hand" Magicians have long exploited our tendency to pick the card closest to the middle — but a new study finds the real reason has nothing to do with where your eyes go or how hard you think. Using the classic conjurer's 'force' — where a magician subtly steers a volunteer to pick a specific card — researchers at Plymouth and Essex tested three rival theories of why we gravitate toward central options. Eye tracking showed participants did stare longer at the central cards, but that gaze predicted nothing about which card they chose. Slower, more reflective thinkers were just as susceptible as snap decision-makers. The real driver? Simple physical effort: people overwhelmingly chose the card easiest to reach. The finding reframes decades of 'centre-stage effect' research and has sobering implications for everything from supermarket layouts to digital menus — our choices may be far more bodily than we think. Why it fits: NS Mind readers love studies that expose the hidden mechanics of everyday decision-making, and this one uses a magic trick to do it.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** single-center, single experiment, laboratory setting, restricted stimulus type (playing cards only). Single laboratory experiment with playing cards only; findings may not generalise to choices involving more meaningful or familiar options; all participants were in a single physical setup, limiting ecological validity *Editor's fact-check note: Abstract states n=137 and that 52% chose the middle-right card. Summary accurately reflects that attentional bias did not predict card selection and that reachability was supported. No corrections needed.*

### 2. Autistic kids can rewire their brain's response to sensory overload — but it doesn't always feel better yet

**Journal:** *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, and Allied Disciplines* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 15
**PMID:** 42454526 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jcpp.70202
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Counterintuitive dissociation between brain change and felt experience in autistic youth is a strong NS Mind hook, with decent sample size, fMRI design, and high topic fit for neurodiversity coverage.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers used fMRI to examine whether cognitive reappraisal — thinking about a sensation objectively rather than emotionally — changes brain responses to unpleasant touch in autistic and typically developing children. Forty-seven autistic youth and 45 age- and IQ-matched typically developing youth aged 8–15 underwent tactile stimulation while scanning. Autistic children showed reduced activity in sensory brain regions during reappraisal, suggesting a real neural effect, but — unlike their typically developing peers — they did not report feeling less bothered by the sensation. Older autistic youth and those with more severe sensory over-responsivity showed distinct patterns of frontal brain engagement, suggesting the strategy's effectiveness may depend on age and individual sensory profile.

**Why it matters:** Sensory over-responsivity affects a large proportion of autistic people and has few evidence-based treatments; finding that a trainable cognitive strategy produces measurable brain changes — even if subjective relief lags behind — opens a plausible route toward targeted interventions.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Autistic kids' brains respond to a calming technique — their feelings just don't know it yet" A mind-over-matter strategy for sensory overload visibly quiets the brains of autistic children, yet they still feel just as overwhelmed — a puzzling mismatch that could reshape how therapists approach autism treatment. Researchers scanned autistic and typically developing children aged 8–15 while applying unpleasant touch to their arms, asking them to either react naturally or reframe the sensation cognitively. Autistic children's sensory brain regions quietened during reframing — exactly the neural shift you'd want — yet they reported no reduction in how awful the sensation felt, unlike their non-autistic peers. The finding raises a provocative question: can training bridge the gap between brain and experience, and might older or less severely affected autistic youth get there first? Why it fits: NS Mind readers drawn to neurodiversity, emotion regulation, and the gap between what the brain does and what we consciously experience will find this genuinely surprising and clinically relevant.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** small sample (N<100), single-center, short follow-up. Small sample (N=92 total, 47 autistic), single-center US study, no long-term follow-up, and subjective ratings were taken immediately after each stimulus so emotional learning over time was not measured

### 3. New fathers' jealousy of the baby-mother bond predicts how much mothers unconsciously push dads away

**Journal:** *Journal of Family Psychology* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 13
**PMID:** 42441646 | **DOI:** 10.1037/fam0001521
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Counterintuitive and emotionally resonant finding — paternal jealousy shaping maternal behaviour, and effects showing up in observation but not self-report — with a strong relationships/parenthood hook, decent sample, and longitudinal design; penalised for restriction to different-sex dual-earner couples limiting generalisability.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at Ohio State University followed 182 different-sex, dual-earner couples from late pregnancy through nine months postpartum, examining how jealousy of the partner-infant relationship related to maternal gatekeeping — behaviours that either encourage or restrict fathers' parenting involvement. Mothers and fathers each reported their own jealousy at three months postpartum; gatekeeping was measured both by self-report and by coding observed mother-father-infant interactions. Maternal jealousy at three months predicted an increase in observed gateclosing (restrictive) behaviours by nine months. Notably, paternal jealousy was associated with fathers perceiving greater maternal restriction and less encouragement, even though it did not predict changes in observed gatekeeping over time.

**Why it matters:** The study highlights that jealousy — an emotion rarely discussed in the context of new parenthood — may quietly shape how involved fathers are allowed to become, with effects that are visible in behaviour but not always conscious or self-reported. This points to emotional dynamics in couples as an underexplored target for supporting father involvement.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "New fathers' jealousy of the baby-mother bond predicts how much mothers unconsciously push dads away" When new fathers feel jealous of their partner's bond with the baby, mothers become measurably more restrictive of dad's parenting — even though neither partner seems to notice it happening. Most research on maternal gatekeeping — the subtle ways mothers control how involved fathers become — has focused on mothers' own attitudes and confidence. This Ohio State study of 182 couples introduces a startling new player: the father's feelings. When dads reported feeling jealous of the mother-infant relationship at three months postpartum, mothers were observed to become more restrictive in their parenting behaviour by nine months — a pattern that didn't show up in anyone's self-reports. The finding suggests that jealousy ripples through new families in ways that are real but invisible to those experiencing them, potentially entrenching unequal parenting roles before anyone realises what's happening. Why it fits: The transition to parenthood, relationship dynamics, and the invisible forces shaping family life are core NS Mind territory, and this study has a genuinely surprising emotional twist.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** observational design, self-reported outcomes (for jealousy and reported gatekeeping), restricted to different-sex dual-earner couples, US sample only, single-center. Sample restricted to different-sex, dual-earner US couples — findings may not apply to same-sex parents, single-income families, or other cultural contexts; jealousy was self-reported and may be subject to social desirability bias; observational design precludes causal conclusions *Editor's fact-check note: Abstract states N=182 couples and that maternal jealousy predicted increases in observed (not self-reported) gateclosing — a critical distinction accurately captured in the summary. Paternal jealousy finding (associated with perceptions but not observed change) also accurately represented.*

Both formats were associated with significant reductions in anxiety and gains in career adaptability. The finding is counterintuitive: we tend to assume human empathy is the engine of therapeutic progress. The result arrives as AI therapy apps proliferate globally, forcing a reckoning with what human therapists actually add — and whether speed of improvement is even the right metric. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are acutely interested in AI's encroachment on human connection and mental health care — this speaks directly to both.

### 5. Your THC blood test might flag you as impaired the morning after smoking — even when your driving is fine

**PMID:** 42443621 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s00213-026-07129-1
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — Counterintuitive policy-relevant finding with a clear societal hook (drug-driving laws), but small sample and observational design limit strength.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at CAMH in Toronto tested 65 frequent cannabis users (≥4 times/week) on a driving simulator and cognitive tasks 12–15 hours after their last evening cannabis use. Blood THC ≥2 ng/mL and oral fluid THC ≥25 ng/mL were compared against current legal per-se cut-offs used in some jurisdictions. While oral fluid THC thresholds were significantly associated with worse cognitive performance (Trail Making Test), neither blood nor oral fluid cut-offs showed statistically significant associations with driving simulation performance after correcting for multiple comparisons, suggesting the thresholds are poor proxies for actual driving impairment in frequent users.

**Why it matters:** Millions of cannabis users in jurisdictions with per-se THC driving laws could face legal consequences based on biological thresholds that may not reliably reflect real driving impairment, raising serious questions about the validity of current drug-driving legislation.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Morning-after cannabis tests flag impairment — but the driving simulator disagrees" Frequent cannabis users who smoked the night before tested above legal THC limits the next morning, yet showed no statistically significant driving impairment on a simulator. Governments in Canada, the US, and elsewhere have set blood and oral-fluid THC thresholds as legal proof of impairment — but a new study of 65 frequent users found those cut-offs don't reliably predict whether someone drives worse the morning after. The finding is counterintuitive because it suggests the science behind drug-driving law may be shakier than legislators assumed, with serious implications for people being wrongly charged. Why it fits: NS Mind readers who care about legal rights, cognitive science, and drug policy will find this directly relevant to real-world questions about fairness and accuracy of impaired-driving laws.

### 6. People who constantly compare their bodies to others are surprisingly immune to a technique designed to improve body image

**PMID:** 42435138 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10339-026-01376-y
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — Counterintuitive finding with a clear body-image hook — the people most prone to social comparison are least helped by a body-image conditioning technique — appeals strongly to NS Mind's audience, though the lab-based design with artificial stimuli limits real-world translation.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Polish researchers tested whether evaluative conditioning — a learning technique that pairs neutral symbols with positive or negative images — could shift attitudes toward body-related cues in 146 adults. Participants completed a conditioning task pairing geometric shapes with either average-weight or obese body images, alongside measures of self-esteem and social comparison tendencies. Shapes paired with average-weight bodies became significantly more positively rated, but — counterintuitively — participants who scored high on body-related social comparison showed no improvement in liking across either condition, while low-comparison participants improved regardless of which body type was used. Self-esteem did not moderate the effect.

**Why it matters:** Evaluative conditioning is being explored as a potential tool for body image interventions, but this study suggests that the people most likely to engage in harmful body comparisons — arguably those most in need of help — may be the least responsive to this approach. This has implications for how such techniques might need to be tailored.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "People who constantly compare their bodies to others are surprisingly immune to a technique designed to improve body image" A psychological conditioning technique improved body-related attitudes in most people — except those who habitually compare their bodies to others, the very group it might matter most for. Evaluative conditioning — training the brain to associate neutral symbols with positive images — has been touted as a low-cost tool for improving body image. But a Polish study of 146 adults found a striking catch: participants who frequently compared their bodies to others showed no improvement whatsoever, while those who rarely made such comparisons benefited regardless of which body images they saw. The finding suggests that habitual social comparison may create a kind of psychological armour against attitude-change techniques, raising questions about whether the most at-risk individuals require fundamentally different approaches. With body dissatisfaction driving eating disorders globally, understanding who responds to these tools — and who doesn't — is urgent. Why it fits: Body image, social comparison, and the limits of psychological interventions are perennial NS Mind topics with huge reader engagement.

Laboratory study using abstract geometric shapes, not real body-image stimuli; no clinical population; single Polish sample; no long-term follow-up to assess whether attitude changes persist or translate to real-world behaviour *Editor's fact-check note: Abstract states N=146, which is above the <100 threshold for 'small sample' flag; however it is modest for a conditioning study with moderation analyses, so flagged as borderline. ### 7. Trainee therapists don't consistently overestimate their skills — they misjudge in surprising directions

**Journal:** *Psychotherapy Research* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 13
**PMID:** 42444297 | **DOI:** 10.1080/10503307.2026.2701026
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — Overturning the assumption of trainee overconfidence is counterintuitive and relevant to anyone who has been in therapy or training, but small N, secondary analysis, and limited direct reader lifestyle hook keep the score moderate.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers analysed data from 73 psychology students taking part in an RCT on clinical supervision, comparing students' self-assessments of their therapy skills with ratings from independent observers and supervisors. Students' communication skills, empathy, and therapeutic alliance were evaluated from multiple viewpoints using a truth-and-bias model. Contrary to the widely held assumption that trainee therapists overestimate themselves, no consistent pattern of overestimation emerged: students overestimated therapeutic alliance relative to observers but actually underestimated their own empathy relative to supervisors. Across all skills, self-assessments showed no significant accuracy when compared to external ratings.

**Why it matters:** Training programmes widely rely on self-reflection as a tool for therapist development, but if trainees' self-assessments are neither reliably biased nor reliably accurate, supervision and feedback structures may need rethinking.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Trainee therapists don't think they're better than they are — the reality is stranger than that" The long-standing assumption that student therapists overrate their own skills turns out to be largely a myth — but their self-assessments are still surprisingly disconnected from reality. A study of 73 psychology students found no consistent self-inflation in how they rated their own therapy skills — students overestimated their therapeutic rapport but underestimated their empathy, and overall their self-ratings bore little relation to what independent raters or supervisors observed. The finding challenges decades of assumption baked into therapist training programmes that self-reflection is a reliable developmental tool. It raises the question of what self-assessment is actually measuring, and whether structured feedback rather than introspection should be the standard. Why it fits: NS Mind readers curious about how therapists are made, the psychology of self-knowledge, and what actually makes therapy work will find the nuanced mismatch here more interesting than a simple overconfidence story.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** small sample (N<100), single-center, self-reported outcomes, secondary analysis. Small sample (N=73), secondary analysis of an existing RCT, single institution in Germany, and all ratings are subjective — the study cannot tell us whether more accurate self-assessment would actually produce better therapists

### 8. Your brain can't extract visual averages without paying attention first

**Journal:** *International Journal of Psychophysiology* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 13
**PMID:** 42442506 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2026.113439
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — Counterintuitive finding that overturns prior belief scores well, but a tiny lab sample (N=30), narrow psychophysics task, and limited lifestyle/societal hook limit NS Mind appeal.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers tested whether the brain can statistically summarise groups of visual objects (ensemble perception) without directed attention, using a spatial cueing task with EEG in 30 young adults (mean age 19.6). When attention was misdirected to the wrong side, performance on judging the average orientation of a cluster of patterns dropped to chance — worse than for single-object perception. EEG and multivariate pattern analysis confirmed attention was genuinely misdirected on those trials, suggesting ensemble perception is not the attention-free shortcut it was previously thought to be.

**Why it matters:** The finding challenges a widely held view that the brain computes visual crowd statistics automatically and without effort, with implications for understanding how attention shapes our perception of complex, busy environments.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Your brain can't read a crowd without actually looking at it" A core assumption in vision science — that the brain silently averages the world around us without needing attention — turns out to be wrong. Scientists have long believed the brain effortlessly extracts statistical summaries of visual scenes — useful for navigating crowds or reading a room at a glance. A new EEG study finds that when attention is pointed elsewhere, this supposed superpower collapses entirely. The result overturns textbook assumptions about 'pre-attentive' processing and raises fresh questions about how much of our rich visual world is actually constructed on the fly. Why it fits: NS Mind readers fascinated by how the mind constructs reality will be surprised to learn that a supposedly automatic perceptual shortcut is anything but.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** small sample (N<100), single-center, short follow-up. Very small sample (N=30 university students), artificial lab stimuli not representative of real-world scenes, single-centre study — results need replication in larger and more diverse groups

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 136 pediatric neurologists across Japan between January and March 2025 to map current practices around prehospital use of buccal midazolam for childhood status epilepticus (prolonged seizures). Of 134 who knew the drug, 118 had prescribing experience. **Why it matters:** Inconsistent caregiver instructions from clinicians could lead to dangerous delays in emergency care for children having seizures; the findings highlight the need for clearer national guidelines on prehospital seizure management.

A nationwide Japanese survey of paediatric neurologists found wide variation in the instructions given to caregivers about prehospital seizure treatment — particularly around calling emergency services. For a condition where minutes matter, inconsistent guidance could cost lives. The findings echo concerns about the lack of harmonised emergency protocols in other countries too. Why it fits: Parents of children with epilepsy are a significant NS Mind readership segment, and uncertainty in clinical guidance is a perennial source of public interest.
