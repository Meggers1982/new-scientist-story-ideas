### 1. Exposure therapy for spider phobia works less well when progesterone is high

**Journal:** *Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics* | **Published:** September 5, 2026
**PMID:** 42700422 | **DOI:** 10.1159/pps/aemag012
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — genuinely new question ("when does therapy work?") with a clean biological hook, held back by a small sample and a null result on the headline comparison
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Researchers recruited 45 women with spider phobia, grouped by hormonal status — early-to-mid follicular phase, mid-luteal phase, or using hormonal contraceptives — and put them through group exposure therapy, measuring fear and avoidance at baseline, one week after, and 12–14 weeks later. Everyone improved. Menstrual cycle group made no difference, but women with higher progesterone on the actual day of the exposure session showed less improvement in behavioral avoidance, the measure that tracks what people will physically do rather than what they say they feel.

**Why it matters:** It raises the possibility that the *timing* of a therapy session against a person's internal hormonal rhythm shapes how much fear extinction sticks — a variable no clinic currently considers. This is preliminary and needs replication in far larger samples before anyone reschedules an appointment.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Everything we know about psychotherapy is about *who* it works for and *why*; almost nothing about *when*. Open on a woman standing in front of a tarantula tank on a Tuesday, and the question of whether next Tuesday would have gone better. Lead with the progesterone-avoidance link and the concept of "bio-rhythm informed psychotherapy". Call Susanne Fischer and Ulrike Ehlert at the University of Zurich, and get an independent fear-extinction researcher to say how much weight 45 participants can carry.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on the neglect of hormonal variation across clinical trial design generally — from analgesia to vaccines to psychotherapy — and the growing push to treat the menstrual cycle as a measured variable rather than noise. Fits a health-policy or women's-health-focused outlet, or a long read for a general-interest magazine.

**Caveats:** Small N (45), three-arm design with unequal hormonal groups, no randomization to cycle phase (participants were enrolled according to their own cycles), single site, the primary group comparison was null and the hormone finding is a secondary correlational result, self-reported measures alongside the behavioral test.

### 2. People want their psilocybin natural, even when the synthetic version is chemically identical

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychoactive Drugs* | **Published:** September 1, 2026
**PMID:** 42678038 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02791072.2026.2727013
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a clean, quotable irrationality in how people think about psychedelic medicine, with decent sample sizes; limited by the fact that intentions are not behavior
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Across two studies with 1,102 participants in total, researchers measured how willing people were to use psilocybin for a mental health problem compared with psychotherapy or conventional psychiatric medication. Psilocybin came last. And in the second study, people were significantly more willing to take psilocybin "isolated from mushrooms" than an identical synthetic version, or a synthetic analog producing the same biological effects. Willingness was higher in men, in younger people, and in those reporting more current depression, more mindfulness — and more narcissism.

**Why it matters:** If regulators approve psilocybin, uptake will be shaped by a naturalness bias that has no pharmacological basis, and by the fact that the public still ranks it below talking therapy. That is a marketing and public-health problem before it is a clinical one.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Hype notwithstanding, the public is less keen on psychedelic therapy than on ordinary talking therapy — and prefers its molecules to have come out of a mushroom. Open on the identical-molecule comparison, which is a textbook naturalness fallacy running straight into a soon-to-be-regulated medicine. Call Brian Meier at Gettysburg College, and Matthew Johnson (Medical College of Georgia) for the clinical-translation view.
- **Wider angle:** A consumer-psychology piece on the "appeal to nature" across medicine and food — supplements, bioidentical hormones, natural flavorings — and how the psychedelics industry is already positioning itself around botanical extracts. Fits a business or consumer-affairs outlet.

**Caveats:** Hypothetical intentions rather than real uptake; online convenience samples of unclear representativeness; self-reported depression and personality measures; population may not generalize beyond the US.

### 3. Psychotherapy use in the US jumped 41 per cent in four years — and worsening mental health explains only a third of it

**Journal:** *Psychiatric Services* | **Published:** September 11, 2026
**PMID:** 42723007 | **DOI:** 10.1176/appi.ps.20260060
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — very large, well-analyzed national dataset with a genuinely unexplained residual, but it is a trends paper without a single striking mechanism
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Using 147,488 respondents from the 2019–2023 National Health Interview Surveys, researchers tracked mental health care use before and after the pandemic. Any mental health care rose from 19.2 per cent to 23.8 per cent; psychotherapy use rose from 9.5 per cent to 13.4 per cent, a 41 per cent increase. Decomposition analysis attributed 36.7 per cent of the overall rise to changes in anxiety and depression symptoms and 11.3 per cent to insurance coverage — leaving roughly half unaccounted for.

**Why it matters:** The standard story is that more Americans are in therapy because more Americans are unwell. This says symptoms and insurance together explain under half the surge, pointing to shifts in help-seeking, stigma, telehealth and expectation that nobody has yet quantified.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Therapy went mainstream faster than distress did. Open on the missing half of the increase and what plausibly fills it — teletherapy apps, a post-2020 collapse in stigma, therapy as self-improvement rather than treatment. Call Y. Nina Gao and Mark Olfson at Columbia; ask them directly what they think the unexplained share is.
- **Wider angle:** A trend piece on therapy as a consumer category — waitlists, subscription platforms, the workforce shortage it creates — and whether a system built for illness can absorb a population seeking optimization. Fits a business, health-policy or general-interest outlet.

**Caveats:** Observational survey data, cannot establish causation; self-reported service use and symptoms; decomposition results depend on model specification; US-only.

### 4. Children's therapy works better when the therapist unconsciously mirrors them

**Journal:** *Psychotherapy* | **Published:** September 10, 2026
**PMID:** 42721331 | **DOI:** 10.1037/pst0000639
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — an appealing, very visual mechanism tested with real coded sessions and a mediation model; correlational within a trial, and the mediator is measured in-session
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Researchers coded 245 sessions from early, middle and late phases of Mentalization-Based Treatment for Children, involving 95 children (mean age 7.8) with internalizing and externalizing problems and 16 therapists. Therapists who showed more nonverbal coordination — matching posture, rhythm and movement during play — had children who regulated their emotions better within the session, and that in-session regulation predicted lower total problem scores and better parent-reported emotion regulation over treatment. The effect of nonverbal coordination on outcomes ran entirely through in-session regulation; the direct path was not significant.

**Why it matters:** It gives an empirical spine to something child therapists do intuitively, and suggests the active ingredient in play therapy may be bodily synchrony rather than anything said out loud.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The most important thing a child therapist does may happen below the neck. Open in a playroom where the therapist is silently matching a seven-year-old's tempo with a toy car. Hook: a measured, mediated pathway from therapist movement to parent-reported behavior at home. Call Sibel Halfon at Istanbul Bilgi University; pair with an interpersonal-synchrony researcher for scepticism about coding-based mediation.
- **Wider angle:** A broader feature on interpersonal synchrony — in parenting, teaching, music ensembles, therapy — and what happens when this research meets video calls, where bodily coordination largely breaks down. Fits a psychology-focused outlet or an education trade title.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis within a clinical trial; mediation from observational session data cannot establish causation; small number of therapists (16) limits generalization; parent-reported outcomes; single center.

### 5. Social life doesn't improve during therapy — it improves for years afterward

**Journal:** *Journal of Clinical Psychology* | **Published:** September 4, 2026
**PMID:** 42695247 | **DOI:** 10.1002/jclp.70220
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a nice reversal of expectation with 2.5 years of follow-up in 370 patients; limited to self-report instruments and a naturalistic design
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Following 370 Norwegian adult outpatients through open-ended psychotherapy with up to 2.5 years of follow-up, researchers tracked the quantity and quality of social relationships alongside a measure of self-affiliation — how warmly people treat themselves. Social connection did not change from before to after treatment. It grew steadily *after* therapy ended, particularly in support and in non-family relationships. And the two measures fed each other in lagged sequence: better social connection predicted more self-kindness at the next assessment, which predicted better social connection after that.

**Why it matters:** Therapy outcomes are almost always measured at discharge, which may be exactly the wrong moment to look for relational change. It also offers evidence for the long-suspected loop between self-compassion and friendship running in both directions.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Therapy's social payoff arrives late — sometimes years after the last session. Open on a patient who left treatment feeling no more connected than when they arrived, and whose friendships slowly widened afterward. Lead on the reciprocal loop between being kind to yourself and having people around you. Call Erkki Heinonen (University of Oslo / Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare) or first author Anna Voldstad at Oxford.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on how mental health services measure success — discharge-day symptom scores versus the things patients actually want, such as friends, work and a life — and the case for long-tail outcome tracking. Fits a health-services or specialist psychiatry publication.

**Caveats:** Naturalistic observational design, no control group, cannot establish causation; entirely self-reported measures; attrition likely across 2.5 years; Norwegian outpatient sample may not generalize.

### 6. AI writes usable therapy notes — but misses what clinicians notice

**Journal:** *Psychotherapy Research* | **Published:** September 9, 2026
**PMID:** 42715612 | **DOI:** 10.1080/10503307.2026.2729093
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — first head-to-head, blinded comparison on a task AI is being sold into right now; the result is a measured "not yet", which is honest but less headline-grabbing
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers generated 1,008 summaries — 504 written by clinicians and 504 by a large language model using a multi-step prompting pipeline — covering 108 five-minute segments from 36 real therapy sessions. Judges blinded to authorship rated factual consistency, preservation of salient meaning, and clinical value. Clinicians won overall, but the model was *better* at preserving central themes, and the two were indistinguishable on clinical value when describing the therapeutic relationship and the client's internal functioning.

**Why it matters:** Documentation is one of the biggest time sinks in mental health care, and this is the first direct evidence on where AI drafting is adequate and where it is not — supporting a draft-and-review workflow rather than replacement.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** An AI can summarize your therapy session, and blinded judges rated it roughly as insightful as a human on the therapeutic relationship — the bit you would least expect a machine to get. Open on a therapist who spends an hour each evening on notes. Call Dana Atzil-Slonim at Bar-Ilan University; ask what clients think about a model reading their sessions.
- **Wider angle:** A privacy and consent story: therapy transcripts are among the most sensitive records that exist, and AI scribes are entering clinics faster than governance frameworks. Fits a technology-policy or investigative outlet.

**Caveats:** Only 36 sessions, judged at five-minute segment level rather than whole sessions; evaluation by expert raters, not by the clinicians or clients involved; no test of downstream effects on care; single research pipeline, results may not transfer to commercial tools.

### 7. Automated sentiment analysis of therapy sessions barely matches what human coders see

**Journal:** *Psychotherapy Research* | **Published:** September 8, 2026
**PMID:** 42708250 | **DOI:** 10.1080/10503307.2026.2724951
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a useful negative result against a fast-growing assumption, though the subject matter is one step removed from readers' own lives
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Using 108 early therapy sessions, trained human coders rated positive, negative and neutral affect turn by turn. The researchers then ran the same transcripts through a dictionary method (LIWC) and a neural language model (XLM-T). At the level of individual conversational turns, the automated tools showed essentially no convergence with human ratings; agreement appeared only weakly when averaged across whole sessions. Automated negativity tracked human coding better for clients' speech than therapists'. Both tools nonetheless predicted therapist- and client-rated early alliance.

**Why it matters:** A lot of computational psychotherapy research, and a growing number of products, assume text sentiment is a proxy for emotion in the room. Turn by turn, it isn't — though it may still carry signal about the relationship.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Machines can tell you something about a therapeutic relationship while getting almost every individual moment wrong. Open on a single exchange where a client says something upbeat in a devastated tone. Lead with the near-zero turn-level convergence. Call Paul Peluso at Florida State University, and pair with someone building AI therapy-monitoring tools for a response.
- **Wider angle:** A skeptical feature on sentiment analysis across the board — customer service, social media mood tracking, political forecasting — and the systematic errors that follow from treating words as feelings. Fits a technology or data-science outlet.

**Caveats:** 108 sessions from one dataset; human coding is itself imperfect as a gold standard; two specific tools tested, results may not extend to newer models; alliance outcomes are self-reported.

### 8. Whether a patient had a "sudden gain" in therapy depends on which questionnaire you used

**Journal:** *Cognitive Behaviour Therapy* | **Published:** September 2026 (online September 16, 2025)
**PMID:** 40955789 | **DOI:** 10.1080/16506073.2025.2556723
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a solid, uncomfortable result for a whole literature, in a large sample; the topic requires some setup for general readers
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** "Sudden gains" — abrupt, lasting drops in symptoms between sessions — are a well-studied phenomenon linked to better outcomes. Analyzing data from 937 university students in a five-week internet-delivered treatment for depression and anxiety, researchers looked for sudden gains using both standard scales (PHQ-9, GAD-7) and their ultra-brief two-item versions. The measures disagreed on both how many sudden gains occurred and, critically, *which individuals* had them.

**Why it matters:** If a phenomenon's presence in a given person changes depending on the instrument, findings across the sudden-gains literature may not be comparable — a measurement problem sitting underneath a body of research about how therapy actually works.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** One of psychotherapy research's most appealing ideas — the week everything suddenly shifts — may be partly an artifact of the questionnaire. Open on the lived experience of a sudden gain, then pull the rug. Call Blake Dear at Macquarie University's eCentreClinic; ask a sudden-gains researcher elsewhere whether they accept the critique.
- **Wider angle:** A measurement-validity feature: how much of psychology's knowledge base is contingent on the specific scales in use, from depression to personality to wellbeing indices. Fits a research-culture or metascience-oriented outlet.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of existing trial data; university student sample in a brief internet-delivered treatment may not generalize to face-to-face clinical care; all outcomes self-reported.

### 9. Trauma-focused therapy for PTSD plus addiction helps veterans more than civilians

**Journal:** *Psychological Trauma* | **Published:** September 2026 (online October 20, 2025)
**PMID:** 41114957 | **DOI:** 10.1037/tra0002043
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — large pooled patient-level dataset and a real moderation finding, but the effect is confined to alcohol outcomes and the population is specialized
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Pooling individual patient data from 26 randomized trials (n=3,228) in the Project Harmony meta-analysis, researchers tested whether veteran status changes how well psychotherapy works for co-occurring PTSD and substance use disorder. Both trauma-focused therapy for PTSD alone and integrated therapy targeting both conditions beat treatment as usual on alcohol use severity — and the advantage was larger for veterans than for civilians. There was no such difference for PTSD symptoms or drug use.

**Why it matters:** It is the first patient-level test of a long-suspected divide, and it addresses the persistent clinical worry that trauma-focused therapy destabilizes people who are drinking. It did not; it helped.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Clinicians have long hesitated to take people with active addictions into trauma-focused therapy. This pooled analysis of 3,228 patients suggests the opposite of harm — and that veterans benefit more than civilians, for reasons nobody can yet explain. Call Shannon Blakey at RTI International or Denise Hien at Rutgers; press them on whether it is the veterans or the veteran-oriented services that differ.
- **Wider angle:** A piece on why so much PTSD evidence comes from military samples, and what that means for civilian survivors of assault, accidents and displacement who are treated on the strength of it. Fits a specialist psychiatry publication or a long-read current-affairs outlet.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of trial data with propensity-score weighting, not a randomized comparison of veterans versus civilians; effect limited to one outcome domain; several authors are authors of the COPE treatment manual and one reports extensive industry ties and patents; funded by the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism.

### 10. Muslim clients say they censor their faith in the therapy room

**Journal:** *Psychology and Psychotherapy: Theory, Research and Practice* | **Published:** September 2026 (Epub May 1, 2026)
**PMID:** 42068016 | **DOI:** 10.1111/papt.70071
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a rich, well-conducted qualitative study on a real gap in service delivery; qualitative design caps how far it can be pushed as news
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Twenty-five Muslim adults in the UK who had received therapy in NHS and private settings were interviewed about how religion was handled. Reflexive thematic analysis identified two patterns: barriers — therapists' visible discomfort and limited knowledge, secular service frameworks, and clients' resulting self-censorship — and recommendations, including proactive rather than reactive engagement with faith and organizational change to legitimize religion as clinically relevant material.

**Why it matters:** For many clients, religion is not an add-on but the organizing frame of their distress and their coping. If they edit it out to make therapists comfortable, the therapy is working on an incomplete account.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Clients are managing their therapists' discomfort about religion — and losing part of the treatment in the process. Open on a participant deciding, mid-session, not to mention prayer. Lead on self-censorship as a mechanism rather than a complaint. Call Rabiah Islam at the University of Bath; get a response from an NHS talking-therapies commissioner on training for religious competence.
- **Wider angle:** A broader feature on the secular assumptions embedded in Western psychotherapy — from CBT's cognitive model to conceptions of the self — and the parallel rise of faith-adapted interventions worldwide. Fits a religion-and-society outlet or a general-interest magazine.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (25), not designed to generalize; self-selected participants likely to have had notable experiences; retrospective self-report; single researcher conducting analysis; UK-specific service context.

### 11. In psilocybin trials, giving the active metabolite may be gentler than the drug itself

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychopharmacology* | **Published:** August 29, 2026
**PMID:** 42668404 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02698811261478603
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a genuinely novel within-subject comparison that could reshape trial design, but N=20 healthy volunteers and industry involvement limit it
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** In a randomized crossover trial, 20 healthy adults (10 men, 10 women, mean age 40) took up to four sessions of botanical whole-mushroom-derived preparations: oral psilocybin (25 mg), oral psilocin (17.5 mg), or sublingual psilocin at three doses. All sessions included therapeutic support and psychotherapy before and after dosing. Oral psilocin produced a subjective and temporal experience much like oral psilocybin, but with fewer adverse effects; sublingual psilocin was well tolerated but delivered apparently lower exposure.

**Why it matters:** Nearly every modern psychedelic trial uses synthetic oral psilocybin, which the body must convert to psilocin. Delivering psilocin directly may cut side effects without altering the experience — relevant to how future psychedelic-assisted therapy is actually dosed.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Skip the prodrug. A crossover trial in which the same 20 people took three different mushroom-derived preparations suggests the active metabolite gives you the same trip with less nausea and cardiovascular load. Open on the logic of why psilocybin was ever the standard. Call Joshua Woolley at UCSF; be explicit about the commercial interest in botanical extracts.
- **Wider angle:** A business-of-psychedelics piece on formulation patents — botanical versus synthetic, sublingual versus oral — as the route to intellectual property in a field built on unpatentable natural molecules. Fits a business or pharmaceutical-industry outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (20), healthy volunteers rather than patients, no clinical outcomes measured; open-label subjective ratings; sublingual arm not comparable due to lower exposure; formulations supplied by Filament Health Corp., two of whose employees are co-authors; short follow-up.

### 12. European psychiatrists learn to handle suicide risk mostly by living through a patient's suicide

**Journal:** *International Journal of Psychiatry in Clinical Practice* | **Published:** August 20, 2026
**PMID:** 42622500 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13651501.2026.2715438
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — an uncomfortable finding about medical training with a clear narrative, weakened by a convenience-sample survey design
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** A survey of 259 psychiatric trainees and early-career psychiatrists across 36 European countries measured suicide-related knowledge and skills, exposure to patient suicide, and burnout. Average knowledge scores were 76.7 per cent correct, with wide variation between respondents. Those who had experienced at least one patient suicide scored higher on knowledge — and reported less support and supervision afterward. Psychotherapy training and comfort in asking direct questions about suicide both tracked greater perceived competence.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that a core clinical skill is being acquired reactively, through adverse events, rather than through structured teaching — and that clinicians who go through the event are left comparatively unsupported.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The psychiatrists who know most about suicide risk are the ones who have already lost a patient. Open on the aftermath of a patient suicide and the supervision that didn't follow. Note the separate finding that psychotherapy training predicts confidence in asking the direct question. Call Umberto Volpe at the Polytechnic University of Marche, and the European Federation of Psychiatric Trainees.
- **Wider angle:** A medical-education feature on "postvention" — what health systems owe clinicians after a patient death by suicide — alongside the wider literature on second-victim support in medicine. Fits a medical trade publication or a health-focused news outlet.

**Caveats:** Convenience sample of 259 across 36 countries, so country-level comparison is not possible; cross-sectional and self-reported, including the perceived-competence measure; the knowledge–exposure association could run in either direction; respondents self-selected.

### 13. Indigenous Australians reach mental health services more often but get fewer therapy sessions

**Journal:** *Australian & New Zealand Journal of Psychiatry* | **Published:** August 13, 2026
**PMID:** 42592892 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00048674261476557
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a huge linked administrative dataset with a genuinely unexpected split between access and dose; national policy specificity narrows the appeal
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Researchers linked 2021 Census, Medicare Benefits Schedule and Indigenous identifier data for 2,494,381 people in Southeast Queensland, of whom 2.98 per cent were Indigenous. Indigenous residents were *more* likely than non-Indigenous residents to use any subsidized mental health service — but received fewer total sessions, fewer psychotherapy sessions specifically, and waited longer between a mental health care plan and follow-up treatment. Indigenous men, people over 40, and those with unstable housing or lower educational attainment were least likely to use any service at all.

**Why it matters:** Access statistics and treatment adequacy are not the same thing. A population can look well served on a headline "any use" figure while receiving substantially less of the treatment that actually requires sustained contact.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** A counterintuitive equity finding: getting through the door more often, getting less therapy once inside. Open on the gap between a care plan and the first follow-up session. Lead on why psychotherapy specifically — the part requiring continuity — is where the shortfall shows. Call Sandra Diminic or Xiaolin Zhou at the Queensland Centre for Mental Health Research, and an Institute for Urban Indigenous Health co-author for the community view.
- **Wider angle:** A health-services feature on how "access" is measured in publicly funded mental health systems worldwide, and why session counts and dropout rates tell a different story from contact rates. Fits a health-policy or public-health outlet.

**Caveats:** Observational administrative data, cannot establish causation; one urban region of Australia, explicitly framed as a case study; Medicare-subsidized services only, so care through Aboriginal community-controlled or state services is not captured; no clinical outcomes.

### 14. When hypnosis makes facial pain worse, it may be revealing where the pain came from

**Journal:** *American Journal of Clinical Hypnosis* | **Published:** September 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42685357 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00029157.2026.2710805
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — striking clinical idea that reframes treatment failure as diagnostic information, but it is three cases with no controls
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Clinicians at a tertiary orofacial pain clinic describe three patients with chronic facial pain whose symptoms got *worse* during standardized hypnosis sessions. The authors argue these paradoxical reactions were not failures but revealing moments, exposing a link between the physical symptom and emotional distress the patients had not previously articulated. In all three cases, the reaction led patients to engage with psychotherapy they had previously refused.

**Why it matters:** Not much yet in terms of hard evidence — but it opens a line of enquiry into whether provoking a symptom can serve as a diagnostic tool in functional and psychogenic pain, and it reframes an adverse response as clinically informative rather than a reason to stop.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** A treatment that makes the symptom worse, and a clinician who treats that as the point. Open on a patient in a dental clinic whose jaw pain spikes under hypnotic induction, and who finally connects it to something they had never said out loud. Frame it around the difficult category of functional symptoms and how patients resist psychological explanations. Call Gil Wasserman and Iftah Biran at Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center; get a functional-neurological-disorder specialist for a second opinion.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on the fraught status of functional and psychogenic diagnoses — patients who feel dismissed, clinicians with poor tools, and the hunt for anything that makes the mind-body link tangible to a skeptical patient. Fits a general-interest health magazine or a narrative medicine outlet.

**Caveats:** Case series of three patients, no control group, single center; interpretations are clinical and psychodynamic rather than measured; no outcome data on whether the subsequent psychotherapy helped; risk of harm from deliberately provoking symptoms is not systematically assessed.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch converge on the same uncomfortable point — psychotherapy research's conclusions are hostage to the instruments and timing it uses. "Whether a patient had a 'sudden gain' in therapy depends on which questionnaire you used" (PMID 40955789) shows a celebrated phenomenon changing not just in frequency but in *which people have it* depending on scale length; "Automated sentiment analysis of therapy sessions barely matches what human coders see" (PMID 42708250) finds near-zero turn-level agreement between machines and humans on emotion, yet both still predict alliance; "Social life doesn't improve during therapy — it improves for years afterward" (PMID 42695247) finds the main relational benefit arriving long after the discharge-day assessment that most trials treat as the endpoint; and "Indigenous Australians reach mental health services more often but get fewer therapy sessions" (PMID 42592892) shows a population looking well served on the headline access metric while receiving substantially less actual treatment. "AI writes usable therapy notes — but misses what clinicians notice" (PMID 42715612) sits in the same space: machine and human accounts of the same session diverge in specific, patterned ways.

**Why pitch this now:** Computational tools are being sold into therapy rooms — AI scribes, sentiment dashboards, session analytics — on the assumption that the underlying measurements are sound, at exactly the moment when studies from within the field are showing that scale choice, measurement timing and proxy metrics can each flip the answer.

**Angle:** A reported piece on the measurement layer beneath psychotherapy evidence, following one question — how do we know therapy worked? — through questionnaires, discharge-day endpoints, session counts and now algorithms, for readers who have been in therapy or are being offered app-based versions of it. Written for a general science audience rather than methodologists, with the automation angle as the news peg.

**Potential headlines:**
- What therapy research measures, and what it misses
- The week everything changed in therapy may depend on which form you filled in
- Your therapy is being scored by instruments that disagree about whether it worked

**Potential outlets:**
- **New Scientist Features** — fits the Mind section's appetite for a rigorous look at how psychological evidence is built, with the AI-in-the-clinic hook giving it immediacy.
- **Undark** — a natural home for a measurement-validity story that implicates both a research literature and a commercial product wave.
- **STAT** — strong for the clinical and vendor side: AI scribes and session analytics entering mental health care ahead of validation.
- **Nature (news feature)** — would take the metascience framing, given the sudden-gains instrument-dependence result sits squarely in the reproducibility conversation.
