### Study 1: Copying a smoker's body language makes them smoke more — and want to quit less
**PMID:** 42427246 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Confederate" detail not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "smokers who were subtly mimicked by a confederate smoked more cigarettes"
- **Abstract says:** "A first laboratory experiment showed that nonverbal mimicry (physical actions, hand and arm movements and body posture) increases cigarette consumption due to increased liking for the mimicker."
- **Problem:** The abstract never specifies who did the mimicking or that it was covert. It is the standard paradigm, but it is an assumption at this stage. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "smokers who were mimicked during the interaction smoked more cigarettes, an effect carried by increased liking for the mimicker (the abstract does not describe who performed the mimicry)."

**Issue B. NS fit score of 7 with no reported sample sizes** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "NS fit: 7/10 — two experiments (one in the wild) showing a purely social, non-pharmacological driver of nicotine consumption; limited by thin reporting of sample details in the abstract."
- **Abstract says:** No N reported for either experiment.
- **Problem:** A 7 implies solid evidential footing, but neither experiment's size, randomisation nor effect magnitude can be checked from the abstract. The score is being carried by the design in principle rather than by verifiable numbers.
- **Suggested fix:** "NS fit: 6/10 — pending sight of the full text; two experiments with unreported sample sizes and effect sizes."

### Study 2: People quitting sugar report cravings, low mood and body aches that look like withdrawal
**PMID:** 41905576 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one Moderate)

**Issue A. High-burden group described as a withdrawal profile** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "roughly half of people quitting sugar carry a symptom profile that ticks recognisable substance-withdrawal boxes"
- **Abstract says:** "Group-based trajectory modelling identified a low (47%) and high (53%) symptom burden group." The most frequent symptoms overall were "cravings, preoccupation and difficulty remaining abstinent" — i.e. impaired-control criteria, not withdrawal.
- **Problem:** The 53% group is defined by overall DSM-5 SUD symptom burden across five criterion clusters, not by a withdrawal syndrome. The pitch converts a broad symptom-burden trajectory into a specific withdrawal claim, which is the more exciting but unsupported reading.
- **Suggested fix:** "roughly half of participants fell into a high symptom-burden group, reporting DSM-5-style symptoms — mostly craving, preoccupation and difficulty staying abstinent, with some withdrawal-like low energy, aches and low mood."

**Issue B. Invented clock time** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the evening slump at 8pm — the peak time participants broke"
- **Abstract says:** "Sugar consumption was reported in 9% of EMAs and peaked in the evening EMA (11.5%), primarily as snacks and desserts or baked goods."
- **Problem:** The abstract says "the evening EMA", not 8pm. The survey schedule is not given.
- **Suggested fix:** "the evening survey window — when sugar consumption peaked — mostly on snacks, desserts and baked goods."

**Issue C. Two predictors of high-burden group membership dropped** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the high group predicted by baseline food-addiction scores, higher BMI, craving, anxiety and stress"
- **Abstract says:** "...presence of food addiction as per Yale Food Addiction Scale, higher sugar consumption, BMI, craving, anxiety, and stress, as well as lower self-efficacy."
- **Problem:** Higher baseline sugar consumption and lower self-efficacy are omitted; also "associated with" in the abstract becomes "predicted by".
- **Suggested fix:** "high-burden membership was associated with baseline food-addiction scores, higher baseline sugar intake, higher BMI, craving, anxiety, stress and lower self-efficacy."

**Issue D. NS fit 7 on fully self-reported, uncontrolled data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "NS fit: 7/10"
- **Problem:** Every outcome is self-reported via smartphone, there is no control group and no pre-quit baseline (the digest's own caveats concede this). A 7 overstates the evidential weight on a contested question.
- **Suggested fix:** "NS fit: 6/10 — novel real-time data on a contested question, but self-reported, uncontrolled and without a pre-quit baseline."

### Study 3: Brains of people recovering from heroin use fail to sync up when solving problems together
**PMID:** 41941931 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "First hyperscanning study" claim not made in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Novelty: First-in-class … first hyperscanning study of social cognition in heroin abstainers"
- **Abstract says:** "Despite extensive research on the neurobiology of addiction, little is known about how repeated drug use and withdrawal are related to social functioning impairments in humans."
- **Problem:** The authors claim novelty of insight, not priority. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "an early — possibly the first — hyperscanning study of social cognition in heroin abstainers (priority claim to be confirmed with the authors)."

**Issue B. Participant location stated but not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "participants are abstinent heroin users in one region of China"
- **Abstract says:** Author affiliations are in Xi'an, China; participant recruitment location is not stated.
- **Problem:** Reasonable inference, but presented as fact. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "participants were recruited by a Chinese research group (recruitment site not specified in the abstract)."

### Study 4: Four out of five trading card packs don't tell buyers their odds of a rare card
**PMID:** 42611551 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Significant-adjacent (Moderate)

**Issue A. "Any individual card" reverses the scope of the finding** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Not one pack let a buyer work out their odds of any individual card."
- **Abstract says:** "No pack (0.0%) allowed the consumer to know their odds of getting every individual card."
- **Problem:** "Every" and "any" are not interchangeable here. The study found no pack gave complete card-by-card odds; the digest's wording claims no pack gave odds for even a single card, which is contradicted by the 20% that provided (incomplete) disclosures.
- **Suggested fix:** "No pack gave complete odds — none let a buyer work out their chance of getting every individual card."

### Study 5: Teenage gambling is falling overall — but rising among gamers and esports fans
**PMID:** 42599567 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Tripled" understates the reported odds ratio** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Being male tripled the odds of gambling (OR 3.71)"
- **Abstract says:** "males compared to females (OR 3.71, 95%CI 3.39-4.06)"
- **Problem:** 3.71 is closer to quadruple than triple; "tripled" also reads as causal for a cross-sectional association.
- **Suggested fix:** "Boys had nearly four times the odds of gambling as girls (OR 3.71, 95% CI 3.39–4.06)."

**Issue B. "Rather than traditional gambling routes" is an addition** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "among girls, a new higher-risk profile emerged defined by gaming, esports and internet gaming disorder rather than traditional gambling routes"
- **Abstract says:** "In female adolescents, the new profile with a higher likelihood of gambling includes videogame players, esport spectator and/or player, and presence of internet gaming disorders."
- **Problem:** The abstract describes an additional profile; it does not report that traditional routes have been displaced in girls.
- **Suggested fix:** "…a newly identified higher-risk profile among girls: video game players, esports spectators and players, and those with internet gaming disorder."

### Study 6: Short videos cut depression in older adults through one route and raise it through another
**PMID:** 42374855 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Moderate issue

**Issue A. Causal headline on a cross-sectional SEM** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Short videos cut depression in older adults through one route and raise it through another"
- **Abstract says:** "Short video use was associated with fewer depressive symptoms via more positive attitudes toward aging, but also with increased symptoms via Internet addiction."
- **Problem:** "Cut" and "raise" assert causation from a single-wave national survey with modelled pathways. The digest's own caveat concedes "the direction of every arrow is assumed" — the headline contradicts it.
- **Suggested fix:** "Short video use is linked to less depression in older adults by one route — and more by another."

### Study 7: Thirteen years of forced addiction treatment: most people relapse, 15% are dead within six months
**PMID:** 42017394 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Moderate issue

**Issue A. "Most people relapse" is not supported by 48%** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "most people relapse, 15% are dead within six months"
- **Abstract says:** "Six months after the end of treatment, 15% were deceased and 48% of patients had returned to their previous level of substance use. 27.6% were either using less or abstinent at 6 months."
- **Problem:** 48% is not "most". The headline claims a majority relapsed when the study reports just under half returning to previous levels, with a further quarter improved and 15% deceased.
- **Suggested fix:** "Thirteen years of involuntary addiction treatment: nearly half return to previous use, 15% are dead within six months."

**Issue B. "The" vs "a" substance of concern** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Alcohol was the substance of concern for 91%"
- **Abstract says:** "Alcohol was a substance of concern for 91%."
- **Problem:** The abstract allows for polysubstance presentations; "the" implies alcohol was the sole concern.
- **Suggested fix:** "Alcohol was a substance of concern for 91%."

### Study 8: Nearly one in five students shows "maladaptive daydreaming" — and it tracks with ADHD and autism traits
**PMID:** 42102505 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. N (848), mean age (21.50), prevalence (18.3% at MDS-16 ≥50), the strongest regression predictors, the secondary contributors and the inverse associations with age and year of study all match. Framing is correlational throughout and the caveats (self-report, 73% female, cut-off dependence) match the abstract. NS fit of 6 is defensible.

### Study 9: Young people rate nicotine pouches more favourably than vapes or cigarettes
**PMID:** 42581660 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issue

**Issue A. Market claim not supported by the study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Nicotine pouches are the fastest-moving new nicotine product in Europe"
- **Abstract says:** "Youth vaping and nicotine pouch use have risen as cigarette smoking declines, yet comparative evidence on their determinants remains limited."
- **Problem:** The "fastest-moving in Europe" claim comes from outside the paper and needs an independent source before it goes in a pitch. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Nicotine pouch use is rising among young people as smoking declines [source needed], and this suggests prevention aimed at facts and consequences targets the wrong kind of attitude."

Otherwise all figures (N=1,194; ages 15–25; health literacy but not SES predicting all three; affective attitudes strongest; instrumental attitudes null; friend norms > family norms) match the abstract exactly.

### Study 10: Trying harder to self-regulate makes short-video addiction worse — for some people
**PMID:** 42374874 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Moderate issue

**Issue A. Causal headline contradicts the body's own hedge** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Trying harder to self-regulate makes short-video addiction worse — for some people"
- **Abstract says:** "For individuals with low inhibitory control, greater use of self-regulatory strategies was associated with higher addiction severity, suggesting a conditional risk pattern consistent with a possible 'backfire effect.'"
- **Problem:** "Makes … worse" asserts causation from cross-sectional moderated mediation. The digest's pitch instructs "be explicit that this is correlational" — the headline does the opposite, and the caveat notes reverse causation is equally consistent.
- **Suggested fix:** "In people with low inhibitory control, more self-regulation strategies go with worse short-video addiction, not better."

**Issue B. Focus groups described as a separate stage** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "focus groups with 169 addicted and non-addicted short-video users to define constructs, then a survey of 1,274 people"
- **Abstract says:** "...core affective and cognitive variables first defined through focus groups with a strategically selected subsample (N = 169) of addicted and nonaddicted users."
- **Problem:** The 169 were a subsample of the survey participants, not an independent prior sample.
- **Suggested fix:** "a survey of 1,274 people, with constructs first defined through focus groups with a strategically selected subsample of 169 of them."

### Study 11: Most teenagers who self-harm stop within two years — and for boys, cutting internet use tracks with stopping
**PMID:** 42633531 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. Baseline N (652), follow-up N and rate (585, 89.7%), NSSI prevalence change (22.05%→8.55%), remission rate (82.17%), aOR 8.43 for reduced depressive symptoms, aOR 8.87 for reduced internet addiction in boys, and the girls' depression/anxiety pathways all match. The headline correctly uses "tracks with" rather than causal language, and the caveats on wide CIs and reverse causation are appropriate. NS fit of 5 is defensible.

### Study 12: Women who smoke are twice as likely to develop postpartum depression
**PMID:** 42604887 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline states an observational association as a fact about risk** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Women who smoke are twice as likely to develop postpartum depression"
- **Abstract says:** "Meta-analytical assessment of systematically collected data from 38 studies indicated that smoking women are twice as likely to develop PPD, compared to their non-smoking counterparts… although this association involve mediation of additional environmental and psychosocial elements."
- **Problem:** The headline mirrors the abstract's phrasing, but strips the authors' own acknowledgement of confounding. On a topic where readers will hear "smoking causes PPD", a hedge is worth carrying into the headline.
- **Suggested fix:** "Smoking is linked to double the rate of postpartum depression across 38 studies."

**Issue B. "New mechanism" novelty tag for an in silico analysis** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Novelty: New mechanism"
- **Abstract says:** The gene-level work is "in silico analyses" of "common protein-encoding genes… predicted to alter multiple pathophysiological pathways."
- **Problem:** Nothing mechanistic was demonstrated; hub genes were computationally predicted. The digest's caveats say as much, but the novelty label oversells it.
- **Suggested fix:** "Novelty: Consolidates a contested association (mechanistic component is computational only)."

### Study 13: Severe social withdrawal explains the link between autistic traits and problem gaming
**PMID:** 41742036 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Moderate issue

**Issue A. Mediation extended to outcomes it wasn't tested on** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "mediation analysis found that withdrawal partly explained the route from autistic traits to gaming addiction, hours played and gaming-related academic problems"
- **Abstract says:** "AdAS Spectrum and HQ-25 total scores… emerged as significant positive predictors of AICA-S total score, of a greater number of hours spent playing video games and of the presence of school-related issues… A mediation analysis demonstrated significant direct and indirect effect through the HQ-25, of the AdAS Spectrum total score on the AICA-S total score."
- **Problem:** The mediation was tested only on the AICA-S total score. Hours played and school-related issues come from a separate regression in which both constructs were predictors — not from the mediation model.
- **Suggested fix:** "Mediation analysis found withdrawal partly explained the route from autistic traits to gaming-addiction scores; both autistic traits and withdrawal separately predicted hours played and gaming-related school problems."

**Issue B. Headline "explains" is stronger than partial mediation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Severe social withdrawal explains the link between autistic traits and problem gaming"
- **Abstract says:** "significant direct and indirect effect through the HQ-25" — i.e. partial, not full, mediation, in cross-sectional data.
- **Problem:** "Explains" implies the link is accounted for; a direct effect remained.
- **Suggested fix:** "Social withdrawal partly accounts for the link between autistic traits and problem gaming."

### Study 14: People with cocaine use disorder have two separate kinds of impulsivity — with different brain wiring
**PMID:** 42562118 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Overturns prior research" is the wrong novelty label** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Novelty: Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** "However, accumulating evidence suggests that disinhibition comprises multiple partially dissociable dimensions. It remains unclear how these dimensions manifest in CUD."
- **Problem:** The multidimensional view is already established in the literature; this study extends it to CUD and to structural connectomes. It does not overturn anything.
- **Suggested fix:** "Novelty: Extends an existing dimensional framework to cocaine use disorder."

**Issue B. "Two separate kinds" overstates "partially dissociable"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** Headline: "have two separate kinds of impulsivity"
- **Abstract says:** "two partially dissociable dimensions… partially overlapping but largely separable structural connectome architectures."
- **Problem:** The body text correctly says "partially separable"; the headline hardens it to fully separate.
- **Suggested fix:** "Cocaine use disorder involves two largely separable kinds of impulsivity — with different brain wiring."

### Study 15: Anxious ultramarathon runners get injured more — and for women, a lot more
**PMID:** 42628162 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Strongest predictor wasn't weekly mileage" is not established** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the strongest predictor in the data wasn't weekly mileage, it was anxiety"
- **Abstract says:** Reports only that "Age, BMI, and generalized anxiety disorder were significantly associated with injury risk"; training parameters were collected but no result for weekly mileage is given.
- **Problem:** The absence of training variables from the reported results is not the same as a demonstrated null, and no head-to-head comparison of effect strength is reported. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "the variables that came out significant were BMI, age and anxiety — training volume did not feature in the reported results."

**Issue B. Causal headline on a retrospective self-report survey** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Anxious ultramarathon runners get injured more"
- **Abstract says:** Cross-sectional web survey; "self-reported injury history"; GAD "associated with injury".
- **Problem:** "Get injured more" implies a prospective relationship; injury history was recalled at the same moment anxiety was measured, and the digest's own caveat raises recall bias.
- **Suggested fix:** "Ultramarathon runners with anxiety report more injuries — and for women, far more."

### Study 16: Exercise addiction and eating disorders look similar but come from different emotional places
**PMID:** 42504448 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issue

**Issue A. "Come from" implies origin from cross-sectional correlations** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Exercise addiction and eating disorders look similar but come from different emotional places"
- **Abstract says:** "The findings provide preliminary support for partially differentiated psychological patterns… These cross-sectional findings should be interpreted cautiously and require replication in longitudinal and clinical samples."
- **Problem:** "Come from" asserts an origin story the design cannot support; the authors explicitly label the result preliminary and partial. The body text ("partly different profiles") is fine.
- **Suggested fix:** "Exercise addiction and eating disorders look similar but map onto different emotional patterns."

### Study 17: "Love addiction" is linked to distress and impulsivity — but only through a loss of emotional control
**PMID:** 42533520 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Moderate issue

**Issue A. Gender invariance misread as evidence against a gender stereotype** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the gender invariance undercuts the stereotype that compulsive romantic attachment is a distinctively female pattern"
- **Abstract says:** "multigroup analysis confirmed that the structural model was strictly invariant across genders."
- **Problem:** Structural invariance means the *pathways between constructs* operate the same way in men and women. It says nothing about whether men and women report equivalent *levels* of love addiction — which is what the stereotype is about. The digest's pitch angle repeats the misreading ("the same model fits men and women identically" is fine; the stereotype claim is not).
- **Suggested fix:** "the model works identically in men and women, meaning the mechanism is not gender-specific — though the study does not report whether levels of love addiction differ by gender."

**Issue B. Sample nationality asserted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Spanish sample skewed female and young"
- **Abstract says:** Authors are based at Cadiz and Seville; participant nationality is not stated.
- **Problem:** Reasonable inference, but not in the abstract. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Sample skewed female and young; recruitment country not specified in the abstract (authors are Spain-based)."

### Study 18: Employees who use generative AI think more creatively — until tolerance sets in
**PMID:** 42364395 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Moderate issue

**Issue A. Headline implies a temporal progression not tested** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Employees who use generative AI think more creatively — until tolerance sets in"
- **Abstract says:** "A cross-sectional questionnaire survey was conducted among 194 knowledge workers… GAI use is positively associated with employee creative thinking… the tolerance dimension of GAI addiction is negatively associated with creative thinking."
- **Problem:** "Until tolerance sets in" describes a within-person trajectory over time. This is a single-timepoint survey of 194 people; two opposing cross-sectional associations were found simultaneously, not sequentially. The "Why it matters" section is appropriately hedged, so the headline is the outlier.
- **Suggested fix:** "Generative AI use goes with more creative thinking — but signs of tolerance go with less."

Other figures (N=194, mediation via creative self-efficacy, null withdrawal pathway, self-efficacy negatively predicting both addiction dimensions, satisfaction as moderator of the use→self-efficacy path) all check out.

### Study 19: Across 15 countries, conflict with others is the hub connecting internet addiction to teen mental health
**PMID:** 42612478 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Moderate issue

**Issue A. Central node conflated with the connecting bridge** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** Headline: "conflict with others is the hub connecting internet addiction to teen mental health"; and "converges on interpersonal conflict rather than time online as the connective tissue"
- **Abstract says:** "the domain-level analysis identified conflict as the most central domain… Internet-related withdrawal formed the strongest cross-domain associations with psychological problems."
- **Problem:** Conflict was the most central node *within* the network; the strongest *bridge* between internet addiction and psychological problems was withdrawal. The headline and "Why it matters" assign the bridging role to conflict, which the study attributes to withdrawal. The digest's own body text gets this right, so the framing contradicts itself.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Across 15 countries, conflict is the most central symptom of compulsive internet use in young people." Why it matters: "…converges on interpersonal symptoms rather than time online, with withdrawal forming the strongest link to psychological distress."

**Issue B. "Teen" for a 15–24 sample** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "teen mental health"
- **Abstract says:** "6204 participants aged 15-24 years."
- **Problem:** Over half the age band are young adults, not teenagers.
- **Suggested fix:** "young people's mental health" (as the body text correctly says).
