### 1. Stopping antipsychotics does not trigger a distinct kind of rapid relapse, trial data suggest

**Journal:** *The Lancet Psychiatry* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 24 August 2026)
**PMID:** 42636852 | **DOI:** 10.1016/S2215-0366(26)00225-7
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 8/10 — individual-participant data from five double-blind trials speaking directly to one of psychiatry's hottest arguments; limited only by it being a single drug (paliperidone).
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers pooled individual participant data from five randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled discontinuation trials of paliperidone held in the Yale Open Data Access database — 271 people on long-acting injections and 146 on tablets, aged 18 to 65 — and modeled the symptom trajectories of everyone whose illness relapsed. Two patterns emerged: rapid-onset and delayed-onset relapse. Crucially, rapid relapse was no more common in people switched to placebo than in those who stayed on the drug, and symptom profiles at relapse were indistinguishable between the two groups. What did predict rapid relapse was how severe symptoms were at baseline.

**Why it matters:** A widely discussed idea holds that abruptly stopping antipsychotics produces a distinct, pharmacologically driven "withdrawal psychosis" — rebound from a brain adapted to dopamine blockade. These data do not fit that prediction, and instead point to pre-existing severity as the marker of who crashes fast when medication is withdrawn.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Millions of people take antipsychotics long-term and many want to stop; the fear that stopping itself makes you ill has shaped that conversation. Open on the clinical dilemma of a stable patient asking to come off their injection, then unpack what pooled trial data actually show about who relapses quickly and why. Call Robert McCutcheon at Oxford — and get a counterpoint from researchers in the antipsychotic-tapering community, who will contest the interpretation. Frame it as risk stratification during deprescribing, not as advice to stop or continue.
- **Wider angle:** A longer piece on the politics of psychiatric drug withdrawal — how "discontinuation effects" became a battleground across antidepressants, benzodiazepines and antipsychotics, and how open trial-data repositories are being used to reopen settled arguments. Fits a features desk at a general-interest outlet or a health-policy magazine.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of trials of a single antipsychotic molecule; not preregistered; trial populations are pre-selected for stability and may not resemble people tapering in routine care; authors note possible measurement artifact, with symptoms minimized at screening to meet entry thresholds. Several authors declare extensive pharmaceutical consultancy and speaker fees, including from manufacturers of antipsychotics; funding from NIHR Oxford BRC and the Wellcome Trust.

### 2. The biggest study yet of a genetic syndrome linked to psychosis finds an uneven profile of thinking skills

**Journal:** *Psychological Medicine* | **Published:** 21 September 2026
**PMID:** 42765380 | **DOI:** 10.1017/S0033291726105224
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — 1328 participants makes this definitive for a rare condition, and the "scores fall with age" finding is genuinely arresting; limited by IQ-profile analyses being intrinsically dry.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** An international consortium assembled cognitive data on 1328 people with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome — a chromosomal deletion that carries perhaps the strongest known single genetic risk for schizophrenia — and mapped their profile across verbal IQ, performance IQ, processing speed and working memory. The ranking was consistent: processing speed strongest, performance IQ weakest. The same profile appeared at every age, but absolute scores were highest in children, lower in adolescents and lowest in adults. People who had developed a schizophrenia spectrum disorder scored lower overall, with no domain-specific signature.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that in a population at very high genetic risk of psychosis, cognitive scores drift downward relative to peers across development regardless of whether psychosis appears — and that the domains most obviously tested in conversation (verbal skills) can mask weaker practical, hands-on ability, leading families and clinicians to overestimate what someone can manage.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the mismatch — a young person with 22q11.2 deletion who talks fluently and is assumed to cope, but whose non-verbal reasoning lags badly behind. Then the bigger finding: the gap with typical peers widens with age even in those who never develop psychosis, which is why one-off childhood assessments mislead. Call Ania Fiksinski at UMC Utrecht or Jacob Vorstman at SickKids in Toronto. Be careful not to present cross-sectional age-group differences as proven decline in individuals.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on 22q11.2 deletion syndrome as psychiatry's "model organism" in humans — the one place where a known genetic cause of schizophrenia can be followed prospectively from birth, and what two decades of international consortium work has and has not delivered. Good fit for a specialist genetics or medicine outlet.

**Caveats:** Observational; age comparisons are mostly cross-sectional (only 37% contributed longitudinal data), so apparent decline with age could reflect cohort and ascertainment differences; data pooled across many sites and Wechsler versions; clinically ascertained samples may over-represent more severely affected individuals.

### 3. Screening service members as they leave the military flags undetected early psychosis

**Journal:** *Psychiatric Services* | **Published:** 11 September 2026
**PMID:** 42723010 | **DOI:** 10.1176/appi.ps.20260088
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a single added question to more than 5000 people uncovering hidden risk is a clean, concrete story; limited by cross-sectional design and low follow-up uptake.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers added one question about perceptual disturbances to a standard program for US service members leaving active duty and screened 5019 people, who are typically in the exact age window when psychotic disorders emerge. Ten percent screened positive. Those who did were roughly three times more likely to report lifetime suicidal ideation, more than four times more likely to report a past suicide attempt, and about three times more likely to report depression and anxiety. Of 66 who completed a follow-up structured interview, 12 met criteria for attenuated psychosis syndrome and five for full threshold psychosis — none previously identified.

**Why it matters:** Psychosis is routinely diagnosed years after onset, and this shows that a single extra question at a moment when a whole population is already being assessed can surface cases that the system otherwise misses entirely.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the transition itself — the medical out-processing appointment where a 24-year-old answers one unfamiliar question about hearing or seeing things others don't, and ends up in a conversation nobody had had with them before. The surprise is how much a one-item screen picked up, and how strongly a positive answer tracked suicide risk. Call Elizabeth Nelson or Cheryl Corcoran at the VISN 2 MIRECC in New York. Do not imply screening has been shown to improve outcomes — feasibility is what was demonstrated.
- **Wider angle:** The broader debate about population screening for psychosis risk: false positives, the ethics of telling a young person they may be at risk, and whether "attenuated psychosis syndrome" is a useful category at all. Suits a health-policy or bioethics outlet, or a longer piece for a psychiatry-focused publication.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional with no outcome follow-up; only 55.8% of those contacted initiated the interview, so diagnostic rates among screen-positives are likely biased; a self-reported single-item screen; military leavers are not a general population; conducted at partnered installations only.

### 4. How childhood abuse and neglect may translate into hearing voices later on

**Journal:** *Psychological Medicine* | **Published:** 15 September 2026
**PMID:** 42740395 | **DOI:** 10.1017/S0033291726105492
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a well-known risk factor gets a plausible psychological pathway, tested in patients, their siblings and controls; mediation analysis on cross-sectional data caps the score.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using the Dutch GROUP cohort, researchers tested two candidate routes from childhood trauma to psychotic experiences in 110 people with psychotic disorders, 120 of their unaffected siblings and 48 controls. Negative beliefs about the self fully accounted for the link between childhood abuse and psychotic experiences in patients, while disturbances in the basic sense of self — a fractured feeling of being the author of one's own thoughts — partly accounted for the link with neglect. In siblings, both pathways fully mediated the trauma–psychosis association; in controls, only negative self-beliefs did.

**Why it matters:** It suggests abuse and neglect may reach psychosis by different psychological routes, which matters because negative self-beliefs are directly targetable in therapy while self-disturbance largely is not yet.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the split: being hurt and being ignored appear to leave different psychological fingerprints. Open on the clinical observation that critical, shaming voices so often echo a childhood voice. Then the finding that neglect tracks with something stranger — a loosened grip on the sense of self. Call Lieuwe de Haan or Frederike Schirmbeck at Amsterdam UMC. Frame as pathway hypothesis, not proof of causation.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on the resurgence of phenomenology in psychiatry — the century-old idea of "basic self-disturbance" being revived and measured with questionnaires, and what it means to study the sense of selfhood empirically. Fits a philosophy-of-mind or long-form science outlet.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional mediation cannot establish temporal order or causation; retrospective self-reported childhood trauma; small control group (n=48); psychotic experiences self-reported via CAPE; single cohort in the Netherlands.

### 5. Metformin, but not the standard add-on drug, curbs weight gain in people taking clozapine

**Journal:** *The Canadian Journal of Psychiatry* | **Published:** 15 September 2026
**PMID:** 42742489 | **DOI:** 10.1177/07067437261486956
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — directly useful comparison of two widely used strategies in an under-studied group; retrospective design and N=80 hold it back.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** In a 105-bed residential facility in British Columbia treating people with both psychotic illness and substance use disorders, researchers tracked monthly weights for 80 adults taking clozapine over admissions of at least 90 days, yielding 306 weight measurements matched to blood drug levels. Mixed-effects models showed adjunctive metformin was associated with a flatter weight trajectory over time, while adjunctive aripiprazole — often prescribed partly for this purpose — showed no discernible effect. Higher norclozapine concentrations and opioid agonist therapy were both associated with higher weight.

**Why it matters:** Clozapine is the only drug that reliably works for treatment-resistant schizophrenia, and the weight it puts on is a major reason people stop taking it; this suggests the two common counter-measures are not equivalent in real-world inpatient settings.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is the drug that didn't work. Aripiprazole augmentation is widespread partly on metabolic grounds; here it did nothing while a cheap diabetes drug did. Open on the trade-off patients describe — the medication that quiets the voices and adds 15 kilograms in a year. Call Reza Rafizadeh at UBC or Chad Bousman at Calgary. Emphasize this is a naturalistic cohort, not a randomized comparison, and treatment decisions belong with clinicians.
- **Wider angle:** Repurposed metabolic drugs in psychiatry — metformin, and now the GLP-1 agonists — as the field confronts a 15-to-20-year life expectancy gap in severe mental illness that is driven mostly by cardiometabolic disease, not suicide. Strong feature for a medical or health-systems outlet.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, observational and non-randomized, so prescribing choices likely differed systematically between groups; single center; N=80, predominantly male, with concurrent substance use disorders, limiting generalizability; one author is founder and CEO of Sequence2Script Inc.

### 6. People with schizophrenia look away exactly when their conversation partner smiles

**Journal:** *Journal of Behavior Therapy and Experimental Psychiatry* | **Published:** 2 September 2026
**PMID:** 42702091 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbtep.2026.102134
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a vivid, second-by-second mechanism for something usually described in vague terms; small N and video-coding software limit it.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Twenty conversation partners each held one unscripted conversation with a person with schizophrenia and one with a matched healthy control (20 in each group). Automated facial analysis of the video showed that people with schizophrenia did not avert their gaze more overall — but they did look away significantly more at the specific moments their partner was smiling, and these averted moments accounted for much of their previously documented failure to smile back.

**Why it matters:** Reduced emotional mimicry in schizophrenia is often treated as a deficit in the emotion system itself; this points instead to something upstream and potentially more tractable — the person simply isn't looking when the smile happens.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the moment: your partner smiles, and that is precisely when you glance away, so the automatic smile back never fires — and they read you as cold. Open on a naturalistic lab conversation and what the frame-by-frame video shows. The authors raise stigma as a possible driver of the looking away, which is the sharpest thread to pull. Call Stéphane Raffard at Montpellier or Ludovic Marin at EuroMov.
- **Wider angle:** A piece on automated facial and gaze analysis arriving in clinical research — what it reveals that human raters miss, and the accuracy and consent questions raised by software like OpenFace and FaceReader being trained on patients' faces. Suits a technology or AI-ethics outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (20 per group); partly a reanalysis of previously reported video data; commercial facial-analysis software with known accuracy limits across faces and lighting; single site; correlational — gaze aversion is associated with, not shown to cause, failed mimicry; medication effects not disentangled.

### 7. Gut bacteria look different in people with treatment-resistant schizophrenia on clozapine

**Journal:** *The Canadian Journal of Psychiatry* | **Published:** 18 September 2026
**PMID:** 42758012 | **DOI:** 10.1177/07067437261487782
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — first look at the microbiome in this specific, clinically important group, but a 25-versus-25 pilot that cannot separate drug from disease.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Twenty-five people with treatment-resistant schizophrenia on long-term clozapine were compared with 25 healthy controls carefully matched for age, sex, BMI and smoking status — the last two being unusual and important controls in this field. Fecal 16S rRNA sequencing found reduced microbial diversity in the patient group, less Firmicutes, and more Eggerthella, Mucispirillum and unclassified Clostridia. Predicted functional pathways suggested a metabolically stressed, pro-inflammatory community, with nominal associations to symptom scores and gastrointestinal symptoms.

**Why it matters:** Clozapine's twin burdens — severe constipation and rapid metabolic deterioration — are the main reasons its use is restricted, and the gut microbiome is a plausible but entirely unproven mediator of both. Not much changes yet, but it opens a line of enquiry with a clear next experiment.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the matching: because controls were matched on BMI and smoking, two factors that usually swamp microbiome studies in psychiatry, the differences are harder to dismiss. Open on clozapine's notorious gut side effects, which can be fatal, and ask whether the drug is remodeling the microbiome or the illness is. Call Daniel Müller at CAMH in Toronto. No clinical or dietary implications should be drawn.
- **Wider angle:** A skeptical audit of the psychiatric microbiome field — a decade of small cross-sectional studies, almost no longitudinal work, and the persistent confound that psychiatric medications are themselves potent antimicrobials. Fits a specialist microbiology or research-critique outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (25 per group); cross-sectional, so causation and direction are unknowable; no unmedicated or non-clozapine psychiatric comparison group, so drug and disease effects are fully confounded; 16S sequencing of a single variable region gives coarse taxonomy; functional pathways are predicted, not measured; associations with symptoms were nominal and exploratory.

### 8. Brain "background noise" is altered in schizophrenia and tracks memory for one's own life

**Journal:** *Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology & Biological Psychiatry* | **Published:** 15 September 2026
**PMID:** 42744260 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pnpbp.2026.111910
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — the aperiodic-signal idea is a genuinely fresh way of reading brain recordings, but the sample is modest and the correlations are unadjusted.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Forty people with schizophrenia and 27 healthy controls had resting-state magnetoencephalography recorded, and researchers separated the rhythmic oscillations in the signal from the "aperiodic" broadband background that most studies discard. The patient group showed a steeper aperiodic exponent in visual and parietal regions and weaker alpha peaks. Aperiodic activity in the pericalcarine cortex correlated with positive symptom scores, while reduced alpha power in the posterior cingulate and supramarginal gyrus tracked poorer verbal learning, visual learning, and performance on a test of recalling personal memories.

**Why it matters:** The aperiodic component of brain signals is increasingly thought to index the balance of excitation and inhibition across cortex — a long-standing but hard-to-measure theory of what goes wrong in schizophrenia — and this suggests it is not just statistical noise to be thrown away.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** For decades brain-wave researchers subtracted the background hum and analyzed the peaks; the argument now is that the hum was carrying the signal. Open on the specific human detail — that the flattening of alpha tracked how well people could retrieve memories of their own lives. Call Yuxiu Sui's group at the Affiliated Brain Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, and an independent aperiodic-activity researcher for context.
- **Wider angle:** How a methodological reappraisal — the decision to model the 1/f background of neural recordings — is quietly rewriting findings across ADHD, ageing, anaesthesia and psychosis, and how many published "alpha decreases" may have been aperiodic shifts all along. A good piece for a neuroscience-focused or methods-literate outlet.

**Caveats:** Modest N (40 vs 27) and single center; patients were medicated, and antipsychotics affect spectral measures; symptom correlations are uncorrected for multiple comparisons and modest (r ≈ 0.33–0.35); cross-sectional and observational.

### 9. Voice hearers describe a constant negotiation over who is in charge

**Journal:** *Psychology and Psychotherapy: Theory, Research and Practice* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 7 December 2025)
**PMID:** 41354941 | **DOI:** 10.1111/papt.70026
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — rich first-person material that reframes voice hearing as a relationship rather than a symptom; six participants means it can only ever be exploratory.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Six people who hear voices gave in-depth phenomenological interviews, analyzed using interpretative phenomenological analysis. Rather than describing a simple loss of control, participants described autonomy as something continuously negotiated: their sense of self and space for self-authorship was challenged by the voices, but power was often shared and bargained with them, and the capacity to build a personally meaningful narrative was what most restored a feeling of self-determination.

**Why it matters:** It supports a shift already under way in psychological therapies for psychosis — treating voices as entities to be related to differently rather than purely as symptoms to be suppressed — and gives that approach first-person texture.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on one participant's account of negotiating terms with a voice. The counterintuitive part for most readers is that the voices are not simply tyrants, and that the route back to feeling in charge of one's own life ran through making sense of the experience rather than eliminating it. Eleanor Longden, at the Psychosis Research Unit in Manchester and herself a voice hearer, is the obvious call and an exceptional interviewee.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on the Hearing Voices Movement four decades on — a service-user-led challenge to psychiatric orthodoxy that has now been partly absorbed into mainstream therapy, and where the friction still lies. Strong fit for a long-form general-interest magazine.

**Caveats:** Very small qualitative sample (n=6); no control group and no quantitative outcomes; findings are interpretative and not designed to generalize; participants likely self-selected and may be unusually reflective about their experiences.

### 10. Computational models of memory turn out to be as reliable as the simple scores they replace

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychopathology and Clinical Science* | **Published:** 21 September 2026
**PMID:** 42765980 | **DOI:** 10.1037/abn0001164
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — an important psychometric reassurance for a fast-growing field, but the topic is inherently technical and hard to make personal.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers tested 105 healthy controls and 216 people with schizophrenia spectrum disorder, bipolar disorder or major depression on working memory and episodic memory tasks, twice at least 28 days apart. They compared old-fashioned summary scores with parameters from a computational model that separates memory capacity, memory precision and the rate of attention lapses. The computational parameters generally showed good-to-excellent split-half and test-retest reliability above 0.80 — comparable to, and in one case better than, the simple metric.

**Why it matters:** Cognitive tasks have a well-documented reliability problem that has undermined attempts to link brain measures to individual differences; this suggests that decomposing behavior into model parameters need not make things worse, and can sharpen them.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The backdrop is a quiet crisis — beloved cognitive tasks that work beautifully at group level turn out to be near-useless for measuring any one person, which sinks the search for psychiatric biomarkers. Here, adding a layer of modeling made the measurements more useful, not less. Call Steven Luck at UC Davis or Deanna Barch at Washington University in St. Louis. Frame it as an infrastructure story for precision psychiatry.
- **Wider angle:** The reliability paradox in psychology and neuroscience — why tasks optimized for detecting robust average effects are precisely the wrong tools for individual differences, and what that means for everything from brain-imaging biomarkers to cognitive testing apps. Fits a research-methods or meta-science outlet.

**Caveats:** Not a clinical outcome study — this is a psychometric evaluation, with no direct bearing on diagnosis or treatment; sample sizes per diagnostic group are modest (68–76); participants able to complete two lab sessions a month apart may be a healthier subset; findings apply to specific memory paradigms and one class of model.

### 11. An EEG marker touted to predict who responds to clozapine does not deliver

**Journal:** *Clinical EEG and Neuroscience* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 22 October 2025)
**PMID:** 41124326 | **DOI:** 10.1177/15500594251389251
**Novelty:** Failed replication
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a useful negative result against biomarker hype, but a small single-center sample and a modest-profile journal.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Thirty-nine clozapine-naive patients with treatment-resistant schizophrenia at a tertiary hospital in North India were assessed with symptom scales and quantitative EEG at baseline, three weeks and six weeks; 36 completed. Responders (67%) did show significantly higher right temporal delta power at three and six weeks, along with increases in delta and theta power — but the discriminative performance was weak (area under the curve 0.757 at six weeks) and the authors concluded the changes were insufficient to reliably predict who would respond.

**Why it matters:** Clozapine is delayed for years in many patients partly because nobody can tell in advance who will benefit; this shows the most-discussed EEG candidate is not ready to fill that gap, which is worth saying out loud.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Frame around the honest negative. EEG slowing on clozapine has been floated for years as a response marker; run properly and prospectively it separates groups but cannot call individuals. Open on the clinical delay itself — the typical years-long wait before clozapine is tried. Call Priti Arun at Government Medical College and Hospital, Chandigarh. Avoid any suggestion that EEG should or should not guide prescribing.
- **Wider angle:** Why psychiatry keeps announcing biomarkers that evaporate — the statistical difference between distinguishing groups and predicting individuals, illustrated across EEG, imaging and blood markers. A good explainer for a data-journalism or statistics-minded outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (36 completers); single center; no control group and no placebo comparison; short six-week follow-up; response defined by a 20% BPRS improvement threshold, which is lenient; population drawn from one tertiary hospital in North India and may not generalize.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- **Clozapine's metabolic burden, two runs in a row.** Last run's 95-trial network meta-analysis ranked semaglutide and metformin as the only drugs producing clinically meaningful weight loss on antipsychotics ("Semaglutide beats every other drug tested for antipsychotic weight gain", PMID 42418179), and the probiotic result showed how fast a fashionable alternative evaporates (PMID 42596905). The new digest's "Metformin, but not the standard add-on drug, curbs weight gain in people taking clozapine" (PMID 42742489) lands in exactly that space from a real-world inpatient cohort — and adds the sharper point that adjunctive aripiprazole, widely prescribed on metabolic grounds, did nothing.
- **Voice hearers want a workable account of their experience.** "People who see hallucinations face a choice between explanations that convince and ones they can live with" (PMID 42627099) and the new "Voice hearers describe a constant negotiation over who is in charge" (PMID 41354941) — both in *Psychology and Psychotherapy* — converge on narrative and meaning, not symptom suppression, as what restores a sense of agency.
- **Biomarker deflation continues.** Last run's unvalidated striatal connectivity model (PMID 42574776) is now joined by an outright negative: "An EEG marker touted to predict who responds to clozapine does not deliver" (PMID 41124326), where group separation did not translate into individual prediction.

**Advances**
- **Excitation/inhibition balance gets a new measurement route.** The 40 Hz auditory steady-state reversal in clinical high-risk youth (PMID 42274463) argued that the E/I signal runs high before illness consolidates; "Brain 'background noise' is altered in schizophrenia and tracks memory for one's own life" (PMID 42744260) attacks the same theory from the aperiodic component of resting MEG, and links it to autobiographical memory.
- **Automated behavioral sensing moves from what to when.** Acoustic analysis distinguished schizophrenia from schizotypal personality disorder last run (PMID 42685439); "People with schizophrenia look away exactly when their conversation partner smiles" (PMID 42702091) uses automated facial coding to pin a social deficit to a specific second-by-second timing failure rather than a global trait.
- **Prescribing logic under scrutiny from both ends.** Last run covered the cost of delaying clozapine (PMID 42155318); this run covers the other end of the course — "Stopping antipsychotics does not trigger a distinct kind of rapid relapse, trial data suggest" (PMID 42636852) — together sketching an evidence base forming around when to start and when to stop.

**Counterpoints**
- **Childhood adversity and psychosis.** Last run's multiplex-family study found adverse childhood experiences predicted alcohol use disorder but not schizophrenia (PMID 42680113, AUC 0.526). The new "How childhood abuse and neglect may translate into hearing voices later on" (PMID 42740395) assumes and elaborates a trauma–psychosis link, splitting it into abuse via negative self-beliefs and neglect via self-disturbance. Worth reporting as a live disagreement about whether trauma is a risk factor for the diagnosis or a shaper of the experience.
- **Measurement pessimism meets a reassurance.** Where last run's batch documented failures of measurement (experience sampling not reproducing an interview-based trial result, PMID 42658758; poor-performing empathy scales, PMID 42664611; low-sensitivity clinician risk ratings, PMID 42044698), "Computational models of memory turn out to be as reliable as the simple scores they replace" (PMID 42765980) is the rare positive psychometric result in the same debate.

**New themes**
- **Genetic high risk followed across development:** "The biggest study yet of a genetic syndrome linked to psychosis finds an uneven profile of thinking skills" (PMID 42765380) — the first 22q11.2 item in this topic memory.
- **Population-level case finding:** "Screening service members as they leave the military flags undetected early psychosis" (PMID 42723010) introduces psychosis screening feasibility, a new strand alongside the biomarker-prediction work.
- **Microbiome in treatment-resistant illness:** "Gut bacteria look different in people with treatment-resistant schizophrenia on clozapine" (PMID 42758012) extends last run's gut–brain item (PMID 42596905) from intervention to description, with the same confound — the drug is itself antimicrobial.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Five studies in this batch make their advance by re-examining data or signals that already existed rather than collecting something new: pooled individual-participant data from five archived paliperidone trials undercut the idea of a distinct withdrawal psychosis ("Stopping antipsychotics does not trigger a distinct kind of rapid relapse", PMID 42636852); the "aperiodic" background that brain-wave researchers routinely discard turns out to track symptoms and autobiographical memory (PMID 42744260); a reanalysis of existing conversation videos locates failed smiling in gaze timing (PMID 42702091); a prospective test finds the much-discussed EEG predictor of clozapine response cannot call individuals (PMID 41124326); and a psychometric re-evaluation shows computational model parameters are as reliable as the crude scores they replace (PMID 42765980). The through-line is that psychiatry's current gains are coming from the discard pile — archived trials, subtracted signal, unreported reliability.

**Why pitch this now:** Open trial-data repositories such as Yale Open Data Access are being used to reopen arguments the field thought were settled, at the same moment precision psychiatry is discovering that its favorite measures were never reliable enough to describe an individual — two stories usually told separately that are now visibly the same story.

**Angle:** A piece about where new knowledge in psychiatry actually comes from: not new cohorts and bigger scanners, but second looks at data already sitting in repositories, hard drives and video archives, and what that says about a field that has spent two decades announcing biomarkers that do not survive individual-level testing. For readers who follow the replication debate and want to see it play out in a specialty where the stakes are prescriptions rather than priming effects.

**Potential headlines:**
- Psychiatry's most useful new findings are coming from data collected years ago
- The brain signal researchers spent decades throwing away
- Why the tests meant to personalize psychiatric treatment still cannot measure one person
