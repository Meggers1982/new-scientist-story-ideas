### 1. Hot nights are stealing up to 17 minutes of sleep per person — and the poor lose more

**Journal:** *Sleep* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 13
**PMID:** 41081739 | **DOI:** 10.1093/sleep/zsaf323
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 9/10 — Massive global dataset (N=317,758), counterintuitive magnitude of effect, clear climate-change and inequality societal hook, clean longitudinal design with robust statistical controls, and direct relevance to sleep — a core NS Mind topic.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers analysed approximately 165 million nights of sleep tracker data from 317,758 users of Withings devices across the globe, collected between January 2020 and September 2023. Using a case-time-series design, they linked each night's sleep duration to local ambient temperature. At high temperatures (99th versus 50th percentile of the global distribution; 27.3°C vs 12.2°C), participants lost roughly 15–17 minutes of sleep per night and were approximately 40% more likely to sleep fewer than six hours. People in lower-GDP countries and older adults suffered the greatest losses.

**Why it matters:** As climate change pushes average temperatures higher, these findings suggest sleep deprivation could become an amplifier of existing global health inequalities, disproportionately affecting populations least able to afford air conditioning or adapt their environments. The scale of the dataset makes this one of the most robust estimates of temperature's impact on sleep to date.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Climate change is already robbing the world's poorest people of sleep" On the hottest nights, people around the world lose up to 17 minutes of sleep — and those least able to cool their homes lose the most. A study of 317,758 sleep tracker users across the globe found that high ambient temperatures are associated with a 40% increase in the chance of sleeping fewer than six hours in a night. The effect was worst in lower-income countries and among older adults — groups least likely to have air conditioning. As the planet warms, the findings suggest climate change will widen the already-yawning global sleep gap. Why it fits: Sleep, inequality, and climate change are three of the biggest concerns for NS Mind readers, and this study puts hard numbers on their intersection for the first time at global scale.

**Why it matters:** Witness misidentification is a leading cause of wrongful convictions; this study suggests a low-cost courtroom intervention — revised jury instructions — may help jurors give appropriate weight to the most reliable memory evidence, the first identification attempt.

Specially crafted jury instructions that foreground the initial lineup result as the gold-standard test could dramatically shift verdicts. At a time when DNA exonerations keep exposing eyewitness error, this is a story about how a few sentences of legal language might be all that stands between conviction and justice. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are fascinated by the gap between how memory actually works and how the legal system assumes it works — this study sits squarely in that space.
