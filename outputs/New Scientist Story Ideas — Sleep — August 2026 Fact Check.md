### Study 1: Autistic children keep more regular bedtimes than their peers — but get up at wildly different times
**PMID:** 42593049 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

All core numbers check out: 11,462 US children aged 5–17, NHIS 2022 + 2024, aOR 0.64 (bedtime), aOR 1.60 (wake-up), aOR 2.27 in children with no association in adolescents, adjustment set as described. Author and institution correct (Chang C-S, University of Maryland).

**Issue A. "Wildly different" overstates the effect** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "but get up at wildly different times"
- **Abstract says:** "higher odds of irregular wake-up times (aOR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.01–2.56)"
- **Problem:** A 1.6-fold odds ratio with a lower CI bound of 1.01 is a modest, borderline-significant association in a parent-reported binary variable ("irregular" vs not). "Wildly different times" implies a magnitude the data don't quantify.
- **Suggested fix:** "but are more likely to have irregular wake-up times"

**Issue B. NS fit score** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "NS fit: 7/10"
- **Problem:** Every variable in the analysis — sleep timing regularity, ASD diagnosis, fatigue, mental health — is parent-reported, cross-sectional, and both main estimates have confidence intervals abutting 1.0 (0.43–0.95; 1.01–2.56). The nationally representative sampling is the strongest feature, but the finding is fragile.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10, with the fragility of the CIs noted in the score rationale rather than only in Caveats.

### Study 2: Three nights of poor sleep predict next-day mood — and how long you slept doesn't
**PMID:** 42617377 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. N = 136, population (psychiatric inpatients following discharge after recent suicidal crises), EMA design, the three-night cumulative sleep-quality "pile-up" index outperforming raw and variability indices at both within- and between-person levels, and the TST null are all accurately rendered. Framing is correctly associative; the pitch explicitly warns that the outcome is affect and not suicidal behaviour, matching the abstract's "Future studies should explore whether this translates into increased STB risk." German Research Foundation funding (non-commercial) is disclosed in the abstract and carried into Caveats. NS fit of 7/10 is defensible given the intensive-sampling design, though the score note correctly concedes the self-report limitation.

### Study 3: Half of people in a depressive episode also meet criteria for nightmare disorder
**PMID:** 42617369 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Cut-off (NSI ≥10), sensitivity 96.67%, specificity 93.94%, the 49.6% figure, the severity profile, the actigraphy findings and the anxiety mediation are all accurately reported.

**Issue A. Headline generalises a specialist-clinic sample to "people in a depressive episode"** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Half of people in a depressive episode also meet criteria for nightmare disorder"
- **Abstract says:** "This prospective case-control study was conducted in routine clinical settings. Participants were adults diagnosed with MDE... Nearly half (49.6%) of MDE patients met ND criteria."
- **Problem:** Recruitment was through a psychiatry/addictology department and the Centre ChronoS — a chronobiology and sleep centre — so the sample is plausibly enriched for sleep complaints. A prevalence figure from that setting cannot be read as the prevalence among people in a depressive episode generally. The Caveats mention single-centre recruitment, but the headline and the pitch line ("Sell the number — one in two people in a depressive episode") assert the general claim.
- **Suggested fix:** "Half of depressed patients at a Paris sleep-and-psychiatry clinic meet criteria for nightmare disorder" — and in the pitch, "one in two patients in this clinic sample".

**Issue B. Design labelled cross-sectional in the body when the abstract says prospective** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this is cross-sectional and the mediation is statistical"
- **Abstract says:** "This prospective case-control study was conducted in routine clinical settings."
- **Problem:** The caution against causal reading is right, but the design descriptor conflicts with the abstract's own label. The comparison is between-groups at assessment; call it that rather than mislabelling the study.
- **Suggested fix:** "this is a case-control comparison and the mediation is statistical, so direction can't be inferred"

**Issue C. Claim that depression lacks specific behavioural treatments** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "nightmares, unlike depression itself, have specific, well-tested behavioural treatments"
- **Abstract says:** Nothing on treatment for either condition.
- **Problem:** Depression has several specific, well-tested behavioural treatments (behavioural activation, CBT). The contrast as written is inaccurate and not sourced to the study.
- **Suggested fix:** "nightmares have a specific, well-tested behavioural treatment in imagery rehearsal therapy"

**Issue D. NS fit score with an unknown N** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "NS fit: 7/10"
- **Problem:** The Caveats concede that the sample size is not stated in the abstract. A 7 rests on a prevalence figure whose denominator can't be verified, from one centre, with the diagnostic cut-off derived and tested in the same sample.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10 until N is confirmed from the full text.

### Study 4: It's long sleep, not short sleep, that tracks with inflammation in 30,000 US adults
**PMID:** 42546640 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 29,605, NHANES 2005–2020, the six composite indices, the null for sleep trouble overall, the long-sleep (>9 h) associations with NLR/SII/SIRI/PIV, the short-sleep null and the subgroup pattern (BMI ≥25, males, age 40+) are all accurate. Corresponding author and institution correct.

**Issue A. Caveat attributes a specific author acknowledgment that isn't in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "residual confounding by chronic illness is the obvious explanation for the long-sleep finding and is acknowledged by the authors"
- **Abstract says:** "Cross-sectional design and self-reported sleep measures preclude causal inference; residual confounding may remain."
- **Problem:** The authors acknowledge residual confounding in general terms; they do not name chronic illness or apply it specifically to the long-sleep result. Attributing the reverse-causation reading to them overstates the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "the authors acknowledge possible residual confounding; reverse causation via chronic illness is the obvious candidate explanation for the long-sleep finding, though the paper does not test it"

**Issue B. "Overturns prior research" novelty label** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Novelty: Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** Conclusion is framed as an association in high-risk adults, with explicit caution about the sleep-trouble measure.
- **Problem:** A cross-sectional analysis with self-reported, coarsely binned sleep does not overturn anything; it fails to replicate a short-sleep association. The digest's own body text handles this well ("cuts against the narrative"), but the label promises more.
- **Suggested fix:** "Novelty: Contradicts expectation"

### Study 5: Sleep changes forecast later depression — but depression doesn't forecast sleep
**PMID:** 42541934 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 12,747, CHARLS 2011–2020, ≥3 repeated measures, PVAR model, β = −0.064 (sleep → subsequent depressive symptoms) and β = −0.001, 95% CI −0.007 to 0.005 (depression → subsequent sleep) all check out. Corresponding author Yang Y at Zhengzhou University correct. Causal framing is appropriately hedged throughout.

**Issue A. Wave interval not verifiable from the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "sleep duration self-reported at wide intervals (roughly two years)"
- **Abstract says:** "CHARLS, 2011–2020… participants with ≥3 repeated measurements"
- **Problem:** The two-year spacing is correct for CHARLS but is not stated in the abstract. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** Keep, but attribute: "sleep duration self-reported at each CHARLS wave (waves are roughly two years apart)".

**Issue B. "Overturns prior research" novelty label** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Novelty: Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** "The temporal directional relationship between sleep duration and depressive symptoms remains unclear."
- **Problem:** The authors position the field as unresolved, not as holding a bidirectional consensus this study defeats. A single small coefficient in one national panel doesn't overturn the literature.
- **Suggested fix:** "Novelty: Resolves a contested direction" — and soften "the traffic ran one way" to "the traffic ran predominantly one way in this sample".

### Study 6: Genetic analysis suggests PTSD raises the risk of sleep apnoea — but not the other way round
**PMID:** 42611531 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

rg = 0.336 / 0.301, 12 novel pleiotropic SNPs, TRAF3 (PP.H4 = 96.35%) and TMEM106B (PP.H4 = 95.63%), IVW OR = 3.24 (2.42–4.34), null reverse direction, and cerebellar/cortical heritability enrichment are all accurate. Affiliation (Brain and Nerve Research Laboratory, First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University) correct.

**Issue A. "Rather than in metabolic tissue" is not tested in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Heritability was enriched in cerebellum and cortex rather than in metabolic tissue."
- **Abstract says:** "Tissue-specific SNP heritability enrichment was observed in the cerebellum, cerebellar hemisphere, and cortex."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports where enrichment was found; it does not report a negative result for adipose, liver or other metabolic tissue. "Rather than" implies a tested contrast.
- **Suggested fix:** "Heritability was enriched in the cerebellum, cerebellar hemisphere and cortex — brain rather than the metabolic tissues usually invoked, though the abstract does not report which tissues were tested and found null."

**Issue B. Ancestry caveat not verifiable from the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "GWAS samples are overwhelmingly European-ancestry"
- **Abstract says:** No ancestry information given.
- **Problem:** Almost certainly true for these GWAS, but unverifiable from abstract only — confirm from the full text before it goes in a pitch.

### Study 7: Heart-and-metabolic illness makes depression harder to recover from — and poor sleep makes it worse still
**PMID:** 42628578 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Significant framing issue

Cohort sizes (14,468 CHARLS; 8,551 ELSA), the multi-state Markov and Cox approach, HR 1.29 / 1.21 for onset and HR 0.79 / 0.74 for reversion (CMM vs no CMD), and the unhealthy-sleep definition (<6 or ≥9 h) are all accurate.

**Issue A. Causal language throughout for observational cohort data** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Heart-and-metabolic illness **makes** depression harder to recover from — and poor sleep **makes** it worse still"; "Cardiometabolic multimorbidity **raised** the hazard"; "physical multimorbidity **acts as a brake** on recovery"; "**with sleep duration amplifying** the effect"
- **Abstract says:** "CMM **is associated with** higher hazards of DS onset and lower hazards of transitioning from DS to non-DS."
- **Problem:** Four separate causal constructions applied to two observational ageing cohorts with baseline-only exposure measurement. The Caveats correctly note the design, but the headline and body contradict the caveat rather than being softened by it — which is exactly the case where a re-flag is warranted.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Heart-and-metabolic illness is linked to slower recovery from depression — and poor sleep to slower still." Body: "Cardiometabolic multimorbidity was associated with higher hazards of developing depressive symptoms… and lower hazards of recovering from them."

**Issue B. "In both cohorts" for the additive interaction** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Unhealthy sleep duration (<6 or ≥9 hours) showed significant additive interaction with multimorbidity in both cohorts."
- **Abstract says:** "Significant additive interactions between CMM and unhealthy sleep duration were observed (P for additive interaction <0.001)."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not specify that the interaction held in both CHARLS and ELSA separately. Unverifiable from abstract only. (Related: the abstract calls the ELSA sample "8551 UK participants"; the digest says "England", which is right for ELSA but differs from the abstract's wording.)
- **Suggested fix:** "Unhealthy sleep duration (<6 or ≥9 hours) showed a significant additive interaction with multimorbidity (P < 0.001); check the full text for whether this held separately in each cohort."

### Study 8: "You can sleep when you die": how hustle culture rations sleep in women of colour
**PMID:** 42624738 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Eight focus groups, 77 female community health workers, the four community groupings, Utah, grounded theory, and "hustle culture" as the central theme are all accurate, as is the description of trade-offs against caregiving, work and community obligations. The "Why it matters" and the framing note in the pitch ("Frame as a study of why generic sleep advice fails, not as a claim about health outcomes") are appropriately restrained for qualitative work.

**Issue A. Concealment claim is not in the abstract** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Lead with the title quote and the stigma finding — that in several of these communities, sleeping enough is read as laziness, **so people conceal it**."
- **Abstract says:** "Sleep-related stigma and culturally rooted practices provided important context for culturally responsive interventions."
- **Problem:** The abstract names sleep-related stigma but reports neither its content ("sleep as laziness") nor any behavioural consequence such as concealment. Both specifics are presented in the digest as study findings. A writer building a lede on "people hide the fact that they sleep" needs that verified in the full text first.
- **Suggested fix:** "Lead with the title quote and the stigma finding — the paper reports sleep-related stigma as a key context for intervention design; check the full text for how participants described it before building the lede on it."

**Issue B. Author's editorial role at the publishing journal omitted from Caveats** — Severity: Minor

- **Abstract says:** "Given her role as Sleep Health Senior Associate Editor, Kelly Glazer Baron had no involvement in the peer review of this article…"
- **Problem:** Not industry funding, but a disclosed conflict relating to the venue of publication, and the digest recommends Baron as an interviewee. Worth a line in Caveats for transparency.
- **Suggested fix:** Add: "Senior author Kelly Glazer Baron is a Senior Associate Editor at Sleep Health; the journal states she was recused from peer review."

### Study 9: Night and weekend shifts predict which toddlers get a bedtime routine — unless the mother has a strong support network
**PMID:** 42545383 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. N = 1,364 three-year-olds (mean age 35.66 months), racial/ethnic composition (50.04% non-Hispanic Black, 22.26% Hispanic), FFCWS source, and all four directional findings — nonstandard employment predicting lower probability of routines and communication activities; social capital predicting higher probability; externalizing behaviour predicting *more* routines; social capital eliminating the employment-type gap — match the abstract. "Eliminated the gap" is the authors' own word, so it isn't hype here. Framing is associative throughout and the pitch explicitly flags that the externalizing result is plausibly reverse-causal. Description of FFCWS as an older urban US birth cohort is background knowledge rather than abstract content, but it sits correctly in Caveats.

### Study 10: Sleep quality doesn't predict firefighters' long-term resilience — a sense of meaning does
**PMID:** 42495965 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 528 baseline from 11 Dutch regions, follow-up N = 237 (12 months) and N = 203 (24 months), instruments (SOC-R, MLQ, PSQI, VOS-D, CD-RISC), and the nulls for sleep quality, colleague support, age, sex and stressful events are all accurate. The attrition figure (62% lost by 24 months) is correctly calculated. The pitch's warning that this is absence of a predictive association rather than proof of irrelevance is exactly right.

**Issue A. Slight over-tidying of the two-timepoint result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "sense of coherence predicted resilience at 12 months and meaning in life at 24 months"
- **Abstract says:** "sense of coherence at baseline significantly predicted resilience after 12 months. After 24 months, meaning in life significantly predicted resilience **as well**."
- **Problem:** "As well" leaves it ambiguous whether sense of coherence also predicted at 24 months alongside meaning in life. The digest reads it as a clean handover between predictors, which may not be what the paper reports.
- **Suggested fix:** "sense of coherence predicted resilience at 12 months; at 24 months, meaning in life did too (check the full text for whether sense of coherence also held at 24 months)"

### Study 11: A night's sleep doesn't help you forget a made-up word
**PMID:** 42593994 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Significant framing issue

Four experiments, 10–20 min and 12 hr intervals, the 12 hr version run with and without sleep, the null for undegraded words and the durable pseudoword interference are all correctly reported, as is the episodic-familiarity interpretation. Single author and affiliation correct.

**Issue A. The sleep-versus-wake null is the headline but isn't reported in the abstract** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** Headline: "A night's sleep doesn't help you forget a made-up word"; body: "that effect survived the 12-hour delay, **with sleep making no apparent difference**"
- **Abstract says:** "In two other experiments, the interval was 12 hr, either with or without sleep during the 12 hr… This effect was still intact after a 12-hr delay."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that the pseudoword effect persisted across 12 hours; it does not report a statistical comparison between the sleep and wake conditions, or state that they did not differ. The entire pitch hook — and the "gentle corrective to the sleep-and-language-learning literature" framing — rests on a sleep-specific null that is unverifiable from abstract only. If the paper reports a numerically small but non-null sleep difference, the headline collapses.
- **Suggested fix:** Provisional headline: "One hearing of a made-up word still trips you up 12 hours later." Body: "the effect was still intact after 12 hours; the abstract does not report whether the sleep and wake groups differed — confirm from the full text before running the sleep angle."

**Issue B. "Slower and worse" adds an unreported reaction-time detail** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "made people slower and worse at rejecting them as non-words"
- **Abstract says:** "consistently impaired the ability to reject the item as a word"
- **Problem:** The abstract specifies accuracy and RT only for the *word* condition (both null). For pseudowords it reports impairment without breaking out which measure(s).
- **Suggested fix:** "made people worse at rejecting them as non-words"

### Study 12: A blood marker rises in people who respond to sleep deprivation therapy for depression
**PMID:** 41903946 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 46, the two arms (MDD on monoaminergic antidepressants for one month; bipolar disorder on chronotherapeutics — repeated total sleep deprivation plus light therapy — for one week), the association of eNAMPT increase with treatment benefit independent of diagnosis or treatment type, the "not necessary for response" point and the high individual variability are all accurate. Benedetti/San Raffaele correct. The "Why it matters" is appropriately modest ("Not much yet clinically").

**Issue A. Headline narrows a treatment-independent result to the sleep-deprivation arm** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A blood marker rises in people who respond to sleep deprivation therapy for depression"
- **Abstract says:** "The increase in circulating eNAMPT concentration was associated with individual benefit from treatment **independent of diagnosis or type of treatment**."
- **Problem:** The headline implies a sleep-deprivation-specific finding. The abstract reports a pooled association across both arms and does not report the chronotherapeutics arm separately; that arm was also bipolar patients, not major depression.
- **Suggested fix:** "A blood marker tracks recovery from depression — whether the treatment is a drug or a week without sleep"

**Issue B. Open-label / no-placebo caveat not stated in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "open-label with no placebo or sham condition"
- **Abstract says:** Describes only before/after measurement in two treated groups; no control condition is mentioned either way.
- **Problem:** Almost certainly correct given the design, but unverifiable from abstract only. Phrase it as an inference or confirm from the full text.

### Study 13: A dawn-simulating lamp changes how people with intellectual disability wake up
**PMID:** 42613717 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Design (practice-based single-case AB, 2-week care-as-usual baseline, 2-week wake-up light phase), measures (actigraphy plus daily observation for waking state; daily observation plus MIPQ for affect), the finding that all seven completers were more often awake at the start of morning care, and the two-participant affect improvement in exactly those who had previously slept until care began, are all accurate. First author and institution correct. The insistence that this be written as exploratory throughout matches the abstract's own "explorative findings" language, and the 4/10 score is well judged.

**Issue A. Seven described as enrolled rather than completers** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "seven adults with moderate to severe intellectual disability went through a two-week care-as-usual baseline followed by two weeks of a wake-up light"
- **Abstract says:** "all completers (n=7) were more often awake at the start of morning care"
- **Problem:** The abstract only gives the completer count; the number who started may have been higher, and any dropouts matter a great deal at this scale. The Caveats get this right ("N = 7 completers") but the body text implies seven enrolled.
- **Suggested fix:** "seven adults completed a two-week care-as-usual baseline followed by two weeks of a wake-up light (enrolment number not given in the abstract)"

### Study 14: Self-blame and catastrophising are the thinking habits that track both poor sleep and poor mental health
**PMID:** 42587372 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 1,030 young adults, the four instruments (PSQI, CERQ, BDI-II, BAI), Spearman plus SEM, the general association of less adaptive strategies with poorer sleep and more symptoms, and the striking adaptive-strategy pattern (null for sleep and depression, positive correlation with anxiety) are all accurate. Framing is correctly cross-sectional and the reverse-reading of the anxiety correlation is well handled in Caveats. 4/10 is defensible.

**Issue A. Direction of the SEM result is interpreted, not quoted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Structural equation modelling identified catastrophising and self-blame as the two strategies significantly associated with both **worse** sleep quality and **worse** mental health."
- **Abstract says:** "SEM showed that catastrophising and self-blame were significantly **positively** associated with both mental health and sleep quality."
- **Problem:** The abstract's wording is ambiguous — on PSQI, BDI-II and BAI, higher scores mean worse sleep and more symptoms, so the digest's reading is almost certainly right, but it is an inference from scale direction rather than something the abstract states. Worth confirming from the full text so the pitch isn't built on a scoring assumption.
- **Suggested fix:** "…significantly associated with both poorer sleep quality and worse mental health (the abstract phrases this as a positive association with the PSQI, BDI-II and BAI scores, on which higher = worse)."
