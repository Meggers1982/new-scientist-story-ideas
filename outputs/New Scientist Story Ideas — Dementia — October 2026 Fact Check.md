### Study 1: Low-dose lithium is linked to fewer dementia diagnoses than valproate in older adults
**PMID:** 42372694 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

All study facts check out: TriNetX target trial emulation, age ≥60, serum lithium ≤0.40 mmol/L, 23 covariates, 853 per matched cohort, 3.1% vs 8.3%, HR 0.38, lower mortality, null for hospitalization, 730-day primary outcome. Framing is appropriately non-causal and mirrors the authors' own hedging.

**Issue A. NS fit score sits above what the evidence base supports** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — a big effect size on a question readers have heard about for years, but observational and the authors are admirably blunt about bias."
- **Abstract says:** "All-cause dementia occurred in 25/795 (3.1%) lithium versus 69/832 (8.3%) valproate users"; "These observational findings cannot distinguish a direct effect from prescribing or surveillance bias, depletion of susceptibles, or differential healthcare engagement; they are hypothesis-generating"
- **Problem:** The entire headline result rests on 94 dementia events (25 of them in the lithium arm) in an uncontrolled retrospective EHR cohort whose authors explicitly label it hypothesis-generating. A 7 implies a stronger evidence base than that; the digest's own caveat line concedes the point.
- **Suggested fix:** "**NS fit:** 6/10 — a big effect size on a question readers have heard about for years, but observational, built on only 94 dementia events, and the authors are admirably blunt about bias."

*Note:* "a long-standing Alzheimer's pathology skeptic" (describing co-author George Perry) is unverifiable from abstract only — it is a characterization of a person, not a study claim, so it is not scored as an issue, but check it before the pitch goes out.

### Study 2: People with bipolar disorder treated with lithium develop dementia less often than those on valproate
**PMID:** 42349060 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Numbers are accurate: 3056 per matched group, 7.1% vs 10.8%, HR 0.689, lower unspecified dementia and suicide attempt, nulls for vascular dementia, mortality and ESRD.

**Issue A. "Novelty" label not supported by the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** "few studies have directly compared subsequent dementia risk between BD patients treated with lithium and valproate, the two first-line mood stabilizers."
- **Problem:** The authors position this as filling an evidence gap, not as contradicting an established finding. Nothing in the abstract identifies prior research being overturned.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** First direct head-to-head comparison"

**Issue B. Wider angle edges into a prescribing recommendation from observational data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Between fetal-exposure restrictions and now cognitive-outcome signals in older adults, is there any population left for whom valproate is the obvious first choice?"
- **Abstract says:** "though observational data preclude causality inferences."
- **Problem:** Framed as a question, but it invites a first-line prescribing conclusion from a propensity-matched claims analysis with acknowledged confounding by indication. Note also that the authors themselves overreach here ("These findings support lithium as a preferred long-term mood stabilizer"), which is worth flagging to the writer rather than inheriting.
- **Suggested fix:** "...a piece on how much weight prescribers should put on observational cognitive-outcome signals when the authors themselves concede causality cannot be inferred."

### Study 3: Depression predicts dementia better than insomnia does — and adding poor sleep tells you almost nothing extra
**PMID:** 42144138 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 6226, 13 rounds of NHATS (2011–2023), HRs 1.37 / 1.25 / 0.94 and the age-75 reversal are all reported correctly.

**Issue A. Framing drops half of the authors' own conclusion** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Insomnia has become the headline risk factor in public conversation about brain health, yet here the sleep component adds essentially nothing once mood is accounted for."
- **Abstract says:** "Findings support joint assessment of mood and sleep symptoms to identify older adults at elevated dementia risk and suggest that depressive symptoms may be more informative than insomnia symptoms for risk stratification in later life, with heterogeneity by age."
- **Problem:** The digest reports only the second half of the conclusion and builds the headline and "Why it matters" around deflating sleep. The authors explicitly endorse *joint* assessment; the co-occurring group still carried a higher hazard than neither-symptom (HR 1.37) and than insomnia-only (HR 1.25). An editor reading this would think the paper says sleep is irrelevant, which it does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Insomnia has become the headline risk factor in public conversation about brain health, yet here co-occurring symptoms were statistically indistinguishable from depression alone — the authors still recommend assessing mood and sleep together, but conclude depressive symptoms are the more informative of the two for risk stratification."

**Issue B. NS fit score with wholly self-reported exposures** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — nationally representative, 12 years of follow-up, and it deflates a popular 'bad sleep causes dementia' narrative; self-reported symptoms limit it."
- **Abstract says:** "Depressive symptoms and insomnia symptoms were self-reported"; "Incident dementia was determined using self/proxy reports, cognitive testing, and the AD8."
- **Problem:** Both exposure and (partly) outcome are self- or proxy-reported, and the "deflation" framing is the part of the conclusion the authors did not actually draw (see Issue A). A 7 is one point generous.
- **Suggested fix:** "**NS fit:** 6/10 — nationally representative with 12 years of follow-up, but exposures are self-reported and dementia is partly proxy-ascertained."

### Study 4: Autistic adults report far worse memory and thinking than tests can detect
**PMID:** 42815817 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The 15 cross-sectional studies, 13 constructs, d = 0.90 subjective effect, and d = −0.28 to −0.38 for processing speed, verbal fluency and working memory are all reproduced correctly, as is the "first synthesis" claim and the under-65 and heterogeneity limitations.

**Issue A. NS fit score rests on a self-report effect in cross-sectional source studies** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — the first synthesis of cognitive aging in autistic adults, with a striking subjective–objective gap; weakened by cross-sectional source studies."
- **Abstract says:** "Fifteen cross-sectional studies were included"; "interpretation is limited by underrepresentation of participants over 65, and the absence of longitudinal studies"; "Heterogeneity was high across several analyses."
- **Problem:** The headline number (d = 0.90) is pooled self-report from cross-sectional studies, many of which used trait questionnaires rather than diagnosis, with high heterogeneity and no longitudinal data. The digest's caveats are honest, but the score doesn't reflect them.
- **Suggested fix:** "**NS fit:** 6/10 — the first synthesis of cognitive ageing in autistic adults, with a striking subjective–objective gap; the headline effect is pooled self-report from cross-sectional studies with high heterogeneity."

### Study 5: Loneliness after a stroke is linked to a sharply higher risk of later dementia
**PMID:** 42119905 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 394,308, 7267 with prevalent stroke, median 10.2 years, loneliness–dementia HR 1.67, 95.4% White: all correct.

**Issue A. Interaction significance overstated, and applied to the wrong outcome** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The excess risks were significantly larger in stroke survivors than in people without stroke, and health behaviors mediated 12–55% of the associations."
- **Abstract says:** "These associations—particularly for all-cause mortality (Pinteraction = 0.006) and CVD mortality (Pinteraction = 0.042)—were more pronounced in stroke survivors than in those without stroke."
- **Problem:** Only two interaction p-values are reported, both for mortality outcomes. The abstract gives no evidence that the *dementia* association — the subject of the headline and the whole pitch — was significantly amplified by stroke. As written, a reader assumes the stroke-specific dementia amplification was demonstrated.
- **Suggested fix:** "The stroke-versus-no-stroke difference reached statistical significance for all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality; no interaction test is reported for dementia."

**Issue B. Mediation percentages merged across two different exposures** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "health behaviors mediated 12–55% of the associations"
- **Abstract says:** "Health behaviours mediated a substantial proportion of the associations for social isolation (32-55%) and loneliness (12-50%) with most outcomes."
- **Problem:** The digest fuses two separate ranges into one, producing a figure (12–55%) that appears in the abstract for neither exposure.
- **Suggested fix:** "health behaviours mediated 32–55% of the social-isolation associations and 12–50% of the loneliness associations"

**Issue C. Isolation's association with depression dropped** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "isolation instead predicted mortality and cardiovascular death"
- **Abstract says:** "social isolation was independently associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.41), CVD mortality (HR: 1.57), and depression (HR: 1.35)."
- **Problem:** Omitting depression sharpens the "isolation is about the body, loneliness is about the brain" contrast the pitch leans on. Both exposures were associated with depression.
- **Suggested fix:** "isolation instead predicted mortality, cardiovascular death and depression"

### Study 6: People with dementia who have sleep problems are at higher risk of suicide — and depression doesn't explain it
**PMID:** 42790647 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

85,920 / 19,982 / 2325 events, aHR 1.12, 868.3 vs 729.7 per 100,000 person-years, and the non-significant indirect effect through depression are all accurate.

**Issue A. Emergency department figure loses its denominator** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the three-visits-to-the-emergency-department signal (aHR 2.54) that is hiding in the same dataset"
- **Abstract says:** "frequent emergency department visits were additional predictors (≥3 visits/year: aHR, 2.54; 95% CI, 2.20-2.94)"
- **Problem:** The threshold is three or more visits *per year*, not three visits in total. A reporter writing from the digest alone would get this wrong.
- **Suggested fix:** "the three-or-more-emergency-department-visits-a-year signal (aHR 2.54)"

**Issue B. Named mechanisms are the digest's, not the paper's** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "pointing at mechanisms such as impulsivity or circadian disruption"
- **Abstract says:** "This association persisted after accounting for depression, suggesting involvement of other mechanisms."
- **Problem:** The abstract names no candidate mechanism. Presenting impulsivity and circadian disruption without attribution reads as a study finding. (Relatedly, the "New mechanism" novelty tag is generous for what is a null mediation result.)
- **Suggested fix:** "pointing at mechanisms the authors leave unspecified — a writer could put impulsivity and circadian disruption to them as candidates."

### Study 7: Nearly a third of people newly diagnosed with dementia in German family practices get an antipsychotic first
**PMID:** 42772309 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Practice counts, date range, cohort sizes (94,036 / 55,341), 54.2% / 27.3% / 8.7% / 22.2% and the 16.0% vs 5.6% ratio ("nearly three times") all check out. Industry funding and author disclosures are correctly carried into Caveats.

**Issue A. "Nearly a third" rounds up, and conflates "only" with "first"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Nearly a third of people newly diagnosed with dementia in German family practices get an antipsychotic first"
- **Abstract says:** "In general practices, 54.2% received neither drug class, 27.3% antipsychotics only, 8.7% anti-dementia drugs only."
- **Problem:** Two small slips in one headline. 27.3% is "more than a quarter", not "nearly a third"; and the abstract's 27.3% figure is patients who received antipsychotics *exclusively*, which is not the same category as patients who received an antipsychotic *first*. The abstract reports sequence only as "anti-dementia treatment was initiated first more often (16.0% vs. 5.6%)."
- **Suggested fix:** "More than a quarter of people newly diagnosed with dementia in German family practices get an antipsychotic and nothing else"

### Study 8: A hidden background signal in the brain's electrical noise predicts who develops Parkinson's or Lewy body dementia
**PMID:** 42747400 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

134 iRBD / 51 controls, 26 converters, FOOOF parameterization, HR 1.342 and HR 7.457, and the MoCA-K/slope and MDS-UPDRS-III/offset correlations among converters are all accurate.

**Issue A. "Independently" is not stated in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "both posterior 4–8 Hz periodic power (HR 1.34) and aperiodic offset (HR 7.46) independently predicted conversion"
- **Abstract says:** "Survival analyses showed that higher posterior periodic 4-8 Hz power (hazard ratio = 1.342...) and offset (hazard ratio = 7.457...) were associated with phenoconversion risk."
- **Problem:** Whether the two measures were entered in the same model and remained significant after mutual adjustment is not stated. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "both posterior 4–8 Hz periodic power (HR 1.34) and aperiodic offset (HR 7.46) were associated with conversion risk"

**Issue B. "Strongest prognostic signal" compares hazard ratios on different scales** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Here it carries the strongest prognostic signal in the dataset"
- **Abstract says:** HRs of 1.342 (periodic 4–8 Hz power) and 7.457 (offset), with no statement ranking them.
- **Problem:** Hazard ratios for two differently scaled continuous predictors are not directly comparable; a larger HR can simply reflect a smaller measurement unit. The abstract makes no "strongest" claim.
- **Suggested fix:** "Here it carries a large and statistically robust prognostic signal — the authors note it adds information beyond conventional band power"

**Issue C. "Single-centre" descriptor not supported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "single-center cohort"
- **Abstract says:** Author affiliations span Seoul National University Hospital, Kyung Hee University Hospital at Gangdong and Kangbuk Samsung Hospital; recruitment sites are not described.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only, and the affiliation list suggests more than one recruiting centre may be involved.
- **Suggested fix:** "recruitment sites not specified in the abstract; check whether this is single- or multi-centre"

### Study 9: Motor symptoms outrun thinking problems in people destined for Parkinson's — and the gap shows up years early
**PMID:** 42213896 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (two Moderate — revise before pitching)

The study description is otherwise solid: 766 training patients, three parameters (τ, α, ω), blind set of 49 with DAT imaging and HD-EEG, 35% faster motor progression, 73% discrimination, parkinsonism converters worsening earlier than dementia converters, and the dopaminergic and theta-synchronization correlations.

**Issue A. Headline overstates both the population and the timing** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Motor symptoms outrun thinking problems in people destined for Parkinson's — and the gap shows up years early"
- **Abstract says:** "Motor scores progress 35% faster than cognitive scores in our iRBD cohort"; "Converter iRBD patients exhibited a faster and earlier disease progression than non-converters."
- **Problem:** Two problems. The 35% figure is for the whole iRBD cohort, most of whom did not convert — not for "people destined for Parkinson's". And no lead time in years is given anywhere in the abstract, so "the gap shows up years early" is unsupported.
- **Suggested fix:** "Motor symptoms outrun thinking problems in people with the sleep disorder that precedes Parkinson's"

**Issue B. Pitch attributes the PD-versus-dementia split to the wrong model parameter** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Can you tell, years in advance, whether someone will get Parkinson's or dementia? This model says it is partly written into how fast their motor scores drift relative to their cognitive ones."
- **Abstract says:** "Patients with iRBD who developed parkinsonism worsened earlier than those who developed dementia."
- **Problem:** The abstract distinguishes the two converter groups by *time-shift* (how early they started to decline), not by the motor-versus-cognitive spacing parameter. The pitch hook names a result the study did not report. The abstract also notes converters showed earlier motor than cognitive worsening "regardless of the clinical diagnosis of overt parkinsonism" — i.e. explicitly not a PD-versus-dementia discriminator.
- **Suggested fix:** "Can you tell in advance how fast someone with iRBD will decline? This model says those who go on to parkinsonism start declining earlier than those who go on to dementia — and does it with three numbers per patient."

### Study 10: Tiny white-matter lesions damage the brain's acetylcholine supply lines — and that's what drags cognition down in Parkinson's
**PMID:** 42117428 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one Moderate)

N = 127, mid-to-advanced PD without dementia, FLAIR/UBO lesion quantification, basal forebrain volumetry, ¹⁸F-FEOBV PET, the insular–limbic–frontal–cingulum cluster, 81–88% Ch4 pathway involvement, the direct and basal-forebrain-mediated routes, and stronger mediation for memory and executive function are all reported accurately.

**Issue A. Headline asserts causation from cross-sectional mediation** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Tiny white-matter lesions damage the brain's acetylcholine supply lines — and that's what drags cognition down in Parkinson's"
- **Abstract says:** "Mediation analyses indicated that periventricular lesions had a direct effect on cortical terminal integrity... Our findings indicate that periventricular white matter lesions contribute to cognitive vulnerability"; the design is a single-timepoint imaging study, as the digest's own caveat concedes ("Cross-sectional — mediation analysis cannot establish a causal sequence").
- **Problem:** "Damage" and "that's what drags cognition down" are causal verbs applied to statistical mediation on cross-sectional data, directly contradicting the digest's own Caveats line. "Tiny" is also the digest's addition — lesion size is not characterized in the abstract, only burden.
- **Suggested fix:** "White-matter lesions track with damage to the brain's acetylcholine supply lines — and that may be what links them to cognition in Parkinson's"

**Issue B. Prevention leap in "Why it matters"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "it means blood-pressure and vascular risk management could plausibly protect a neurotransmitter system, not just blood vessels"
- **Abstract says:** "White matter lesion burden may represent a mechanistically meaningful biomarker for predicting cognitive trajectories and informing targeted interventions in Parkinson's disease."
- **Problem:** The study tested no intervention and no vascular risk factor. "Could plausibly" is hedged, but it is the digest's extrapolation, not the paper's.
- **Suggested fix:** "the authors suggest lesion burden could become a biomarker for predicting cognitive trajectories — whether modifying vascular risk changes anything is untested here"

### Study 11: Genetic sleuthing shows many "sporadic" cases of frontotemporal dementia are actually inherited
**PMID:** 42789481 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

3524 individuals, European ancestry, 793 relative pairs, 31.5% previously unrecognized, 15.9% sporadic FTD and 5.5% sporadic ALS, 73 new IBD-defined pedigrees, SOD1 and C9orf72 founder networks, and the 13q21.33 locus in a single five-person pedigree are all correct — including the digest's careful caveat that the locus is unconfirmed.

**Issue A. "Many" overstates one in six** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Genetic sleuthing shows many 'sporadic' cases of frontotemporal dementia are actually inherited"
- **Abstract says:** "Among individuals recorded as 'sporadic', 5.5% of ALS and 15.9% of FTD were genetically related to another affected individual in the cohort"
- **Problem:** 15.9% is better rendered as "one in six" than "many", and the finding is relatedness to another affected individual within this cohort — which is evidence of heritable disease masked by reduced penetrance, not a demonstration that each case is inherited. The digest body gets this right; the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Genetic sleuthing shows one in six 'sporadic' frontotemporal dementia cases is related to another patient"

### Study 13: Four in ten older people with epilepsy struggle with everyday tasks — and one in six at a level seen in dementia
**PMID:** 42704320 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 57, mean age 66.6, FAQ thresholds, 38.6% and 17.5%, financial and memory-dependent clustering, and 4 of 13 worsening over two years are all accurate.

**Issue A. Multivariable predictors apply to the MCI-level outcome only** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Temporal lobe localization, epilepsy onset before age 60 and lower education independently predicted impairment"
- **Abstract says:** "temporal lobe epilepsy (OR = 3.73...), EOE (OR = 6.91...), and lower education (OR = 0.69...) remained independently associated with baseline MCI-level functional impairment. Lower education (OR = 0.62...) was the only factor associated with dementia-level IADL impairment."
- **Problem:** Only education predicted the dementia-level outcome. As written, the sentence sits next to the "17.5% at dementia level" figure and implies all three predictors apply to both.
- **Suggested fix:** "Temporal lobe localization, epilepsy onset before age 60 and lower education independently predicted MCI-level impairment; only lower education predicted dementia-level impairment"

**Issue B. "Decades of it" misdescribes early-onset epilepsy** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the counterintuitive detail that earlier-onset epilepsy — decades of it — predicted worse function in old age"
- **Abstract says:** "early-onset epilepsy (EOE; <60 years)"
- **Problem:** EOE here means onset before age 60 in a cohort with mean age 66.6 — for many participants that is a few years, not decades. The parenthetical invents a duration the study did not measure.
- **Suggested fix:** "the counterintuitive detail that epilepsy beginning before 60 — rather than late-onset epilepsy — predicted worse function in old age"

### Study 14: Hearing loss makes people feel their memory is failing — but tests suggest something else is going on
**PMID:** 42126868 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one Moderate)

N = 143 (60 community-dwelling, 83 audiology clinic), the instruments, the group differences, the loss of independent effect in regression, and the rho values of 0.21/0.25 are all accurate.

**Issue A. Headline asserts causation that the study's own regression failed to support** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Hearing loss makes people feel their memory is failing — but tests suggest something else is going on"
- **Abstract says:** "Regression-based models did not show an independent and significant effect of hearing on subjective cognitive complaints when age, sex, depressive symptoms, and cognitive test scores were also included in the analysis."
- **Problem:** "Makes" is a causal verb on a cross-sectional study — and the specific causal claim it makes is the one the paper's central analysis did not support. The headline and the body are pulling in opposite directions.
- **Suggested fix:** "People with hearing loss report more memory problems — but the link disappears once mood and test scores are accounted for"

### Study 15: A biological marker in caregivers' blood predicts depression months later — but only in the anxiously attached
**PMID:** 42804652 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 166 dementia spousal caregivers, PBMC mitochondrial respiration at baseline only, 7-month follow-up, adjustment for baseline depressive symptoms and covariates, interaction b = −1.11, P = .016, and the "at mean and high levels of attachment anxiety" qualifier are all correct. The Caveats are commendably honest about the single moderation effect.

**Issue A. Direction of the effect is never stated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Spare respiratory capacity — the cells' reserve energy capability — predicted future depressive symptoms"
- **Abstract says:** "This study aimed to assess whether the negative relationship between mitochondrial respiration in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and future depressive symptoms... The interaction between mitochondrial spare respiratory capacity and attachment anxiety predicted future depressive symptoms (b=-1.11...)"
- **Problem:** A reader cannot tell whether higher or lower spare respiratory capacity went with worse mood. The abstract's framing (a *negative* relationship) indicates lower reserve capacity predicted more depressive symptoms.
- **Suggested fix:** "Lower spare respiratory capacity — less cellular reserve energy — predicted higher future depressive symptoms"

**Issue B. Sample demographics in Caveats are not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "caregiver samples skew toward older, white, volunteering spouses"
- **Abstract says:** "A sample of 166 dementia spousal caregivers provided self-report data on demographics and health information at baseline" — no demographic breakdown is given.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only. It may well be true of this cohort, but it is presented as a fact about this sample.
- **Suggested fix:** "sample demographics are not reported in the abstract — check the full text before characterizing generalizability"

### Study 17: Most people with advanced dementia do die at home — but who ends up in hospital is not who you'd expect
**PMID:** 40913758 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues (one Moderate)

N = 284, Singapore home-based palliative care service, final place of care defined as the last three days of life, 81.7% home and 9.2% each hospital and inpatient hospice, the better-status/hospital finding, the discomfort/feeding/psychosocial/unemployment predictors of hospice transfer, and the null for stable sociodemographic factors are all accurate.

**Issue A. Headline reports a different outcome from the one the study measured, and overgeneralizes the population** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Most people with advanced dementia do die at home — but who ends up in hospital is not who you'd expect"
- **Abstract says:** "this study's aim was to identify factors affecting the relatively understudied concept of final place of care (where care was received in the last three days of life...)"; "A retrospective cohort study was conducted using secondary data (electronic medical records) from 284 individuals enrolled in a home-based palliative care service for people with advanced dementia in Singapore."
- **Problem:** The 81.7% figure is final place of *care*, not place of *death* — a distinction the paper exists to make and that the digest's own pitch depends on. The headline also generalizes from a cohort already enrolled in a home palliative care service to "people with advanced dementia" in general, who have very different odds of dying at home.
- **Suggested fix:** "Most people with advanced dementia on a home palliative care service spend their final days at home — but who ends up in hospital is not who you'd expect"

### Study 18: People with Down syndrome understand what dementia is — their families often won't discuss it
**PMID:** 42725843 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Study design (qualitative, applied thematic analysis with inductive and deductive coding), Australian setting, and the three core findings are accurately rendered, and the digest correctly notes that no sample size is given in the abstract.

**Issue A. Quantifiers strengthened beyond the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "several were reluctant to raise it with their relative, and most lacked confidence in existing diagnostic pathways" / headline: "their families often won't discuss it"
- **Abstract says:** "Families were aware of the association between Down syndrome and Alzheimer's disease, but some were reluctant to discuss this with their family member. Families lacked confidence in current dementia diagnostic pathways."
- **Problem:** "Some" becomes "several" and then "often" in the headline; "families lacked confidence" (unquantified) becomes "most". In a qualitative study with an unstated N, these numerical intensifiers are not supportable.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "People with Down syndrome understand what dementia is — some families are reluctant to discuss it"; body: "some were reluctant to raise it with their relative, and families lacked confidence in existing diagnostic pathways"

**Issue B. Background genetics presented inside the study paragraph** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Families knew about the strong link between Down syndrome and Alzheimer's — roughly a lifetime certainty of Alzheimer's pathology, given trisomy of the amyloid precursor protein gene"
- **Abstract says:** "People with Down syndrome are at high risk of Alzheimer's disease."
- **Problem:** The APP-trisomy mechanism and the near-universal pathology figure are correct background but are not in this abstract, and the sentence construction implies they are what families reported knowing. Also note the "single health service in Melbourne" caveat is unverifiable from the abstract, which gives no recruitment site.
- **Suggested fix:** "Families knew about the strong link between Down syndrome and Alzheimer's (background: trisomy of the amyloid precursor protein gene means near-universal Alzheimer's pathology by midlife — not from this paper)"
