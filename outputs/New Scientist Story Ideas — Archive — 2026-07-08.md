### 1. Beating your own expectations matters more than success or failure for feeling proud or ashamed

**Journal:** *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* | **Published:** 2026 Jul
**PMID:** 41854697 | **DOI:** 10.1037/pspa0000486
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 9/10 — Counterintuitive challenge to a decades-old model of self-conscious emotion, large multi-study human sample, clean causal design including longitudinal data, and a clear everyday-life hook around pride and shame.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at the University of British Columbia ran six studies (total N=1,643) testing what triggers self-conscious emotions like pride, shame, and guilt. Rather than simply evaluating outcomes as successes or failures — the long-standing view — participants felt pride when they exceeded their own expectations and shame or guilt when they fell below them, regardless of whether the outcome was objectively good or bad. The effect was robust across hypothetical scenarios, lab manipulations, and a longitudinal naturalistic study tracking emotions over time.

**Why it matters:** This reframes how we understand self-evaluation: it suggests people's emotional reactions to their own behaviour are anchored to personal benchmarks, not absolute outcomes, which has implications for understanding motivation, self-esteem, and performance anxiety.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "You feel proud when you beat your own expectations — not when you simply succeed" Doing well isn't enough to feel proud — what matters is whether you did better than you thought you would. For decades, psychologists assumed self-conscious emotions like pride and shame hinge on evaluating outcomes as successes or failures. Six studies with over 1,600 participants now suggest the real trigger is something subtler: the gap between what you expected and what actually happened. Exceed your expectations and you feel pride; fall short of them and shame or guilt follows — even if the outcome was objectively fine. The finding reframes achievement culture, perfectionism, and why high-performers can feel hollow after meeting a goal they always knew they'd hit. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are fascinated by the hidden mechanics of self-perception, and this upends a foundational assumption about how pride and shame actually work.

### 2. An antibiotic added to antidepressants lifts the 'emotional numbness' of melancholic depression — but only in that subtype

**Journal:** *Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology & Biological Psychiatry* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 1
**PMID:** 42385835 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pnpbp.2026.111819
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Strong RCT design with decent sample (N=300), highly counterintuitive subtype-specific antibiotic finding with a clear biological mechanism, and direct relevance to mental health treatment — docked one point for single-centre, short follow-up.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** In a 4-week randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 300 young adults with first-episode, drug-naïve major depressive disorder (MDD) were stratified by melancholic versus non-melancholic features and assigned to minocycline (200 mg/day) or placebo added to sertraline. Melancholic patients had higher baseline levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β and more severe anhedonia than non-melancholic patients. Minocycline augmentation significantly improved anhedonia and depressive symptoms specifically in the melancholic group, with improvement strongly correlated with reductions in IL-1β (r = 0.639). The benefit was not observed in non-melancholic patients receiving minocycline.

**Why it matters:** The findings suggest that the subtype of depression a person has may determine whether anti-inflammatory treatment helps, pointing toward a more targeted, biology-driven approach to prescribing. If replicated, this could help clinicians identify which patients are most likely to benefit from add-on anti-inflammatory therapy.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "An antibiotic only treats depression if you have the right kind" Adding a common antibiotic to a standard antidepressant relieved the joylessness of melancholic depression — but did nothing for other forms of the condition. A rigorous randomised trial found that minocycline — an antibiotic already used for acne and infections — dramatically improved anhedonia (the inability to feel pleasure) in melancholic depression, but had no benefit for non-melancholic patients. The key appears to be inflammation: melancholic patients had higher levels of the cytokine IL-1β, and the more minocycline reduced that cytokine, the better they felt. The story challenges the common-sense view that depression is a single condition and raises the prospect of blood-test-guided prescribing. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are fascinated by the idea that depression is not one disease, and that biological subtypes could one day dictate personalised treatment.

**The study:** Researchers at Central South University, China, used diffusion tensor MRI to examine white matter microstructure in 133 patients with first-episode schizophrenia, 49 of their unaffected siblings, and 154 healthy controls (N=336 total). **Why it matters:** The discovery that healthy siblings share some of the same white matter changes as people with schizophrenia — and that these overlap with genetic risk scores — suggests these brain features may mark inherited vulnerability before psychosis ever develops, potentially opening a window for very early identification.
