### 1. Semaglutide is linked to fewer cognitive complaints in people with psychiatric illness

**Journal:** *BMJ Mental Health* | **Published:** 20 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42624612 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bmjment-2026-302828
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — very large cohort and a topical drug, but strictly observational and based on clinician-recorded signs rather than cognitive testing.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Using de-identified US electronic health records (NeuroBlu, 1999–2024), researchers compared 13,007 adults with a psychiatric diagnosis who started an antidiabetic drug (mean age 62; 1261 started semaglutide). Over 12 months, clinician-rated cognitive signs/symptoms — covering memory, attention and orientation — were lower with semaglutide than with no antidiabetic treatment (mean ratio 0.75), glipizide or empagliflozin, with the strongest signal in memory and in people with major depression.

**Why it matters:** Cognitive fog in depression and psychosis is common, disabling and essentially untreated; this raises the possibility that a widely prescribed GLP-1 drug touches it, though only a randomised trial could show causation.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "The weight-loss drug that may also clear the fog." Open on a patient with depression whose main complaint is not low mood but forgetting names and losing threads mid-sentence. Call Maxime Taquet or Riccardo De Giorgi at Oxford, who have a track record of large EHR emulations, and ask them directly what the confounding-by-indication problem is here.
- **Wider angle:** For a health-policy or business outlet: GLP-1 agonists are accumulating off-label cognitive and psychiatric signals faster than trials can test them, and health systems are now the de facto evidence generators through EHR mining — a piece about who owns and profits from that evidence pipeline (note Holmusk's involvement).

**Caveats:** Observational, cannot establish causation; outcome is a composite of routine clinician-recorded signs, not standardised neuropsychological testing; two co-authors are employed by and hold equity in the data company (Holmusk/KKT Technologies), one author has consultancy fees from Holmusk; unmeasured confounding by weight loss, glycaemic control and prescribing preference is likely.

### 2. Sleep sharpens memory for who told you what — and makes conversation more efficient

**Journal:** *Cognition* | **Published:** Nov 2026 (online 23 Jul 2026)
**PMID:** 42492165 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cognition.2026.106670
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — extends the well-known sleep-consolidation effect into genuinely social territory across three experiments, but the mechanism hunt came up empty.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Across three experiments, participants did a matcher-director communication task (on computer, then face-to-face) with two partners before and after 12 hours of sleep or wake. Sleepers showed better item recognition *and* better source memory — who said what — and used fewer words to describe previously discussed items, though this efficiency was not partner-specific. A third experiment with a polysomnographically recorded two-hour nap found no relationship between slow oscillation–spindle coupling and retention.

**Why it matters:** It suggests sleep does not just file away facts but preserves the shared conversational history that makes talking to a familiar person efficient — a social function of sleep that has barely been studied.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Why a bad night's sleep makes you a worse conversationalist." Open on the awkward moment of re-explaining something you already agreed on last week. Lead with the source-memory result — sleep protects *who* said it, not just *what*. Call Heather Ferguson at Kent or Gareth Gaskell at York; press Gaskell on why the spindle analysis was null.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a general-interest magazine on "common ground" as the invisible infrastructure of relationships — how couples, colleagues and old friends build a compressed private language, and what happens to it in insomnia, shift work and dementia.

**Caveats:** Sleep-versus-wake designs confound time of day and circadian phase; the nap experiment was null on electrophysiology, so no mechanism is established; laboratory communication task may not generalise to real conversation.

### 3. Eye-movement therapy works even when you are not consciously thinking about the memory

**Journal:** *Behaviour Research and Therapy* | **Published:** Sep 2026 (online 28 Jul 2026)
**PMID:** 42531952 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.brat.2026.105119
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — directly challenges a core assumption of EMDR, with objective physiological outcomes; limited by being a lab fear-conditioning analogue, not clinical trauma.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Participants were fear-conditioned to snake/spider images, then re-exposed to those cues either passively (Experiment 1, N=56) or subliminally (Experiment 2, N=83) while performing smooth-pursuit eye movements. Passive re-exposure cut skin conductance responses during the session and 24 hours later; subliminal re-exposure did not change immediate physiology but reduced the frequency and emotional intensity of intrusive thoughts over the following week — and people reported markedly less distress during the unconscious version.

**Why it matters:** EMDR protocols assume the patient must actively hold the traumatic memory in mind; if reactivation below awareness is sufficient, treatment could in principle be made far less aversive — a hypothesis that now needs testing in actual patients.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Could trauma therapy work without you having to relive the trauma?" Open on the dropout problem: many people quit exposure-based therapy because it hurts. Lead with the finding that the *less* distressing subliminal condition still cut intrusive thoughts for a week. Call Yang Wang's group at Zhejiang University, and get a sceptical EMDR researcher to say why lab-conditioned spider fear is not PTSD.
- **Wider angle:** For a specialist psychology or long-read outlet: EMDR's decades-long mechanism controversy — is the eye movement doing anything, is it just working memory taxation, and why has a therapy this widely used remained this poorly explained?

**Caveats:** Analogue fear conditioning in non-clinical volunteers, not trauma patients; short follow-up (24 hours / 1 week); intrusive-thought outcomes are self-reported; no clinical claim can be made.

### 4. Wait 27 seconds and your short-term memory starts inventing words that were never there

**Journal:** *Memory & Cognition* | **Published:** Aug 2026 (epub 11 Jun 2026)
**PMID:** 42277491 | **DOI:** 10.3758/s13421-026-01881-7
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — clean, vivid demonstration that false memories form within seconds, and it survives explicit warnings; incremental to a much-studied paradigm.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Researchers ran a short-term-memory version of the classic Deese/Roediger–McDermott false-memory task: five-word lists followed by recognition tests either immediately or after 3, 9 or 27 seconds of distraction. True recognition fell as the delay grew while false recognition *rose* — and the illusion appeared even with no delay at all, and even when participants were explicitly warned that semantically related lures were coming and told not to be fooled.

**Why it matters:** False memory is usually framed as something that creeps in over days; this places its onset within the first half-minute and shows warning people does not protect them.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Your memory starts distorting the moment you stop paying attention." Open on a witness statement taken 30 seconds after an incident. The hook: forewarning doesn't help. Call Guillermo Campoy at the University of Murcia and ask what phonological versus semantic traces decaying at different rates means for eyewitness protocols.
- **Wider angle:** For a law-and-science outlet: the "immediate recall is reliable" assumption embedded in police first-account procedures, and whether the seconds-scale distortion curve should change how first accounts are taken and weighted.

**Caveats:** Not preregistered; word-list laboratory paradigm with limited ecological reach; student-heavy sample likely.

### 5. To remember to do something later, physically rehearse it — imagining it doesn't help

**Journal:** *Memory* | **Published:** 21 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42627881 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09658211.2026.2719615
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — large lifespan sample and a genuinely negative result for a strategy the field has been promoting; single lab task limits reach.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** 373 participants — children (n=90), adolescents (n=96), young adults (n=97) and middle-aged adults (n=90) — were randomly assigned to form intentions using episodic future thinking (mentally simulating the future action), enactment encoding (physically performing it), or standard encoding, then completed a computerised prospective-memory task. Enactment beat both other conditions across every age group; episodic future thinking gave no benefit over standard encoding at all.

**Why it matters:** Episodic future thinking is a popular recommendation for improving intention memory; this well-powered comparison finds the physical rehearsal wins and the mental simulation does nothing, at least under high ongoing task demand.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Don't visualise it — do it." Open on the universal failure of remembering to take the medication / post the letter / send the email. The surprise is that the widely touted mental-simulation trick failed while a crude physical run-through worked, from age 8 to 60. Call Mareike Altgassen at Mainz and ask whether cue focality explains the null.
- **Wider angle:** A practical, service-y piece for a general health outlet on the science of prospective memory in medication adherence — an area where forgetting has measurable clinical costs — including what enactment might look like in a real routine.

**Caveats:** Single computerised task; randomised but not, as reported, preregistered; the null for episodic future thinking may be task-specific (the authors themselves invoke cue focality and ongoing task load).

### 6. Taking notes you expect to keep makes you encode less deeply than a deliberately shallow task

**Journal:** *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* | **Published:** 20 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42622798 | **DOI:** 10.1037/xlm0001656
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — four experiments with a striking benchmark comparison; laboratory word lists rather than real study material.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Across four experiments, participants either offloaded information into notes (expecting or not expecting to lose access at test) or encoded under deep- versus shallow-processing instructions. When access to notes was unexpectedly removed, recall was *worse* than in the shallow-processing condition. A fourth experiment showed people made fewer "remember" (vivid recollection) judgements when their notes were available at test — access changed the subjective quality of retrieval, not just its accuracy.

**Why it matters:** It puts a number on the cost of externalising memory: expecting a backup can leave you encoding below the level of someone doing a deliberately superficial task, and it thins out the felt richness of what you do recall.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Your phone isn't just holding your memories — it's stopping them forming." Open on the panic of losing a notes app before an exam or meeting. Lead with the benchmark: worse than shallow processing. Call Lauren Richmond at Stony Brook, and pair this with the offloading-under-overload paper (PMID 42607389) for a balanced piece on when offloading helps and when it hollows you out.
- **Wider angle:** An education-sector feature on laptop notetaking, lecture recording and AI meeting summaries: institutions are systematically engineering the "expected access" condition that this work shows suppresses encoding. Fits an education or technology outlet.

**Caveats:** Laboratory word-list recall; effects on real-world learning untested; sample sizes and demographics not reported in the abstract.

### 7. Eye-movement therapy dampens shame, anger and sadness memories, not just fear

**Journal:** *Cognition and Emotion* | **Published:** 20 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42622123 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02699931.2026.2717608
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — big sample and a clean null on the moderator that most theories predicted; online, single-session, self-report only.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** 560 participants were randomly assigned to recall an autobiographical memory dominated by fear, shame, anger or sadness, and then either performed eye movements while recalling (dual task) or simply recalled it. Dual-task recall produced the largest drops in memory vividness and unpleasantness across all four emotions, and the dominant emotion did not reliably moderate the effect.

**Why it matters:** PTSD is increasingly recognised as not purely fear-based; this suggests the dual-task mechanism is emotion-general, which matters for treating moral injury and shame-dominated trauma.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Trauma isn't just fear — and the standard therapy trick seems to know that." Open on a patient whose defining trauma emotion is humiliation, not terror. Call Kevin van Schie at Tilburg and ask what a well-powered null moderator result tells us about the working-memory-taxation account.
- **Wider angle:** For a mental-health or feature outlet: the quiet reclassification of PTSD away from a fear disorder, and what that means for moral injury in veterans, healthcare workers and abuse survivors.

**Caveats:** Online proof-of-principle, single session; outcomes entirely self-rated (vividness, unpleasantness, accessibility); non-clinical autobiographical memories; no follow-up.

### 8. A third of online study participants wander off mid-task — and it shows in their memory scores

**Journal:** *Behavior Research Methods* | **Published:** 17 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42608507 | **DOI:** 10.3758/s13428-026-03149-z
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — two pre-registered experiments with an internal replication, and it lands squarely on the credibility of a huge slice of published psychology.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Two pre-registered experiments tracked whether Amazon Mechanical Turk participants navigated away from the study page during the encoding phase of a memory task. Roughly one third went off task, and doing so predicted worse memory performance. Experiment 2 replicated this and found that an explicit warning reduced page-switching but did not reduce the *proportion of time* spent off task.

**Why it matters:** Since the pandemic, a large fraction of published cognitive and mental-health research has been collected online; this quantifies a systematic, performance-degrading contaminant in that data — and shows the obvious fix doesn't work.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "A third of the people in your favourite online psychology study weren't looking at the screen." Open on the mundane image of a paid participant checking their email during a memory test. Lead with the failed warning manipulation. Call Daniel Peterson at Skidmore; ask how much of the recent literature he thinks is affected and what a workable attention check would look like.
- **Wider angle:** A meta-science piece for a research-culture or science-policy outlet on the post-2020 migration of behavioural science to crowdsourcing platforms, gig-economy incentives for speed over care, and whether journals should require off-task telemetry.

**Caveats:** MTurk-specific — may not generalise to Prolific or other panels; page-switching is a proxy for attention and misses phone use; effect sizes on memory not given in the abstract.

### 9. Notetaking rescues an overloaded working memory — but people botch their own notes

**PMID:** 42607389 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107645
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a useful replication-plus-extension with a nice twist on offloading errors, but the core benefit is unsurprising.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Participants performed a letter-string task at loads of four, eight and 12 items, with or without the option to type notes. Offloading improved performance and the benefit grew with load, peaking at 12 items. But people also made more offloading-specific errors at high load — copying items down wrong, or misreading their own notes at recall.

- **Wider angle:** A human-factors feature for a safety, aviation or healthcare-quality publication: the error taxonomy of transcription under cognitive load, and why checklist design increasingly assumes offloading is free.

### 10. A week of brain stimulation improves both sleep and overnight skill consolidation in insomnia

**Novelty:** First-in-class

**The study:** Forty-two people with insomnia were randomised, double-blind, to seven consecutive days of active or sham transcranial direct current stimulation. ### 11. Acting out an instruction helps you remember it — even if you have never seen anything

**Journal:** *Memory* | **Published:** 20 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42622513 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09658211.2026.2719617
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a rarely tested population gives a clean theoretical answer; small N and an odd sub-result in the residual-vision group.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Fifty-one participants — with total vision loss, residual vision, or typical vision — recalled spoken instructions either after hearing them only or after physically enacting them. Enactment improved recall in all three groups with no significant difference by visual acuity, though people with residual vision appeared to benefit least.

**Why it matters:** The enactment effect has been widely attributed to visual imagery of the action; this suggests kinaesthetic encoding alone can carry it, which matters for how instructions are designed for blind users.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Why doing beats hearing — even in the dark." Open on the practical question of how a blind person memorises a multi-step procedure. - **Wider angle:** An accessibility-design story for a technology or disability-focused outlet: voice assistants and screen readers deliver instructions as pure speech, and this suggests they are using the weakest available channel.

**Caveats:** N=51, small and unevenly divided across three groups; the residual-vision finding is exploratory; single-site; participants with visual impairment are a heterogeneous group.

### 12. You remember cheaters' faces — not just the photos you saw them in

**Journal:** *Memory* | **Published:** 21 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42625545 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09658211.2026.2719607
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — closes a real methodological loophole in the cheater-detection literature; a confirmation rather than a reversal.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Previous studies showing that people have better source memory for individuals who behaved dishonestly used the *same* photograph at learning and test, raising the possibility participants were remembering picture details rather than people. This experiment changed the viewing perspective of the faces between learning and test, and still found a source-memory advantage for cheaters in both the changed-perspective and identical-photo conditions.

**Why it matters:** It shifts the cheater-memory effect from a possible artefact of image matching to genuine person recognition, shoring up an evolutionary-psychology claim that has been methodologically vulnerable.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Your brain really does keep a list of who ripped you off." Open on the experience of recognising someone months later and immediately recalling that they let you down. Call Mirjam Kroneisen at RPTU Kaiserslautern-Landau and press on how large the effect is and whether it survives long delays.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a general-interest or social-science outlet on reputation as cognitive infrastructure — from village gossip to online seller ratings — and whether digital reputation systems are outsourcing a memory system we evolved to run internally.

**Caveats:** Sample size not reported in the abstract; single laboratory experiment; artificial reputational vignettes; short retention interval.

### 13. Handwriting a new word by hand shapes how strongly it lodges in the mental lexicon overnight

**Journal:** *Cortex* | **Published:** 6 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42607453 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cortex.2026.07.006
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — technically impressive EEG-plus-pen-kinematics co-registration with an overnight behavioural payoff; the story is subtle to tell.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Sixty-three native Spanish speakers learned novel words by writing them repeatedly while EEG and pen kinematics were recorded simultaneously. Spelling knowledge plateaued after roughly four exposures, but motor refinement of the handwriting continued improving across all encounters — the two processes did not track each other. Crucially, the size of the exposure-driven neural change during learning predicted how strongly the new word competed with real words in a lexical decision test after a night's sleep.

**Why it matters:** It gives a concrete neural handle on the long-argued claim that handwriting supports word learning, and separates the orthographic from the motor part of the process.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Four goes to learn the spelling; much longer for the hand to catch up." Open on a child learning to write a new word, or an adult learning a foreign script. Lead with the dissociation and the overnight prediction. Call Yang Fu at Shanghai International Studies University.
- **Wider angle:** For an education outlet: the handwriting-versus-keyboard debate in schools has been fought largely on weak correlational evidence; this is the kind of mechanistic data that could settle parts of it, and it deserves a sober audit of what is and isn't known.

**Caveats:** N=63; single language and script; novel pseudowords rather than real vocabulary; the sleep link is correlational (neural modulation predicted, but sleep was not manipulated).

### 14. Cognitive training in schizophrenia shifts brain waves, memory and sweat responses together

**Journal:** *BMC Psychiatry* | **Published:** 17 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42625165 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12888-026-08491-2
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — prospectively registered RCT combining EEG, autonomic and behavioural outcomes, but modest N and an unspecified control condition.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Fifty-seven clinically stable outpatients with schizophrenia were randomised to 12 weeks of Cognitive Remediation Therapy (24 sessions) or control; 48 completed. The CRT group showed larger P300 amplitudes, increased frontal midline theta during a 2-back task, reduced skin-conductance reactivity to negative images, and better working memory, emotion recognition, symptom scores and quality of life. Neural gains correlated with cognitive gains; autonomic changes correlated with emotion-regulation gains.

**Why it matters:** Cognitive remediation is known to help behaviourally; showing convergent neural and autonomic change makes it harder to dismiss as practice effects and points to which mechanisms to target.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Brain training that actually changes the brain — in schizophrenia." Open on the fact that antipsychotics do almost nothing for the cognitive symptoms that most predict whether someone can work. Call Metin Çınaroğlu at Istanbul Nişantaşı University and ask what the control group actually did.
- **Wider angle:** A health-services piece for a specialist psychiatry or health outlet: cognitive remediation has decent evidence but is barely available in most systems — why psychosocial interventions with mechanistic support still lose out to prescribing.

**Caveats:** N=57 randomised, 48 completed — under 100; single centre; control condition not characterised in the abstract (possibly no active comparator, so expectancy effects are plausible); many secondary outcomes without stated correction.

### 15. Daily diaries show mood and PTSD symptoms move together within a day — but not into the next

**Journal:** *European Journal of Psychotraumatology* | **Published:** 20 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42623486 | **DOI:** 10.1080/20008066.2026.2703327
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — the null cross-day result is genuinely useful against a common clinical assumption, but the design is uncontrolled and the sample small.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Seventy trauma-exposed adults completed 21 daily surveys before and after four sessions of the Processing of Positive Memories Technique — a therapy built around retrieving detailed positive autobiographical memories. Negative affect, low positive affect and trauma-related cognitions tracked same-day symptom severity strongly, but none predicted next-day symptoms once prior-day symptoms were accounted for. The same-day negative-affect–symptom coupling weakened after the intervention.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that "a bad day today means a worse day tomorrow" is not how PTSD symptoms actually propagate at the daily timescale — and that a therapy may work by loosening within-day coupling rather than by lowering average distress.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "A therapy for trauma built entirely on good memories." Open on the counterintuitive clinical instruction: don't talk about the trauma, talk in the present tense about a good day. Lead with the finding that today's bad mood doesn't forecast tomorrow. Call Ateka Contractor at the University of North Texas.
- **Wider angle:** For a methods-literate outlet: ecological momentary assessment is quietly overturning cross-sectional assumptions about how symptoms cause each other, and clinical theory has not caught up.

**Caveats:** N=70; all outcomes self-reported; the daily-diary analysis has no control arm (the RCT comparison is reported elsewhere); interrupted-time-series result is marginal (p=.035); trauma-exposed volunteers may not represent treatment-seeking populations.

### 16. A fourth "perception–memory pair" turns up in the parietal lobe

**Journal:** *eNeuro* | **Published:** 20 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42624782 | **DOI:** 10.1523/ENEURO.0155-26.2026
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a tidy extension of an elegant organisational principle, replicated across two datasets, but tiny N and a hard sell to non-specialists.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Across two independent fMRI datasets (total N=24, 14 female) using different stimuli and memory tasks, recalling visual scenes reliably activated a region just in front of a recently discovered scene-perception area in superior parietal cortex. At rest, the perception area coupled with other perception areas and the memory area with other memory areas, and the perception area sat at the junction of four streams linking egocentric vision, map-based navigation, perspective taking and goal-directed movement.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the brain systematically builds paired perception-and-memory regions across the cortical surface — a repeating architectural motif for stitching what you see onto what you remember about a place.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "The brain keeps building the same circuit over and over." Open on walking into a familiar room and instantly knowing what's around the corner. The hook is the repeating motif rather than the region itself. Call Adam Steel at Illinois Urbana-Champaign.
- **Wider angle:** A navigation feature for a science or technology outlet: how the brain's place-memory machinery maps onto — and diverges from — the way robots and self-driving systems fuse live sensing with stored maps.

**Caveats:** N=24 total across two datasets; fMRI localisation only, no causal manipulation; region naming and boundaries in this part of parietal cortex remain contested.

### 17. Harder-looking Chinese characters are easier to pull out of short-term memory

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** 18 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42612440 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107639
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — genuinely odd results with a language-specific hook; three experiments but a narrow paradigm and no sample details.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** Using the Sternberg short-term memory scanning paradigm across three experiments, researchers varied stroke count, phonetic regularity and semantic transparency of Chinese characters. Simpler characters were retrieved more accurately, as expected — but characters whose pronunciation is *not* predictable from their phonetic component, and characters whose meaning is *not* transparent from their parts, were retrieved more accurately than their "helpful" counterparts.

**Why it matters:** It hints that predictability in a writing system can create interference rather than support during memory retrieval — a result that pushes against the assumption that regularity always aids processing.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Why the more confusing Chinese characters are easier to hold in mind." Open on the intuition that a logographic script must be harder to remember, and then invert it. Call Hongli Li at Anyang Normal University, and get a psycholinguist working on alphabetic scripts to say whether the same logic should apply to irregular English spellings.
- **Wider angle:** For a language or education outlet: what writing-system differences reveal about the supposed universals of reading and memory, and why so much of cognitive psychology's evidence base rests on English.

**Caveats:** Sample size, age range and preregistration status not reported in the abstract; Sternberg paradigm is far from natural reading; results need replication in alphabetic scripts before general claims.

### 18. Targeted brain stimulation moves a network — but not the memory it was aimed at

**Journal:** *International Review of Psychiatry* | **Published:** 18 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42610835 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09540261.2026.2682195
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — the dissociation between network change and behavioural non-change is the interesting bit, but it is a single case.
**Media check:** Not verified — no SerpAPI key configured

**The study:** In a randomised placebo-controlled single-case protocol, a patient with posterior cortical atrophy — an atypical, visually dominated form of Alzheimer's — received intermittent theta-burst stimulation to a personally identified left caudal middle frontal target chosen for its connectivity to the default mode network. Active stimulation increased connectivity between the target and distributed DMN regions as predicted, but associative memory did not improve; visuospatial working memory did.

**Why it matters:** It is a clean, honestly reported example of the gap between shifting a brain network and shifting behaviour, which is the central unresolved problem in therapeutic brain stimulation.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "We moved the network. The memory didn't budge." Open on the seductive logic of personalised, connectivity-guided TMS, then the result. Call Mark Eldaief or Alexandra Touroutoglou at MGH and ask what the unexpected visuospatial gain implies about targeting.

### 21. Personalised symptom networks reveal four recurring "shapes" of PTSD

**Journal:** *Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics* | **Published:** 17 Aug 2026
**PMID:** 42607016 | **DOI:** 10.1159/pps/aemag004
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — an interesting bridge between individual and group-level psychopathology with intensive data, but the story is abstract and methods-heavy.

**The study:** Fifty-five trauma-exposed people (31 with PTSD) chose personalised items representing the components of Ehlers and Clark's cognitive model — trauma memory, triggers, negative appraisals, sense of current threat, coping strategies. - **Pitch angle:** "There isn't one PTSD — there are four shapes of it." Open on two patients with identical diagnoses whose symptoms are driven by entirely different engines. ## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Five studies in this batch converge on the same trade-off: memory is built by physical action, and weakened when action is outsourced. "To remember to do something later, physically rehearse it — imagining it doesn't help" (PMID 42627881) found enactment beat mental simulation at every age from 8 to 60; "Acting out an instruction helps you remember it — even if you have never seen anything" (PMID 42622513) showed the enactment effect survives total blindness, so it is kinaesthetic rather than visual; and "Handwriting a new word by hand shapes how strongly it lodges in the mental lexicon overnight" (PMID 42607453) gives the motor route a neural signature that predicts overnight lexical integration. Against that, "Taking notes you expect to keep makes you encode less deeply than a deliberately shallow task" (PMID 42622798) shows expected external access suppresses encoding below the level of deliberately superficial processing, while "Notetaking rescues an overloaded working memory — but people botch their own notes" (PMID 42607389) shows offloading genuinely rescues an overloaded system but generates its own transcription and misreading errors.

**Why pitch this now:** Institutions are currently engineering the exact "expected access" condition these studies flag as corrosive — lecture recording, AI meeting summaries, voice assistants, screen readers delivering instructions as pure speech — while simultaneously stripping out the handwriting and physical rehearsal that the other half of the evidence says does the encoding work.

**Angle:** A feature on the emerging cognitive arithmetic of when to use your body and when to use your device: memory as something enacted rather than stored, with a hard-nosed audit of where offloading genuinely buys capacity (high load, checklists, handovers) and where it quietly hollows out learning. Aimed at general readers who take notes for a living, plus an education and workplace-technology readership.

**Potential outlets:**
- **New Scientist Features** — fits the Mind slot's appetite for a counterintuitive, mechanism-led take on everyday behaviour, and lets the blind-participant enactment result carry the theoretical punch rather than a lifestyle framing.
- **BBC Future** — built for exactly this "what the science says about how you should actually take notes" register, with a global education and productivity audience.
- **The Atlantic (Technology or Family/Education)** — has repeatedly run the handwriting-versus-keyboard and phones-and-memory arguments; this batch supplies the mechanistic evidence those essays have been missing, including the AI-summary angle.
- **Wired** — the "AI is now the default note-taker" hook, framed as an unexamined design decision with measurable cognitive costs, including the accessibility point about voice interfaces using the weakest encoding channel.
