### 1. Halving violence against children by treating the parents' own distress

**Journal:** *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry* | **Published:** 17 September 2026
**PMID:** 42754240 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jcpp.70241
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a large, prospectively registered, pragmatic RCT with unusually big effect sizes in a setting where trials are rare; limited by self-reported violence measures.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers randomized 479 migrant and displaced caregivers from Myanmar living in Thailand to "Parenting for Resilience" — a co-designed program delivered by non-specialists that tackles caregiver mental health and parenting skills at the same time — or to information about local services. Six months after the program ended, physical violence against children in the intervention group had fallen by roughly half (IRR 0.48) and psychological violence by nearly a third, while caregivers' psychological distress, post-traumatic stress symptoms, emotion regulation and psychological flexibility all improved.

**Why it matters:** Most parenting programs treat parenting as a skills-deficit problem; this trial suggests that helping caregivers regulate their own emotions may be the lever that shifts how they treat their children — and that lay workers can deliver it in a displacement camp setting.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The intervention did not primarily teach discipline techniques — it taught distressed adults how to handle their own feelings, and violence against their children dropped by half. Open on a caregiver in Mae Sot attending a group session run by a neighbor, not a psychologist. Call Amanda Sim (McMaster) or Eve Puffer (Duke). Note the notable null: children's own emotional and behavioral difficulties did not change over the trial window.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a global development or humanitarian outlet on the "task-shifting" revolution — mental health care delivered by trained non-specialists in refugee and conflict settings, and whether donors will fund something that looks like therapy rather than food and shelter.

**Caveats:** Violence and distress outcomes are caregiver self-reported, which risks social desirability bias in an unblinded trial; control condition was information only, not an active comparator; six-month follow-up; findings may not generalize beyond displaced Myanmar communities.

### 2. It's how impulsive you feel today, not how impulsive you are, that predicts suicidal thoughts

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 23 September 2026
**PMID:** 42778027 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122522
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — 30 days of intensive real-time data overturning a trait-based view of suicide risk; held back by a modest, self-selected sample.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** A community sample of 103 adults with recent, repeated suicidal ideation (mean age 33) completed a baseline assessment and then 30 days of ecological momentary assessment, reporting stressful events, impulsivity, emotion dysregulation and suicidal thoughts as they went about their lives. Daily stressors were linked to suicidal ideation only when a person's impulsivity was higher than their own personal average at that moment; stable trait impulsivity showed no such interaction. Emotion dysregulation predicted ideation both as a trait and, more strongly, as a momentary state.

**Why it matters:** Clinical risk assessment leans heavily on stable personality traits, but this suggests the dangerous window opens when someone's usual self-control temporarily dips — which is exactly the kind of signal a phone could in principle detect.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the deviation-from-your-own-baseline idea: the risky moment isn't when an impulsive person hits a bad day, it's when anyone becomes more impulsive than they normally are. Open on the mechanics of an EMA study — a phone buzzing six times a day for a month. Call Anne Knorr or Brooke Ammerman, and press them on what a "just-in-time" intervention would actually do when triggered.
- **Wider angle:** A specialist digital health or technology feature on whether passive smartphone sensing can ethically flag suicide risk in real time — covering false positives, consent, and what happens to the data.

**Caveats:** Observational (cannot establish causation); N=103 with high proportions of gender and sexual minority participants, which may limit generalization; all outcomes self-reported; only concurrent, not prospective, ideation showed the key interaction.

### 3. Feeling seen as human by political opponents softens partisan hostility — and disgust is the reason

**Journal:** *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* | **Published:** 10 September 2026
**PMID:** 42723350 | **DOI:** 10.1177/01461672261481208
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — five studies across three countries and a surprising emotional pathway; mostly correlational and attitudinal rather than behavioral.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Across five studies with 1,541 participants in the UK, US and Canada, researchers measured "meta-humanization" — the belief that the other political side sees your group as fully human — and tested anger, disgust and empathy as parallel routes to conciliatory attitudes. Meta-humanization consistently predicted less affective polarization and more willingness to negotiate, cooperate and meet the other side, and the dominant emotional pathway was reduced disgust, not increased empathy. Perceived threat to social cohesion moderated the effect, driving it most strongly at moderate levels.

**Why it matters:** Depolarization efforts usually try to build empathy; this points instead at a more visceral emotion — moral disgust — as the thing that has to be downregulated before people will talk to each other.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Empathy may be the wrong target. Open on the experience of discovering that your political opponents don't actually think you're subhuman — and what that does to the gut-level revulsion that keeps partisans apart. Call Melissa Pavetich (Heriot-Watt Dubai) or Sofia Stathi (Greenwich), and ask whether meta-humanization can be manipulated at scale or only measured.
- **Wider angle:** A politics-and-media piece for a current affairs outlet on the incentive structure that keeps each side convinced the other has written them off as inhuman — and what platforms and campaign strategists gain from it.

**Caveats:** Largely cross-sectional and correlational; outcomes are self-reported attitudes and intentions rather than actual contact behavior; Anglophone Western samples only.

### 4. Resilience stops teenage distress starting — but won't get you out of it

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 15 December 2026 (Epub 16 September 2026)
**PMID:** 42749162 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122517
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a very large prospective cohort with a genuinely asymmetric result; weakened by the fact that everything is self-reported and one-year follow-up.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers followed 9,035 Chinese children and adolescents aged 10 to 17 across two waves 12 months apart, measuring gut symptoms (Rome IV criteria), depression, anxiety, suicidal behavior and resilience. Four profiles emerged, from healthy (~60%) to severe somatic-emotional distress (~6%), and they were sticky: 81% of healthy teens stayed healthy and 56% of severely distressed teens stayed severe. Higher baseline resilience — especially emotion regulation, family support and interpersonal help-seeking — substantially reduced the odds of sliding into distress, but did not predict recovery for anyone already in the mild or severe groups.

**Why it matters:** "Resilience training" is sold as a universal fix, but here it behaves like a preventive measure only — once a teenager's body and mood are both affected, personal resilience does nothing to pull them out.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the asymmetry — resilience as a fence at the top of the cliff, not an ambulance at the bottom. Open on the roughly 6% of teenagers whose stomach pain, anxiety and low mood cluster together and barely budge over a year. Call Yun Tao (Yunnan Normal University). Careful framing: this is observational, so avoid implying resilience programs are proven to prevent anything.
- **Wider angle:** A health feature on the gut–brain relationship in adolescence for a general-interest outlet — why functional gastrointestinal disorders, anxiety and self-harm risk travel together, and how often teenagers with recurrent abdominal pain are never asked about their mood.

**Caveats:** Observational (cannot establish causation); entirely self-report, including gut symptom diagnosis; two waves only, so transition estimates are coarse; single-country sample.

### 5. Transcendental meditation cuts workplace stress in a six-month randomized trial

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 23 September 2026
**PMID:** 42778032 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122555
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a proper randomized trial with six months of follow-up on a much-hyped technique; open-label with no active comparator, so expectancy effects are wide open.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Australian university staff were randomized to Transcendental Meditation training (five days plus regular practice, n=65) or to usual workplace wellbeing resources (n=64), and assessed at baseline, three and six months. The TM group showed a moderate reduction in perceived stress (adjusted mean difference −2.25; Hedges' g = −0.52), plus improvements in anxiety, depression, burnout, sleep quality, wellbeing and health-related quality of life. Exploratory analysis found the twice-daily practitioners accounted for most of the gains.

**Why it matters:** Workplace wellbeing programs have a poor evidence record — a recent large UK analysis found most do nothing — so a six-month randomized result with a moderate effect size is worth examining closely, including its weaknesses.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Against a backdrop of evidence that corporate mindfulness apps and resilience workshops mostly fail, one technique held up for six months. Open on an academic doing 20 minutes at their desk twice a day. Call Barbora de Courten (RMIT) — and ask hard about the missing active comparator, since sitting quietly twice a day may be the whole story. Flag that the dose-response finding is exploratory.
- **Wider angle:** A business or work-culture piece for a specialist outlet on why employers keep buying wellbeing products that don't work, and what a genuinely evidence-graded procurement standard for workplace mental health would look like.

**Caveats:** Open-label with no active control or blinding; all primary outcomes self-reported; single institution; N=129; dose-response analysis exploratory; TM is delivered by a commercial organization, though no industry funding is declared.

### 6. AI-written stories only calm you down if they are both coherent and about you

**Journal:** *Cognition and Emotion* | **Published:** 9 September 2026
**PMID:** 42714089 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02699931.2026.2727136
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a neat, registered component-dismantling experiment with a striking backfire effect; small N and a single session.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Eighty-seven participants were randomized to read AI-generated text in one of three forms: a coherent narrative about their own situation, a coherent narrative that wasn't personalized, or personalized content with no narrative structure. Only the complete version — coherent *and* personalized — reduced state anxiety and lifted positive affect. The personalized-but-fragmented version actually made positive affect worse.

**Why it matters:** "Writing it out" is one of the most commonly recommended emotion regulation tools; this is the first experiment to separate its ingredients, and it suggests that surfacing personal material without organizing it into a story can leave people worse off.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Journaling advice, tested to destruction — and a warning about the half-done version. Open on the AI-generated text itself: a chatbot handed your worries back to you either as a story or as a jumble. Call Bo Wang (Jiangsu Normal University). This also lands squarely in the current debate about chatbots as emotional support, since the manipulation was literally an LLM.
- **Wider angle:** A technology feature on AI mental health tools for a tech-focused outlet: if narrative structure is the active ingredient, chatbots that merely reflect your feelings back at you may be running the condition that backfired.

**Caveats:** Small N (87); single laboratory session with immediate outcomes only; state anxiety and affect self-reported; no follow-up; findings from a Chinese student-age sample.

### 7. Teenagers' emotional habits track their own reading of the family, not their parents'

**Journal:** *Journal of Youth and Adolescence* | **Published:** 17 September 2026
**PMID:** 42753047 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10964-026-02413-x
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a big dyadic dataset with a clean division of labor between closeness and conflict; cross-sectional, so causal direction is unresolved.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Using 1,875 Chinese parent–adolescent dyads (adolescents mean age 13; 76.5% of parents were mothers), researchers used polynomial regression and response surface analysis to test what happens when parent and child agree or disagree about how close and how conflictual their relationship is. Agreed-upon closeness went with more cognitive reappraisal; agreed-upon conflict went with less reappraisal and more expressive suppression. Crucially, teenagers who saw less closeness or more conflict than their parents did reported the worst emotion regulation profile — and the adolescent's own perception mattered more than the parent's throughout, consistently across early, middle and late adolescence.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the mismatch itself — a parent who thinks things are fine while their teenager doesn't — is where the emotional cost sits, and that closeness and conflict shape different regulation strategies rather than being two ends of one scale.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The parents in this study who believed the relationship was warmer than their child did had teenagers who bottled things up. Open on that mismatch. Call Yanfang Li (Beijing Normal University), and ask whether the discrepancy is cause, consequence, or both.
- **Wider angle:** A parenting-science piece for a general-interest outlet on the methodological point beneath the finding: most research on families asks only one person, and response-surface methods are quietly showing how much that distorts the picture.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional (cannot establish causation); self-report on both sides; single-country sample; the cross-age comparison is between-groups, not longitudinal.

### 8. Gay and bisexual women veterans gain more from emotion-skills therapy after military sexual trauma

**Journal:** *Psychological Trauma* | **Published:** September 2026 (Epub 18 December 2025)
**PMID:** 41411035 | **DOI:** 10.1037/tra0002077
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — overturns an assumption that a marginalized subgroup will respond worse to standard therapy; secondary analysis with only 42 sexual minority participants.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** A secondary analysis of a randomized trial comparing Skills Training in Affective and Interpersonal Regulation (STAIR) with present-centered therapy in 161 women veterans who had experienced military sexual trauma, of whom 42 were sexual minority women. Both groups improved on PTSD symptoms, and both did better in STAIR — but sexual minority women improved *more* than heterosexual women on emotion regulation, post-traumatic maladaptive beliefs and depression, despite starting at comparable symptom levels. Therapeutic alliance and satisfaction were high and did not differ by sexual orientation.

**Why it matters:** The default assumption in trauma services is that minority-stress burden blunts treatment response and that bespoke programs are needed; here a standard emotion-regulation therapy worked at least as well, and on some measures better.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** A group widely assumed to need tailored care did better on the off-the-shelf treatment. Open on what STAIR actually involves — learning to name and modulate feelings before touching the trauma memory itself. Call Marylene Cloitre (NYU) or Leslie Morland (VA San Diego). Be careful: this is a subgroup analysis, not a trial designed to answer this question.
- **Wider angle:** A longer piece for a health-policy or veterans-focused outlet on how sexual minority women are counted (or not) in military health research, and why so few trials are powered to say anything about them.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of an existing trial, not pre-specified; small subgroup (n=42); self-reported outcomes; predominantly White US veteran sample.

### 9. Brain scans link punishment sensitivity to a surprising pattern of prefrontal wiring

**Journal:** *Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology & Biological Psychiatry* | **Published:** 10 September 2026
**PMID:** 42722323 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pnpbp.2026.111922
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a decent-sized imaging sample (N=357) combining structure, connectivity and mediation; the key statistical result is a suppression effect, which is hard to explain in 600 words.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** In 357 healthy university students, researchers combined questionnaires, voxel-based morphometry and resting-state functional connectivity. People more sensitive to punishment were worse at cognitive reappraisal — yet they had *more* gray matter in the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, a region central to emotion regulation, and stronger connectivity between that region and the right precuneus. Mediation analysis found this connectivity acted as a statistical suppressor, with direct and indirect effects pushing in opposite directions.

**Why it matters:** It complicates the tidy story that more prefrontal control hardware means better emotional control; here the same circuit that supports reappraisal appears to be enlarged in people who use reappraisal less well, hinting at compensation or overengagement of self-referential processing.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** People who are highly attuned to criticism and punishment have more, not less, tissue in the brain's emotion-control region — and are still worse at reframing. Open on the everyday version: the colleague who replays every negative comment. Call Pan Feng or Tingyong Feng (Southwest University) and ask directly whether this is compensation or a rumination circuit in disguise.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-and-mind feature for a neuroscience-literate outlet on suppression effects in mediation analysis — the statistical phenomenon that makes a brain measure look like it's doing two opposite jobs at once, and how often it gets misread as a mechanism.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and correlational; healthy student sample only; emotion regulation and punishment sensitivity both self-reported questionnaires; no task-based validation of reappraisal ability.

### 10. Bottling up feelings — not spirituality — predicts problematic porn use

**Journal:** *Archives of Sexual Behavior* | **Published:** 21 September 2026
**PMID:** 42766058 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10508-026-03546-7
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a clean null that separates spirituality from religiosity in a well-studied debate; small cross-sectional online sample.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** A general-population online survey of 121 adults (mean age 27, 53.7% male) measured pornography use, problematic use, psychological distress, emotion regulation, moral disapproval, self-esteem and spirituality. Male gender, weekly use frequency, psychological distress and expressive suppression together explained 38.2% of the variance in problematic use. Spirituality, self-esteem and age showed no relationship at all — despite religiosity being one of the best-established predictors in this literature.

**Why it matters:** Much of what gets labeled "porn addiction" reflects moral conflict rather than loss of control; this suggests that conflict is specifically religious rather than broadly spiritual, and points at emotional suppression as a distinct behavioral pathway.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Spiritual but not religious people show no elevated distress about their porn use — the moral-incongruence effect appears to be religion-specific. Pair that with the finding that habitual emotion suppressors report more problems. Call Halley Pontes (Birkbeck), who works on behavioral addiction diagnostics, and ask where the ICD's compulsive sexual behavior disorder sits in all this.
- **Wider angle:** A cultural feature for a general-interest magazine on how "porn addiction" became a diagnosis in the popular imagination, and the researchers arguing that for many people the distress is about values, not compulsion.

**Caveats:** Small N (121); cross-sectional and self-reported throughout; convenience online sample skewing young; no clinical assessment of compulsive sexual behavior.

### 11. Tracking the eyes while making an argument changes whether people act on it

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** 24 September 2026
**PMID:** 42784902 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107881
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — an odd and memorable manipulation with two-week behavioral follow-up, but the moderation only survives when the two memory measures are combined.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** In an online experiment in Germany, 230 participants had their working memory measured (forward and backward digit span), then heard a persuasive audio message about eating more fruit and vegetables. Half simultaneously tracked a dot moving horizontally across the screen — a load intended to occupy working memory and block defensive counterarguing. Two weeks later, self-reported fruit and vegetable consumption differed by working memory profile: people with relatively high forward span benefited most from the eye-movement condition. Tested individually, neither span measure reached significance.

**Why it matters:** It adds evidence that resisting persuasion is an effortful, working-memory-hungry act of self-regulation — and that occupying that capacity changes real behavior weeks later, at least for some people.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the strangeness: following a moving dot with your eyes while listening to health advice made some people eat more vegetables a fortnight later. Open on the honest statistical wobble — the effect appears and disappears depending on how the memory measures are combined — and use it to discuss how we judge borderline findings. Call Arie Dijkstra (Groningen).
- **Wider angle:** A skeptical feature for a psychology or technology outlet on the wider family of "eye movements change your mind" claims, from EMDR to attention-hijacking interface design, and how much of it holds up.

**Caveats:** Outcome is self-reported diet, not measured intake; key interaction non-significant when the two working memory measures are tested separately; online sample; single-message exposure; effect sizes small (η² ≈ 0.05).

### 12. Anxious attachment predicts which children with ADHD stay traumatized a year after an earthquake

**Journal:** *Journal of Attention Disorders* | **Published:** September 2026 (Epub 30 March 2026)
**PMID:** 41910948 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10870547261432423
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — rare longitudinal disaster data in a high-risk group; modest N and self-report instruments.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers followed 124 children diagnosed with ADHD at 3 and 12 months after the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes, measuring attachment style, family functioning, depression, anxiety and PTSD symptoms. Depressive, state anxiety and PTSD symptoms fell over the year while trait anxiety stayed flat. Poor family functioning and insecure attachment — especially anxious attachment — robustly predicted who still had elevated symptoms at 12 months, with avoidant attachment uniquely tied to PTSD. Children on ADHD medication had lower symptom severity across all domains.

**Why it matters:** For a group already prone to emotion regulation difficulties, recovery from disaster looks less like a function of exposure and more like a function of relationships — which argues for family-centered rather than child-only post-disaster care.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Two children survive the same earthquake; a year later one has recovered and the other hasn't, and the best predictor is how the family functions. Open on the 12-month assessment in Adana. Call Serkan Gunes (Adana City Research and Training Hospital). The medication association is observational and should be framed carefully — children on treatment may differ in many ways.
- **Wider angle:** A disaster-response piece for a health or humanitarian outlet on the children who get missed in post-earthquake mental health screening because their symptoms are attributed to a pre-existing diagnosis.

**Caveats:** Small N (124); single center; observational (cannot establish causation), including for the medication finding; no comparison group of children without ADHD; questionnaire-based symptom measures.

### 13. Unit support protects deployed fathers from parental burnout — until combat exposure gets severe

**Journal:** *Psychological Trauma* | **Published:** 31 August 2026
**PMID:** 42671804 | **DOI:** 10.1037/tra0002288
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a clear "protection has a ceiling" result on an under-studied outcome; small, single-context, cross-sectional.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** 105 Israeli military fathers deployed to combat zones completed questionnaires on exposure, emotion regulation strategies, unit and spousal support, and four dimensions of parental burnout. Habitual emotional suppression was associated with more exhaustion, more estrangement from their earlier parenting self, and more emotional distancing from their children. Unit support predicted lower burnout — but only at low and moderate exposure levels; at high exposure the protective association vanished. Marital satisfaction helped mainly among fathers who used cognitive reappraisal less.

**Why it matters:** Social support is usually treated as a general-purpose buffer; this suggests buffers saturate, and that the fathers who most need support are the ones for whom it stops working.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** "Emotional distancing from your own child" is a measurable burnout dimension, and suppression — the coping style combat arguably demands — predicts it. Open on a father returning from deployment and finding he cannot re-enter the role. Call Ofir Ben-Yaakov (Ruppin Academic Center). Frame the moderation honestly as a limit on a buffer, not a causal claim.
- **Wider angle:** A features piece for a general-interest or military-affairs outlet on parental burnout as an emerging construct distinct from depression — what it looks like, who measures it, and why high-demand professions may be producing it at scale.

**Caveats:** Small N (105); cross-sectional (cannot establish causation); all self-reported; fathers only; Israeli military context may not generalize; moderation analyses with multiple outcomes raise multiple-comparison concerns.

### 14. A systematic review maps what prayer, chanting and meditation do to the brain

**Journal:** *Brain and Behavior* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42670176 | **DOI:** 10.1002/brb3.71733
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — first synthesis to span traditions and practice types rather than just meditation; narrative synthesis of heterogeneous, mostly small imaging studies limits how firm the conclusions can be.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** A PRISMA-registered systematic review searched Scopus, PubMed/MEDLINE and ProQuest to June 2026 and synthesized 105 neuroimaging and neurophysiological studies published 1998–2026, spanning Buddhist, Christian, Islamic, Hindu, Santo Daime, Spiritist and Sant Mat traditions, and practices from silent meditation to mediumistic trance. Distinct signatures emerged: meditation suppressed the default mode network and boosted prefrontal activity, prayer engaged social-cognition networks, chanting and recitation produced limbic deactivation with gamma and delta changes, and mystical states showed the most widely distributed profiles. Long-term practitioners showed structural differences.

**Why it matters:** It shifts the conversation from "does meditation change the brain" to the more interesting question of whether different contemplative practices are actually different interventions with different neural targets — and prayer, the most widely practiced of them all, barely features in secular neuroscience.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Meditation gets the research funding, but half the planet prays — and the brain does something quite different when it does. Open on the practice-specific signatures, particularly chanting's limbic deactivation. Call Waqar Husain (COMSATS) or Haitham Jahrami (Arabian Gulf University), and be frank about the quality ceiling: these are mostly small imaging studies with no meta-analysis possible.
- **Wider angle:** A longer cultural-science essay for a general-interest outlet on the secularization of contemplative practice — how mindfulness was extracted from Buddhism and commercialized, while the religious practices it came from went unstudied.

**Caveats:** Review rather than primary research; synthesis without meta-analysis because of heterogeneity; constituent studies largely small, cross-sectional and unblinded; self-selection of long-term practitioners confounds structural findings; an erratum has been published for this paper.

### 15. A psychodynamic online program shifts a personality trait in 10 weeks

**Journal:** *Psychodynamic Psychiatry* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42735080 | **DOI:** 10.1521/pdps.2026.54.3.511
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — first test of internet-delivered psychodynamic therapy against neuroticism, and the mechanism finding is intriguing, but it is an uncontrolled pilot of 30 people.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Thirty adults with mixed anxiety and depressive symptoms completed 10 weeks of therapist-supported internet-delivered affect phobia therapy — a psychodynamic approach centered on tolerating avoided emotions. Self-reported neuroticism on the IPIP-NEO 300 fell by a moderate amount (d = 0.64) and stayed down at six months, with improvements in depression, anxiety, quality of life and emotion regulation. **Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Can you edit a personality trait from your laptop? Open on affect phobia therapy's central premise — that neuroticism is partly a fear of one's own feelings. **Caveats:** Small N (30); no control group; uncontrolled pilot so regression to the mean and expectancy cannot be ruled out; all outcomes self-reported; authors include the developer of the therapy approach (Frederick, Center for Courageous Living).

### 16. A transdiagnostic therapy adapted into Urdu passes its first test in Pakistan

**Journal:** *BMC Psychiatry* | **Published:** 21 September 2026
**PMID:** 42768329 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12888-026-08345-x
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 4/10 — genuinely first-of-its-kind cultural adaptation with large preliminary effects, but it is a feasibility pilot against a waitlist.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Fifty-one adults in Pakistan with diagnosed anxiety and/or depressive disorders were randomized to a 14-week Urdu-adapted Unified Protocol — an emotion-focused, transdiagnostic CBT designed to treat multiple disorders with one set of skills — or a waitlist. Retention was 82.4%, homework completion 79%, and satisfaction high. Preliminary clinical effects were large (anxiety g = 1.18; depression g = 1.15), and qualitative feedback emphasized new emotional awareness.

**Why it matters:** Transdiagnostic protocols are attractive for low-resource settings because one training covers many diagnoses; this shows the Unified Protocol survives translation into Urdu with high engagement, which is the usual failure point.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** One therapy, many diagnoses — and the cultural adaptation work that decides whether it travels. Open on a participant describing emotional awareness as a new concept. Call Asma Nisa (NUST Islamabad). Be explicit that this is a feasibility trial: the large effect sizes come from a waitlist comparison and should not be read as efficacy.
- **Wider angle:** A global-health feature on the "adaptation versus fidelity" tension — how much of a Western manualized therapy can you change before it stops being the tested intervention, for a health-policy or development outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (51); waitlist control, which inflates apparent effects; single center; retrospectively registered; self-reported clinical outcomes; feasibility endpoints were the primary aim.

### 17. Putting people in a bad mood reveals different brain networks in heavy internet users

**Journal:** *Brain Imaging and Behavior* | **Published:** 23 September 2026
**PMID:** 42773324 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11682-026-01201-2
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a smart design that tests the brain in the state that actually triggers the behavior, undercut by a very small sample and partly preliminary results.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Twenty people with problematic internet use and 20 matched controls had resting-state fMRI scans before and after a negative mood induction, with independent component analysis used to compare within-network connectivity. Mood induction worked in both groups, but the problematic-use group showed distinct shifts in four networks, including the default mode and dorsal attention networks; the prefrontal-parietal default mode and dorsal attention effects survived permutation testing, while the limbic and temporo-sensorimotor findings did not.

**Why it matters:** Most imaging of problematic internet use scans people at rest in a neutral state — but the behavior is triggered by feeling bad, so testing the brain under induced negative mood is arguably the right experiment; the result is a modest hint that the difference is state-dependent rather than fixed.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Scanning people while calm may have been the wrong approach all along — the differences show up when you make them feel bad first. Open on the mood induction itself, in the scanner. Call Seçil Tunalı Aslan (Yeditepe University). Keep the framing exploratory: 40 people, and half the effects didn't survive stricter testing.
- **Wider angle:** A methods piece for a neuroscience-focused outlet on the limits of resting-state fMRI in psychiatry, and the move toward "state-dependent" scanning that probes the brain in the condition where the symptom actually happens.

**Caveats:** Very small N (40); single center; cross-sectional group comparison (cannot establish causation); limbic and temporo-sensorimotor findings did not survive permutation testing and are described by the authors as preliminary; problematic internet use defined by self-report.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- **Expressive suppression keeps turning up as the cost-bearing strategy.** The new batch links habitual suppression to problematic pornography use (PMID 42766058), to parental burnout and emotional distancing in deployed Israeli fathers (42671804), and to teenagers who perceive more conflict than their parents do (42753047). That extends the previous digest's cluster: mothers' suppression predicting toddler screen time (42447742), suppression as the downstream product of momentary attachment anxiety in depressed and anxious patients (41885910), and agreeable people suppressing tears at a friend's bad news (42537321).
- **Moment-to-moment state beats stable trait.** The suicidal ideation EMA study — risk rises when impulsivity exceeds a person's *own* average, with trait impulsivity showing nothing (42778027) — is the sharpest version yet of what the previous digest found in depressed adults' experience sampling (42660575), in the dynamic modeling of disclosure and suppression loops (41885910) and in the polyregulation data (41806361).
- **Reappraisal's awkward status persists.** The imaging study in which punishment-sensitive people have *more* right dlPFC gray matter yet reappraise worse (42722323), and the finding that marital satisfaction helps mainly the fathers who reappraise less (42671804), sit alongside last run's repeated reappraisal nulls (42660575, 42413746, 42447742).
- **Family relationships, not exposure history, predict outcomes.** Family functioning and anxious attachment predicting which children with ADHD were still symptomatic a year after the Türkiye earthquakes (41910948), and caregiver distress as the lever on violence against children (42754240), echo the previous digest's refugee finding that post-migration social stress — not trauma exposure — severed people from interpersonal regulation (42619365).

**Advances**
- **From measuring co-regulation to intervening on it.** The previous digest was dominated by observation — second-by-second validation coding in couples (42441643), home audio of mothers (41642821), infant–parent heart rate coupling (42269373). This batch delivers the trial versions: a randomized parenting program that halved physical violence against children by treating caregivers' own distress (42754240), and an Urdu-adapted Unified Protocol feasibility RCT (42768329).
- **Contemplative practice moves up the evidence ladder — and widens.** Last run had uncontrolled VR mindfulness in children (41484577) and HRV biofeedback imaging (42669395); this run has a six-month randomized Transcendental Meditation trial (42778032) plus the first synthesis to put prayer, chanting and trance alongside meditation (42670176).
- **"Just-in-time" intervention gets a target.** The previous digest's wider angle on goal-sensitive app interventions (42660575) now has a candidate trigger signal: within-person deviation in impulsivity (42778027).
- **State-dependent measurement.** Scanning problematic internet users before and after a negative mood induction (42773324) advances the methodological argument running through last run's lab-versus-life mismatch in functional neurological disorder (42704885).

**Counterpoints**
- **Self-report reasserts itself.** Almost every finding here rests on questionnaires — including violence rates (42754240) and Rome IV gut symptoms (42749162) — which is precisely what the previous digest's preregistered FND study warned about, where reported dysfunction vanished under laboratory measurement (42704885).
- **Expression alone is not enough.** The AI-narrative dismantling experiment found that personalized material without narrative structure made positive affect *worse* (42714089), complicating the previous digest's broadly positive picture of disclosure and social sharing (41885910).
- **Tailoring may be over-prescribed.** Sexual minority women veterans improved *more* than heterosexual women on a standard emotion-skills therapy (41411035), against the minority-stress expectation that marginalized groups respond less well to off-the-shelf treatment.

**New themes**
- **Intergroup and political emotion regulation**, with disgust rather than empathy as the operative channel (42723350) — no analogue in the previous digest.
- **Large language models as both tool and experimental manipulation** in emotion regulation research (42714089), and smartphone sensing as suicide risk infrastructure (42778027).
- **Global mental health delivery** as its own strand: lay-delivered parenting in a displacement setting (42754240), cultural adaptation in Pakistan (42768329).
- **Deliberate personality change** — neuroticism moving in 10 weeks of internet-delivered affect phobia therapy, mediated by defense maturity (42735080).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch independently find that psychological buffers have ceilings, and that they fail exactly where need is greatest. Resilience in 9,035 Chinese adolescents strongly reduced the odds of sliding into somatic-emotional distress but did nothing to pull out anyone already in it (PMID 42749162); unit support protected deployed fathers from parental burnout at low and moderate combat exposure and stopped working at high exposure (42671804); meta-humanization softened partisan hostility most at *moderate* perceived threat to social cohesion (42723350); and AI-generated text helped only when both coherent and personalized, with the half-done version leaving people with worse positive affect (42714089).

**Why pitch this now:** Resilience training, peer support and wellbeing tools are being rolled out across schools, militaries and workplaces on the implicit assumption that more protection helps more when things are worse — and four independent designs this month say the opposite, which also reframes why a caregiver program that directly treated distress (42754240) achieved what generic buffering does not.

**Angle:** A feature on the dose–response problem in psychological protection — how prevention science quietly assumes linearity, what saturation and backfire effects look like in real data, and why the people the programs are marketed to help are often outside the range where they work. For readers who have sat through a resilience workshop or been handed a wellbeing app at work.

**Potential headlines:**
