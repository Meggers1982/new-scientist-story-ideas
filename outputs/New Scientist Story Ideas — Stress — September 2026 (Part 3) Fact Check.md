### Study 1: Anxiety disorders and PTSD are linked to a higher risk of stroke, in a pooled analysis of 11 million people
**PMID:** 42767516 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Sample (23 cohorts, 11,181,338 participants), HRs (1.29 anxiety = 29%; 1.50 PTSD = 50%), persistence after adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors and depression, and the OCD single-study caveat all check out.

**Issue A. Unsupported comparison with depression** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The surprise is that PTSD outperformed depression — the usual psychiatric suspect in cardiovascular risk"
- **Abstract says:** "Associations persisted after adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors and depression." No pooled effect estimate for depression is reported, and no head-to-head comparison is described.
- **Problem:** The meta-analysis adjusted *for* depression; it did not estimate depression's own stroke hazard, so it cannot show PTSD "outperforming" it. A writer taking this hook to an editor would be pitching a result the paper does not contain.
- **Suggested fix:** "The surprise is that the PTSD association survived adjustment for depression — the usual psychiatric suspect in cardiovascular risk — suggesting it is not simply depression in disguise."

**Issue B. Hemorrhagic stroke framed as a null** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the association held specifically for ischemic (not hemorrhagic) stroke"
- **Abstract says:** "Associations were observed for ischaemic stroke (HR 1.34, 95% CI 1.13-1.58), whereas haemorrhagic stroke did not reach statistical significance (HR 1.24, 95% CI 0.95-1.62)."
- **Problem:** The hemorrhagic point estimate is similar in size; it simply failed to reach significance, likely a power issue. "Not hemorrhagic" implies a demonstrated absence of effect.
- **Suggested fix:** "the association was statistically significant for ischemic stroke, while the hemorrhagic estimate was similar in size but did not reach significance"

**Issue C. Named confounders not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the link survived adjustment for blood pressure, smoking and the rest"
- **Abstract says:** "adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors and depression" (unspecified).
- **Problem:** Which specific risk factors were adjusted for is unverifiable from abstract only; naming blood pressure and smoking is an assumption.
- **Suggested fix:** "the link survived adjustment for conventional cardiovascular risk factors (check the paper for the specific covariate list)"

*NS fit 7/10:* defensible — 11 million participants pooled from longitudinal cohorts, with the observational limit explicitly priced in.

### Study 2: Stress hormones show up in how other people rate your working relationships — not in how you rate them yourself
**PMID:** 42667999 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

All counts (46 officers, four waves, 2126 sessions, 732 supervisees, 432 self-rated), both directional effects (log AUCg → Caring/Fairness; flatter slope → Trust), and the self-report null are reproduced correctly.

**Issue A. Causal verb in "Why it matters"** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It suggests physiological stress degrades the skilled, effortful part of professional interaction"
- **Abstract says:** "HPA dysregulation covaries with what independent observers see in professional relational behavior but not with what the professional perceives."
- **Problem:** "Degrades" asserts causation from a within-person correlational design. The pitch angle correctly says "this is covariation, not proof that cortisol causes worse behavior" — but the body text contradicts that caution, and the body is what an editor reads first.
- **Suggested fix:** "It suggests physiological stress covaries with the skilled, effortful part of professional interaction — and does so invisibly to the person doing it"

**Issue B. NS fit score** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "NS fit: 7/10"
- **Problem:** The unit of analysis for the cortisol predictor is 46 professionals in a single occupational setting, with no experimental control; the 2126 sessions inflate the apparent sample. The observer-rated (rather than self-reported) outcome is a genuine strength, which keeps this from being a serious overscore.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10.

### Study 3: A close bond with parents fails to protect stressed teenagers' response to reward
**PMID:** 42773853 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=165, ages 9-16, 78.18% White, the three measurement modalities, and the contrary-to-prediction blunted monetary RewP and reduced behavioral reward sensitivity are all accurate. Caveats correctly note the non-significant interactions.

**Issue A. "Overturns prior research" overstates a single contrary-to-hypothesis result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research" / headline "A close bond with parents fails to protect stressed teenagers' response to reward"
- **Abstract says:** "The results suggest that high-quality PCR may *not uniformly* buffer against the effects of peer stress exposure and highlight differential effects for different PVS measures."
- **Problem:** The finding was contrary to the authors' own predictions on two of several outcome measures, with no significant interaction elsewhere. That is a qualification of the buffering hypothesis, not an overturning of it, and the headline's flat "fails to protect" generalizes beyond the two measures that moved.
- **Suggested fix:** Novelty: "Contrary to prediction." Headline: "A close bond with parents doesn't uniformly protect stressed teenagers' response to reward."

### Study 4: Treating PTSD reduces physical pain — even when no one treats the pain
**PMID:** 42766718 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=196, the three randomized arms, 52 weeks, the n=70 / n=93 subgroups, and the PE+PLB advantage on pain interference are all correct. Industry and foundation relationships are properly flagged in Caveats.

**Issue A. Causal headline from a secondary analysis with no pain-only or no-treatment control** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Treating PTSD reduces physical pain — even when no one treats the pain"
- **Abstract says:** "While it is uncertain reduced pain was due to the treatments themselves, lower scores for all treatments suggest that PTSD-focused treatments *may* reduce pain even without a specific pain focus."
- **Problem:** The authors explicitly decline to attribute the pain reduction to treatment, because all arms improved and there is no control for the passage of time. The headline states as established what the paper states as uncertain. The Caveats note this, but the headline is the pitch.
- **Suggested fix:** "Pain fell during PTSD treatment — even though no one was treating the pain"

### Study 5: Post-traumatic stress symptoms predict who moves from suicidal thoughts to an attempt
**PMID:** 42705422 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. N=923, mean age 36.2, 65.2% women, 6-monthly follow-up over 18 months, 86% retention, 11.5% (106) attempts, PTSD HR 1.264, smaller depressive HR, and the negative OCD association (HR 0.960) all match. Framing is appropriately hedged, the attenuation by lifetime attempt history is carried into Caveats, and the suicide-reporting guidance is sound.

### Study 6: About half of postpartum depression cases don't lift within the first year
**PMID:** 42749161 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=191, 12-month repeated measures, 4.7% and 25.6% figures, and the persistent-case risk correlates are all accurate.

**Issue A. Baby blues + PMS signature attributed to the wrong trajectory group** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Note the hormonal-sensitivity signature — baby blues plus PMS — as an observational pattern, not a test."
- **Abstract says:** "In contrast, the *recovering* cases reported frequent baby blues and PMS, which can be understood as proxy indicators of a heightened hormonal sensitivity."
- **Problem:** The digest body lists PMS among the *persistent* case markers (correct) and then invokes the baby-blues-plus-PMS signature without saying it characterized the *recovering* group. A writer could easily pitch it as a persistence marker, reversing the prognostic direction.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note the hormonal-sensitivity signature — frequent baby blues plus PMS — which marked the *recovering* cases, not the persistent ones; an observational pattern, not a test."

**Issue B. "Overturns prior research" is too strong** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** "its long-term progression and the underlying mechanisms of its different trajectories remain poorly understood."
- **Problem:** The paper fills a gap in trajectory data rather than contradicting established findings; the 4-6 week framing is described as incomplete, not wrong.
- **Suggested fix:** Novelty: "Extends prior research / new trajectory data."

### Study 7: Inflammation in the body changes what brain-clearance markers mean in depressed teenage girls
**PMID:** 42777933 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=461 MDD and 253 controls, the multimodal glymphatic markers, the higher SII, and the sex-specific SII × choroid plexus volume moderation are all faithfully reported, as is the "mechanistic lead, not clinical" framing.

**Issue A. Direction of the perivascular diffusion finding is left vague** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Depressed adolescents showed altered perivascular diffusion, higher free water and a higher systemic immune-inflammation index."
- **Abstract says:** "adolescents with MDD exhibited a significantly *elevated* diffusion tensor imaging along the perivascular space index, increased free water fraction, and higher systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) levels."
- **Problem:** "Altered" obscures the direction. An elevated DTI-ALPS index is conventionally read as *greater* perivascular diffusivity, which sits awkwardly with the "glymphatic dysfunction" framing a writer would reach for; the vagueness invites a wrong-direction pitch.
- **Suggested fix:** "Depressed adolescents showed an elevated perivascular diffusion (DTI-ALPS) index, higher free water and a higher systemic immune-inflammation index."

### Study 8: Childhood trauma shows up in teenagers' sleep years later — and perceived stress is the route
**PMID:** 42785565 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=284, the baseline/1.5-year measurement structure, the mediation by perceived stress after controlling for baseline sleep, and the male-only RSA-suppression moderation (weaker stress–sleep link at higher suppression) are all correct.

**Issue A. Unverifiable task detail** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "how hard a teenager's vagus nerve pulls back during a three-minute speech task"
- **Abstract says:** "a speech task during which their data of vagal regulation indexed by respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) suppression was obtained at baseline." No duration given.
- **Problem:** The three-minute figure is Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** Drop the duration or confirm from full text: "how hard a teenager's vagus nerve pulls back during a stressful speech task".

**Issue B. "Years later" and "is the route"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Childhood trauma shows up in teenagers' sleep years later — and perceived stress is the route"
- **Abstract says:** Follow-up was "1.5 years later"; perceived stress "mediated this association" in an observational design.
- **Problem:** "Years later" inflates an 18-month gap (the body text gets it right), and "is the route" states a causal pathway that statistical mediation on observational data cannot establish.
- **Suggested fix:** "Childhood trauma shows up in teenagers' sleep 18 months later — and perceived stress tracks the link"

### Study 9: People who had tough childhoods react hardest to stress when they quit smoking
**PMID:** 42771860 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Group sizes (49 ad libitum, 69 withdrawal, 45 non-smokers), randomization to smoking condition, the two identical sessions with speech preparation/delivery and mental arithmetic, and the significant group × ELA interaction for SBP are all accurate. Caveats correctly note that the adversity comparison is observational.

**Issue A. Specificity claim goes beyond the reported contrast** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "but only in the withdrawal group, not in ad libitum smokers or non-smokers" / "adversity did nothing to stress reactivity until nicotine was withdrawn, and then it did a lot"
- **Abstract says:** "The effect of having ≥1 ELA event on SBP changes in the withdrawal group was significantly greater than that in non-smokers (fixed effect = 3.89; 95% CI = 1.41 to 6.36; P=0.002)."
- **Problem:** Only the withdrawal-versus-non-smoker contrast is reported. Whether ad libitum smokers differed is not stated, and "did nothing" asserts a null that the abstract never reports. "Did a lot" also overstates a ~3.9 mmHg fixed effect.
- **Suggested fix:** "Exposure to at least one early-life adversity was associated with larger systolic blood-pressure responses to stress in the withdrawal group than in non-smokers (about 3.9 mmHg). Check the full text before claiming ad libitum smokers showed no effect."

**Issue B. "Markedly larger"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "markedly larger systolic blood-pressure responses to stress"
- **Abstract says:** "fixed effect = 3.89; 95% CI = 1.41 to 6.36"
- **Problem:** A ~4 mmHg difference in stress reactivity is statistically clear but modest; "markedly" oversells it.
- **Suggested fix:** "measurably larger systolic blood-pressure responses to stress (about 4 mmHg)"

*Note:* al'Absi's University of Minnesota Duluth affiliation is Unverifiable from abstract only (no affiliations listed for this record).

### Study 10: Female footballers' anxiety and depression symptoms peak in the middle of the season
**PMID:** 42759733 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=406, six amateur and five elite German clubs, the age bands, four time points, the 20-30% / 15-25% threshold figures, and the latent growth finding of stability plus significant linear and quadratic trends peaking mid-season are all correct. The "screeners, not diagnoses" caution is well placed.

**Issue A. Authors' speculation presented as finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "in the unglamorous middle, when academic pressure, accumulated physical strain and performance expectations overlap"
- **Abstract says:** "players *might* experience increased performance pressure, academic stress, or accumulated physical strain."
- **Problem:** The abstract offers these as untested explanations for the mid-season peak; the digest presents them as the established mechanism.
- **Suggested fix:** "in the unglamorous middle — a period the authors speculate may combine academic pressure, accumulated physical strain and performance expectations, though the study did not test that"

### Study 11: Group therapy run inside a war zone proves workable for Ukrainian outpatients
**PMID:** 42731584 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=56, five groups (3 face-to-face, 2 online), retention 96.4%, dropout 3.6%, adherence 93%, and the effect sizes (PHQ-9 d=1.44; SF-36 Mental Health d=1.28; PCL-5 d=1.18; SSS-8 d=1.16) all match. The uncontrolled-design caution is stated in both the pitch and the Caveats.

**Issue A. "Proves" in the headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Group therapy run inside a war zone proves workable for Ukrainian outpatients"
- **Abstract says:** "This pilot shows the implementation of Empowerment group therapy is feasible in Ukrainian outpatients during an ongoing war," with progression to an RCT recommended.
- **Problem:** "Proves" is stronger than a 56-person single-arm feasibility pilot supports, even where progression criteria were met.
- **Suggested fix:** "Group therapy run inside a war zone looks deliverable for Ukrainian outpatients, a pilot finds"

**Issue B. Face-to-face versus online comparison** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the in-person groups outperformed the online ones despite the risk of getting there"
- **Abstract says:** "Improvements were generally larger in face-to-face groups," and the conclusion calls for future work "to clarify optimal delivery conditions for in-person versus online formats."
- **Problem:** Participants were not randomized to format and no statistical comparison is reported; "outperformed" reads as a tested difference. The "Why it matters" hedges with "may beat," but the pitch angle does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "improvements were generally larger in the in-person groups, though format was not randomized and the difference wasn't formally tested"

### Study 13: Toddlers' sleep splits into two types — and bedtime struggles with parents mark the difference
**PMID:** 42764403 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=320 Canadian children aged 1-5, parent-reported online questionnaire, the two latent profiles (59% / 41%) and their dimensional contrasts, the income OR of 2.05, and the 21% lower odds per unit of problematic parent-child sleep interaction in preschoolers are all accurate.

**Issue A. "Toddlers" mislabels the age range** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Toddlers' sleep splits into two types"
- **Abstract says:** "320 children aged 1-5 years," with the parent-child sleep interaction analysis restricted to "preschoolers aged 3-5 years only."
- **Problem:** The headline's "toddlers" covers only part of a 1-5 range, and the interaction finding it pairs with applies to the preschool subgroup only. The Caveats flag the second half, but the headline still misstates the population.
- **Suggested fix:** "Young children's sleep splits into two types — and bedtime struggles with parents mark the difference in preschoolers"

**Issue B. Causal verb for a cross-sectional association** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "each one-unit increase in problematic parent-child sleep interaction cut the odds of good-sleeper membership by 21%"
- **Abstract says:** "each one-unit increase in problematic interactions was *associated with* 21% lower likelihood of Sleepy Koalas membership."
- **Problem:** "Cut" implies the interaction produced the change; the design cannot separate cause from consequence (poor sleepers may generate more bedtime conflict).
- **Suggested fix:** "each one-unit increase in problematic parent-child sleep interaction was associated with 21% lower odds of good-sleeper membership"

**Issue C. Unsupported ranking of correlates** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the strongest modifiable correlate is the nightly negotiation at bedtime"
- **Abstract says:** Reports the income and parent-child interaction associations but does not rank correlates by strength or label any as modifiable.
- **Problem:** "Strongest modifiable correlate" is Unverifiable from abstract only and reads as a hierarchy the paper does not present.
- **Suggested fix:** "one of the few correlates a parent could plausibly change is the nightly negotiation at bedtime"

### Study 14: "Pregnancy brain" tracks mood, not pregnancy — and exercise softens the link
**PMID:** 42764724 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=56, the 28-30 week and 6-week postpartum timepoints, the mood–cognitive failures association, and the absence of a direct PA–cognition association are all correct. Caveats commendably flag that the moderation findings were exploratory and inconsistent in direction.

**Issue A. "Not pregnancy" is untestable in this design** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "'Pregnancy brain' tracks mood, not pregnancy" / "the forgetfulness women report in pregnancy is better predicted by mood than by pregnancy per se"
- **Abstract says:** "Higher prenatal mood symptoms were associated with greater prenatal and postpartum cognitive failures." All 56 participants were pregnant; there is no non-pregnant comparison group.
- **Problem:** With no non-pregnant control, the study cannot compare mood against pregnancy as competing explanations. It shows that within pregnant women, mood symptoms track cognitive failures — a much narrower claim than the headline.
- **Suggested fix:** "'Pregnancy brain' tracks mood symptoms — and exercise may soften the link"

**Issue B. Headline states an exploratory, inconsistent moderation as established** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "and exercise softens the link"
- **Abstract says:** "*exploratory analyses* examined whether PA modified relationships"; the moderation ran in the expected direction for depressive symptoms in the meet-PA group, but for anxiety symptoms the lower rate was in the *not* meeting postpartum PA guidelines group.
- **Problem:** Half the moderation evidence runs the opposite way. The Caveats say so; the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "— and meeting activity guidelines may soften part of it (exploratory)"

### Study 15: Feeling that your life has gone off-course drains the good feelings, but doesn't add bad ones
**PMID:** 42765820 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The three-study structure, sample sizes (460 / 224 / 325), the non-significant hypothesized interactions in Studies 1 and 3, the positive-affect-only moderation in Study 2, and the overall conclusion are all reported accurately — including the nulls, which is to the digest's credit.

**Issue A. Causal language for a measured individual difference** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Feeling that your life has gone off-course drains the good feelings, but doesn't add bad ones" / "feeling unmoored from who you were doesn't make you miserable, it makes you flat"
- **Abstract says:** "Derailment was most consistently *related to* diminished positive affect."
- **Problem:** Derailment was measured, never manipulated — only stress and feedback valence were experimentally varied. "Drains" and "makes you flat" assert a causal effect of derailment on affect that none of the three designs can support. This is not flagged in Caveats.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Feeling that your life has gone off-course goes with fewer good feelings — not more bad ones." Pitch: "feeling unmoored from who you were isn't linked to more misery, but to less pleasure."

*NS fit 4/10:* defensible and appropriately modest given the nulls.

### Study 16: Chronic stress is the single best lever against teenage self-harm — but the route differs by gender
**PMID:** 42788378 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=2114, the Ising network and in silico intervention method, the gendered pathways (dysfunctional cognitive reappraisal → NSSI behaviors in females; emotional suppression → NSSI functions in males), and chronic stress as the top simulated target all match the abstract. The pitch and Caveats are admirably firm that nothing was actually intervened upon.

**Issue A. Headline states a simulated result as a demonstrated intervention effect** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Chronic stress is the single best lever against teenage self-harm"
- **Abstract says:** "chronic stress emerged as the most effective universal intervention target, producing the greatest NSSI reduction across genders" — in *in silico* interventions run on cross-sectional network models.
- **Problem:** "Is the single best lever" reads as an established treatment finding. Nothing was reduced in anyone; a node was knocked out in a model built from one-time questionnaire data. The body and Caveats hedge, but the headline is the pitch, and it claims an intervention result.
- **Suggested fix:** "Simulations point to chronic stress as the top target for teenage self-harm — but the route differs by gender"

**Issue B. "Teenage" / "teenager" for a university-student sample** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "teenage self-harm"; "no one has yet done this intervention in a real teenager"
- **Abstract says:** "2114 Chinese university students" (the paper's own title says "adolescent").
- **Problem:** The sample is university students, most of whom will be over 19. The Caveats get this right; the headline and pitch do not.
- **Suggested fix:** "young people's self-harm" / "in a real student".

### Study 17: Gender outranks every psychological factor in predicting teenage gaming disorder
**PMID:** 42732833 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=6573, four algorithms with random forest selected, the SHAP ranking order, and the network centrality of anxiety, academic stress and health adaptation are all reproduced correctly. The caution against reading individual-level prediction into the ML framing is well judged.

**Issue A. The pitch hook rests on an unverifiable absence** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The hook is what the model *didn't* rank highly: nothing about games themselves."
- **Abstract says:** The candidate features are described as "demographic and psychosocial factors" — early adversity, negative life events, psychological symptoms, regulatory emotional self-efficacy. No gaming-behavior variables (playtime, genre, spend) are mentioned as inputs.
- **Problem:** If gaming-behavior variables were never entered into the model, their absence from the SHAP ranking is a design choice, not a finding — and the pitch's entire hook collapses. Unverifiable from abstract only; must be checked in the full text before this angle is pitched.
- **Suggested fix:** "The hook is the shape of the ranking: after gender, it is depression, insomnia, social anxiety and emotional abuse — a distress profile. (Check whether gaming-behaviour variables were candidate features at all before framing this as an absence.)"

### Study 18: People start therapy when their symptoms spike — not when their lives get harder
**PMID:** 42732668 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=1417, up to seven annual assessments, the within/between-person disaggregation, and the concurrent-plus-next-year association of within-person symptom increases with treatment use are all accurate. The digest is honest that the main result is confirmatory.

**Issue A. "Clean null" overstates an inconsistent result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "an unusually long within-person design with a clean null on stressors"
- **Abstract says:** "Fluctuations in family and friend support and self-reported stressors were *inconsistently* associated with treatment use controlling for depressive symptoms."
- **Problem:** "Inconsistently associated" means some associations appeared and some did not — not a clean null. The pitch angle's "did not reliably send people into treatment" is fine; the NS fit line is not.
- **Suggested fix:** "an unusually long within-person design in which stressors and support related only inconsistently to treatment use"

**Issue B. "Start therapy" versus treatment use** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "People start therapy when their symptoms spike"
- **Abstract says:** "individuals are more likely to *use* treatment when they are experiencing higher levels of symptoms than they usually experience."
- **Problem:** The outcome is annual treatment use, which mixes initiation with continuation; the study does not isolate the moment of entry into care.
- **Suggested fix:** "People are in therapy when their symptoms spike — not when their lives get harder"

**Cross-cutting note for the desk:** the recurring failure mode in this digest is not bad arithmetic — the numbers are almost uniformly right — but headlines that assert causation or intervention effects (Studies 4, 14, 15, 16) while the body text and Caveats correctly hedge. Editors read headlines first. Where the Caveats already say "cannot establish causation," the headline should not say "reduces," "drains," "is the best lever," or "tracks mood, not pregnancy."
