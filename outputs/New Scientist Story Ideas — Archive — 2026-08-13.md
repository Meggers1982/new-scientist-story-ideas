### 1. Feeling love and empathy is linked to fewer opioid relapses in people on methadone

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychoactive Drugs* | **Published:** 2026 Aug 7
**PMID:** 42566249 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02791072.2026.2710067
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Counterintuitive finding (love and empathy protecting against opioid use; social support backfiring) with a decent human sample and a strong societal hook around the opioid crisis, offset slightly by cross-sectional design and self-reported outcomes.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 189 adults receiving methadone treatment for opioid use disorder (OUD) at community programs in New Jersey and Utah, measuring self-reported opioid use alongside empathy, love, pain, anxiety, depression, social support, and stigma. Using zero-inflated Poisson regression, higher scores on love and empathy were significantly associated with fewer episodes of opioid use even after adjusting for covariates. Notably, social support was unexpectedly linked to greater opioid use — the opposite of what was predicted. Participants also reported lower levels of love and empathy compared to normative samples.

**Why it matters:** The findings suggest that prosocial emotions like love and empathy may tap into the same brain pathways as opioids, hinting that interventions targeting these emotional states could complement existing medication-based treatments for OUD.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Love and empathy may be as protective against opioid relapse as social support — and social support may backfire" Feeling love and empathy is associated with fewer opioid relapses in people on methadone — but having more social support was, counterintuitively, linked to more drug use. A new study of 189 people on methadone treatment found that those who felt greater love and empathy used opioids less frequently — consistent with the 'Brain Opioid Theory of Social Attachment', which holds that our brains process social bonding using the same pathways that opioids hijack. Strikingly, more social support was linked to more opioid use, possibly because social networks in this population include other people who use drugs. The finding raises the possibility that therapies cultivating prosocial emotion — rather than just social connection in general — could complement medication treatment. Why it fits: NS Mind readers drawn to the psychology of addiction, relationships, and emotion will find the idea that love literally occupies the same neural real estate as opioids both surprising and deeply human.

**The study:** Researchers recorded intracranial brain activity (electrocorticography, ECoG) from 10 epilepsy patients (3 male, 7 female; mean age 27.3) while they imagined familiar melodies presented in different musical keys. Using a relative-pitch decoding framework — focusing on intervals between notes rather than absolute pitch — machine-learning models could reconstruct melodic contours from neural signals at above-chance accuracy. While single-note decoding was modest, sequentially integrating decoded notes preserved the recognisable structure of the imagined melody, providing a proof-of-concept that internally generated music can be partially read from brain recordings.

**Why it matters:** Decoding internally imagined sound could one day help restore communication for people who can hear internally but cannot speak, and sheds light on how the brain constructs subjective auditory experience.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Scientists can reconstruct the tune playing silently in your head from brain activity alone" For the first time, researchers have partially decoded imagined melodies directly from human brain recordings — without the person making a single sound. Ten patients awaiting epilepsy surgery agreed to imagine familiar tunes while electrodes on their brain surface recorded neural activity. A machine-learning system trained on those signals could reconstruct enough melodic structure to identify the contour of the imagined song — not by detecting absolute notes, but by picking up on the relative steps between them. The finding is a proof-of-concept for 'auditory brain-computer interfaces' and raises profound questions about the nature of inner hearing. Why it fits: Music, imagination, and the inner life of the mind are core NS Mind territory, and this study opens a window into one of neuroscience's most intimate questions: what does your brain actually do when a song plays in your head?
