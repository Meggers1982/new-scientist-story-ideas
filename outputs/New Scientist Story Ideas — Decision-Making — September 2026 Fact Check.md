### Study 1: People reject the utilitarian answer when asked to settle a group's disagreements
**PMID:** 42759227 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Dominance claim extended to the utilitarian algorithm** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "People strongly preferred the contractualist solutions, which beat egalitarian ones across parameter settings and beat the utilitarian default outright."
- **Abstract says:** "participants strongly prefer the aggregations recommended by the contractualist algorithm, which also dominates the egalitarian approach across a range of parameterizations."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports formal dominance only over the egalitarian approach. The utilitarian comparison is stated as a preference finding, not a quantified across-parameter win, so "outright" adds a level of certainty the abstract does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "People strongly preferred the contractualist solutions, which dominated the egalitarian approach across a range of parameterizations and were preferred over the utilitarian default."

**Issue B. NS fit score** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 8/10"
- **Abstract says:** No sample sizes, no participant demographics, vignette-based social decision scenarios; LLM comparison reported only as "important misalignment."
- **Problem:** An 8 is a high score for a paper whose N, population and effect sizes are entirely absent from the abstract, and whose outcome is stated preference over vignettes. The idea is strong, but the evidence base as reported is thin.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 7/10 until sample sizes are confirmed from the full text.

### Study 2: Saying "um" doesn't dent your reputation — but it does cost you the conversation
**PMID:** 42691919 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Expert/novice result recast as a benefit of hesitating** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the cost is local and specific, and it flips to a bonus if the listener already thinks you know your stuff."
- **Abstract says:** "speakers who produced filled pauses were rated as less ready and less certain than speakers who did not (even though experts who used filled pauses were more likely to be considered careful than novices; Experiment 3)."
- **Problem:** The comparison is between experts and novices who both used filled pauses — not between an expert who hesitates and an expert who does not. Nothing in the abstract shows filled pauses *benefit* an expert speaker; in the same experiment, filled-pause speakers were still judged less ready and less certain. "Flips to a bonus" is the more exciting result a reader would assume, not the one reported.
- **Suggested fix:** "the cost is local and specific — and experts who hesitate are judged more careful than novices who hesitate in the same way."

**Issue B. Warmth and "which disfluencies"** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "This work suggests which disfluencies buy warmth and which quietly erode trust — a design story about the sound of machine speech."
- **Abstract says:** "filled pauses did not affect the overall perceived competence, trustworthiness, or warmth of the speaker as a person (Experiment 1)."
- **Problem:** Two errors in one clause. Warmth was explicitly unaffected, so no disfluency in this study "bought" warmth; and only one category of disfluency (English filled pauses *um*/*uh*) was tested, so the study cannot discriminate between types of disfluency.
- **Suggested fix:** "This work suggests that filled pauses leave overall impressions of warmth and competence untouched while eroding trust within a single conversation — a design story about the sound of machine speech."

### Study 3: Being told you're wrong when you felt sure makes you doubt yourself for a long while afterward
**PMID:** 42734967 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Claimed spillover to unrelated decisions** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the damage from being corrected isn't to your answer, it's to your confidence system — and it spills over to unrelated decisions."
- **Abstract says:** "Participants exhibit significantly lower confidence following metacognitive conflict... this behavioral adjustment was smaller in blocks with a high versus low proportion of metacognitive conflict trials, and confidence was generally reduced in blocks with a high proportion of metacognitive conflict."
- **Problem:** All reported effects are next-trial adjustments and block-level shifts within the same perceptual decision task. No transfer to a different or "unrelated" decision domain was tested, and the pitch's central hook rests on that untested transfer.
- **Suggested fix:** "the damage from being corrected isn't to your answer, it's to your confidence — and it carries over to the next trial and depresses confidence across a whole block."

**Issue B. Headline implies duration not measured** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Being told you're wrong when you felt sure makes you doubt yourself for a long while afterward"
- **Abstract says:** Next-trial behavioral adjustments plus generally reduced confidence within high-conflict blocks.
- **Problem:** Nothing in the abstract speaks to persistence beyond the immediately following trials or the experimental block. The digest's own Caveats concede "short-term effects only, no follow-up beyond the session," which the headline contradicts.
- **Suggested fix:** "Being told you're wrong when you felt sure makes you cautious about your own confidence on the next decision"

**Issue C. Accuracy comparison not reported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "repeated contradiction produces a generalized dip in confidence rather than better accuracy"
- **Abstract says:** Reports confidence adjustments and model fits (biased postdecision starting points); no accuracy comparison across conflict conditions is given.
- **Problem:** The "rather than better accuracy" contrast — repeated in the wider angle as "without improving judgment" — is unverifiable from the abstract and may be flatly wrong. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "repeated contradiction produces a generalized dip in confidence" (drop the accuracy contrast, or verify against the full text).

### Study 4: People who have attempted suicide quit faster after a setback — and everyone with depression persists too long
**PMID:** 42704954 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Everyone with depression" overreaches the reported groups** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "and everyone with depression persists too long"
- **Abstract says:** "Once a wait began, attempters and depressed non-suicidal participants overpersisted relative to controls."
- **Problem:** The ideator group (n = 59), who are also part of the depression cohort, is not listed among those who overpersisted. "Everyone with depression" overgeneralizes across four groups where only two are reported.
- **Suggested fix:** "and both attempters and depressed non-suicidal participants persist too long"

**Issue B. Exploratory analysis reported as prediction** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "An earlier age at first attempt — not attempt lethality — predicted stronger loss-triggered quitting"
- **Abstract says:** "Exploratory analyses examined attempt lethality and age of first attempt... Earlier age of first attempt, but not attempt lethality, was linked with stronger loss-triggered quitting."
- **Problem:** The authors flag these as exploratory and use "linked with," not "predicted." The digest drops both the hedge and the exploratory label.
- **Suggested fix:** "In exploratory analyses, an earlier age at first attempt — not attempt lethality — was linked with stronger loss-triggered quitting"

### Study 5: Autistic people use rewards to guide their decisions exactly as much as anyone else
**PMID:** 42721846 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Exactly as much" overstates a null** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Autistic people use rewards to guide their decisions exactly as much as anyone else"
- **Abstract says:** "autistic individuals adjusted their decision boundaries in response to monetary reward in a suboptimal yet similar manner to that of non-autistic individuals."
- **Problem:** "Similar" and "intact" are not "exactly as much"; the abstract does not report an equivalence test, and the digest itself notes that null results need careful interpretation.
- **Suggested fix:** "Autistic people use rewards to guide their decisions much like anyone else"

**Issue B. Framing effects were not tested** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "that autistic people are the rational ones who don't fall for framing effects"
- **Abstract says:** "These findings... are inconsistent with accounts proposing enhanced rationality in autism."
- **Problem:** The task manipulated payoff across categories and measured decision-boundary placement; no framing manipulation was run. Attaching the result to "framing effects" imports a different literature.
- **Suggested fix:** "that autistic people are the more coolly rational decision-makers"

**Issue C. Source of the reduced-reward claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Claims of blunted reward processing in autism come mainly from neuroimaging"
- **Abstract says:** "Evidence for general reduced reward processing in autism primarily comes from neurobiological studies"
- **Problem:** "Neurobiological" is broader than neuroimaging; the wider angle then builds a whole "BOLD signal" pitch on the narrower reading.
- **Suggested fix:** "Claims of blunted reward processing in autism come mainly from neurobiological studies"

**Issue D. NS fit score** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10"
- **Abstract says:** "Autistic (n = 32) and non-autistic (n = 48) participants"
- **Problem:** A 7 rests on a 32-person autistic sample, a single task and a null result. The modeling is careful, but the claim ("two popular ideas fall at once") is much larger than the evidence.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10.

### Study 6: Telling women what antibiotics do to their gut cuts demand as effectively as invoking superbugs
**PMID:** 42702911 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Equivalence claim not tested; "demand" is not the outcome** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Telling women what antibiotics do to their gut cuts demand as effectively as invoking superbugs" / "this suggests a purely self-interested message works just as well"
- **Abstract says:** "An internal meta-analysis showed that both societal and individual consequence messages significantly reduced patients' expectations to receive antibiotics."
- **Problem:** Two significant effects are not evidence of equivalence — no head-to-head comparison or equivalence test is reported. And the outcome is self-reported *expectations to receive antibiotics* in a hypothetical consultation, not demand or prescribing.
- **Suggested fix:** "Telling women what antibiotics do to their gut lowers their expectation of a prescription — and so does invoking superbugs" / "this suggests a self-interested message also lowers expectations."

**Issue B. Secondary outcomes attributed to the internal meta-analysis** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "An internal meta-analysis found both messages significantly reduced expectations of receiving antibiotics; neither damaged trust in the physician, and both reduced decisional conflict."
- **Abstract says:** "Neither message type undermined trust in the physician (in Studies 1 and 2), and both communication messages significantly reduced decisional conflict (in Study 3)."
- **Problem:** Only the expectations result is attributed to the internal meta-analysis. Trust was measured in two of three studies and decisional conflict in one; the digest's construction implies all three pooled across 3057 participants.
- **Suggested fix:** "An internal meta-analysis found both messages significantly reduced expectations of receiving antibiotics. Neither message undermined trust in the physician (Studies 1 and 2), and both reduced decisional conflict (Study 3)."

### Study 7: AI teammates can be drawn into sharing what only they know — if someone asks
**PMID:** 42708610 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. N may refer to teams, not participants** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "with 98 participants in human-AI three-member teams and 65 in human-only teams"
- **Abstract says:** "We manipulated team type (Nhuman-AI = 98; Nhuman-only = 65, three-member teams)"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not state whether N counts individuals or teams. Unverifiable from abstract only — and the difference matters for the "moderate sample" claim in the NS fit note.
- **Suggested fix:** Confirm from the full text; until then, "with 98 human-AI three-member teams and 65 human-only teams (N as reported)".

**Issue B. Hidden-profile failure in human-AI teams not reported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Add an AI to the team and the same failure appears — along with the same cure."
- **Abstract says:** "Information inquiry enhanced information sharing and, consequently, improved decision-making performance, irrespective of performance expectations and team type."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that the *remedy* generalized across team types; it does not report baseline failure rates showing human-AI teams fell into the classic hidden-profile trap.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add an AI to the team and the same cure still works — prompting people to ask."

**Issue C. Researcher given name** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call Sarah Kerstan (University of Freiburg)"
- **Abstract says:** "Kerstan S(1)... Department of Psychology, University of Freiburg"
- **Problem:** Affiliation checks out; the given name is unverifiable from abstract only. Confirm before the pitch goes out.
- **Suggested fix:** Verify first name against the paper's author list.

### Study 8: Playing the villain pulls you deeper into a game — but only when the choice is really yours
**PMID:** 42784909 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Ranking guilt above pride** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Because guilt is a stronger engagement engine than pride — provided the game let you choose freely."
- **Abstract says:** "guilt, shame, and pride were modeled as three parallel mediators, whereas autonomy served as the moderator... moral emotions mediate the relationship between moral choice and narrative immersion"
- **Problem:** No comparison of relative mediator strength is reported. The pitch's causal explanation for the crossover interaction is invented rather than measured.
- **Suggested fix:** "Because moral emotions — guilt and shame, not just pride — carry the immersion, provided the game let you choose freely."

**Issue B. "Railroaded morality systems fall flat"** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "which is why railroaded morality systems in games so often fall flat."
- **Abstract says:** "autonomy moderates the direct effect of moral choice on narrative immersion. Specifically, under conditions of high autonomy, low-morality choices unexpectedly produced stronger narrative immersion than high-morality choices"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a moderation of the *difference between choice types*, not that low-autonomy conditions produced lower immersion overall. The claim that constrained morality systems reduce immersion is not a reported finding, and the wider angle then builds on it ("undermines the very immersion it is meant to create").
- **Suggested fix:** "which may be why the pull of a moral choice depends on whether the game really let you make it."

**Issue C. Per-emotion mediation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Guilt, shame and pride all mediated the link between the choice made and reported narrative immersion."
- **Abstract says:** "moral emotions mediate the relationship between moral choice and narrative immersion"
- **Problem:** The abstract confirms mediation by moral emotions as modeled in parallel; it does not state that each of the three was individually significant. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Moral emotions — guilt, shame and pride, modeled as parallel mediators — mediated the link..."

**Issue D. Author affiliation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call the authors at Shaanxi Normal University"
- **Abstract says:** Li Y is at Shaanxi Normal University; Jun YK is at Hong Kong Baptist University.
- **Problem:** Only the first author is at Shaanxi Normal.
- **Suggested fix:** "Call the first author at Shaanxi Normal University, or the co-author at Hong Kong Baptist University"

### Study 9: Time perception in autistic traits looks fine — it's the decision rule that differs

**Issue A. Body text generalizes beyond the subclinical sample** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Reported timing 'deficits' across the autistic spectrum may be a strategy difference" / "The recurring finding that autistic people experience time differently may be about how they decide, not how they perceive."
- **Abstract says:** "65 non-clinical adults varying in autistic traits, assessed via the Autism-Spectrum Quotient (AQ)... temporal differences along the autistic-trait dimension do not reflect a core perceptual deficit"
- **Problem:** The authors confine their conclusion to the autistic-trait dimension in a non-clinical sample. The digest's Caveats note this, but the body and pitch both slide into claims about diagnosed autistic people.

### Study 10: The brains of anxious, depressed gamblers barely flinch at a loss
**PMID:** 42779459 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Not the reckless thrill-seekers" mischaracterizes the subgroups** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "it's not the reckless thrill-seekers whose brains ignore losses, it's the anxious and depressed gamblers."
- **Abstract says:** "an 'impulsive type' (high impulsivity, low negative affect) and an 'emotionally vulnerable type' (high impulsivity with severe anxiety/depression)"
- **Problem:** Both subgroups are high in impulsivity. The distinguishing feature is negative affect, not the presence or absence of recklessness, so the contrast the pitch leads on is a false dichotomy.
- **Suggested fix:** "it's not impulsivity alone that goes with brains ignoring losses — it's impulsivity plus anxiety and depression."

**Issue B. Headline hyperbole** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The brains of anxious, depressed gamblers barely flinch at a loss"
- **Abstract says:** "the emotionally vulnerable type showed blunted FRN and feedback-P3 amplitudes to loss feedback compared to HC"
- **Problem:** "Barely flinch" implies near-absent responses; the finding is reduced amplitudes relative to controls.
- **Suggested fix:** "The brains of anxious, depressed gamblers respond less to a loss"

### Study 12: Money worries don't stop people giving — they change how they want to give
**PMID:** 42713986 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Claim about generosity was not tested** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Money worries don't stop people giving — they change how they want to give" / "hard-up donors aren't less generous, they're more strategic about how their giving looks."
- **Abstract says:** "these findings advance research on financial scarcity and prosocial behavior by shifting the focus from whether individuals donate to how they strategically choose among donation choices"
- **Problem:** The experiments measured preference for single- versus multi-category donation architectures. Whether financially constrained people give, or give less, was explicitly *not* the question — so the headline and pitch assert a finding the study did not produce, and one that cuts against a substantial prior literature.
- **Suggested fix:** "Money worries change how people want their giving to be structured" / "hard-up donors want their giving framed differently — more options, more visible social value."

### Study 13: Nobody knows why finding your way together is so much harder than doing it alone
**PMID:** 42767709 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "So much harder than doing it alone" is not a review finding** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Nobody knows why finding your way together is so much harder than doing it alone"
- **Abstract says:** "Use of GPS devices does not inevitably make it easy to navigate together: conflict in collaborative wayfinding is familiar to most of us, as is failure at it. Yet we know little about how people find their way together."
- **Problem:** The review reports that conflict and failure are *familiar*, not that joint wayfinding is measurably harder than solo navigation — and it explicitly anticipates collaborative process *gains* as well as losses. The headline asserts a comparative result the review says nobody has established.
- **Suggested fix:** "Nobody really knows how we find our way together — or why it goes wrong so often"

**Issue B. Shared history presented as a likely answer** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a shared history of routes may matter more than either person's sense of direction"
- **Abstract says:** "What difference does a shared history make in grounding communication and decision-making in wayfinding?"
- **Problem:** The abstract poses this as an open research question, not a candidate answer. The hedge "may" helps but the comparative ranking against "sense of direction" is invented.
- **Suggested fix:** "the review asks whether a shared history of routes matters as much as either person's sense of direction"

### Study 15: Parents rarely regret the surgery that disconnects half their child's brain
**PMID:** 42730552 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Followed" implies prospective design** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "researchers followed 37 children with Rasmussen's encephalitis who underwent hemispheric disconnection"
- **Abstract says:** "We conducted a retrospective cohort study using caregiver survey data from the Global Pediatric Epilepsy Surgery Registry."
- **Problem:** This is a retrospective analysis of caregiver survey data, not a follow-up cohort. The Caveats say "retrospective," so the body contradicts them.
- **Suggested fix:** "researchers analyzed caregiver-reported outcomes for 37 children with Rasmussen's encephalitis who underwent hemispheric disconnection"

**Issue B. Motor outcomes merged** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "around a third lost the ability to run or roll unassisted"
- **Abstract says:** "35.8% lost the ability to run, 6.1% to walk, 6.4% to stand, and 31.2% to roll unassisted."
- **Problem:** These are separate figures for separate abilities; the digest's "or" construction reads as a single combined third and drops the walking and standing losses.
- **Suggested fix:** "35.8% lost the ability to run and 31.2% the ability to roll unassisted, with smaller proportions losing walking and standing"

**Issue C. Recall-bias window** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "recall bias over up to five years"
- **Abstract says:** "mean age at surgery 8.4 years and mean time to surgery 3.6 years" — no survey interval is given.
- **Problem:** The five-year figure cannot be checked against the abstract. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "recall bias over an unspecified interval since surgery"

### Study 16: Where a seizure starts shapes how a person gambles
**PMID:** 42710371 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Across all epilepsy groups" drops the authors' hedge** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Decision quality and risk adjustment tended to be poorer across all epilepsy groups."
- **Abstract says:** "Decision quality and risk adjustment tended to be lower across epilepsy groups compared to controls, with the strength of evidence varying substantially by group and task."
- **Problem:** The abstract flags that evidence strength varied substantially by group and task; "all" implies a uniform effect.
- **Suggested fix:** "Decision quality and risk adjustment tended to be poorer across the epilepsy groups, though the strength of evidence varied by group and task."

**Issue B. Right-temporal findings stated more firmly than the authors do** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "right temporal patients showed heightened delay aversion alongside unusually risk-averse betting"
- **Abstract says:** "RTLE patients showed a pattern toward heightened delay aversion ($PP = .95$) paired with a conservative, risk-averse betting profile ($PP = .97$)"
- **Problem:** These are posterior probabilities describing a "suggestive divergence," which the authors hedge throughout; "showed" reads as an established difference.
- **Suggested fix:** "right temporal patients showed a pattern toward heightened delay aversion alongside conservative, risk-averse betting"

**Issue C. Headline causal verb contradicts the Caveats** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Where a seizure starts shapes how a person gambles"
- **Abstract says:** Case-control design; "A suggestive divergence in behavioral measures emerged based on focus laterality."
- **Problem:** "Shapes" is causal, and the outcome is Cambridge Gambling Task performance, not gambling behavior. The digest's own Caveats state the design "cannot establish causation."
- **Suggested fix:** "Where a seizure starts may track how a person plays a gambling task"

### Study 17: Parents of autistic children show subtle differences in attention and memory
**PMID:** 42728861 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Novelty label** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** New mechanism"
- **Abstract says:** "This cross-sectional study included 124 parents... These findings support the presence of candidate cognitive endophenotypes in parents"
- **Problem:** This is a cross-sectional group comparison identifying *candidate* endophenotypes, not a mechanism. The digest's own Caveats and wider angle correctly note that the design cannot separate genetics from caregiving burden.
- **Suggested fix:** Relabel as "Incremental" or "Descriptive group difference."

All reported statistics — 124 parents, children aged 2–12, poorer RTI and PAL performance, mixed SWM subtests, RTIFMDMT as the only significant regression predictor, no correlation with CARS/ABC severity — match the abstract.
