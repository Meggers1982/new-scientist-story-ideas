### Study 1: Crows, monkeys and preschoolers all build mental hierarchies — just not reliably
**PMID:** 42837861 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Only adults" overstates the adult/non-adult contrast** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "All groups showed some evidence of hierarchical reasoning, but only adults did so consistently"
- **Abstract says:** "Adults exhibited the most consistent reliance on hierarchical mechanisms, while children and non-humans showed graded performance influenced by task complexity and stimulus type."
- **Problem:** "Most consistent" is a relative ranking; "only adults did so consistently" implies children and animals were never consistent, which the abstract does not state. The abstract also notes "much individual and cross-population variation," including within adults.
- **Suggested fix:** "All groups showed some evidence of hierarchical reasoning, but adults relied on it most consistently; children and animals showed graded performance that depended on task complexity and stimulus type."

**Issue B. Crow-specific claim not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A bird with no language at all can assemble nested structures when the task is easy enough"
- **Abstract says:** Non-humans (crows and monkeys) "showed graded performance influenced by task complexity and stimulus type"; no crow-specific success threshold is reported.
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a pooled, cross-species gradation; it does not break out a result showing crows succeed at low task complexity. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "A bird with no language at all shows graded evidence of nested-structure processing that varies with how complex the task is — which suggests the dividing line linguists drew is a dial, not a switch (confirm the crow-specific pattern with the authors)."

**Issue C. NS fit score** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "NS fit: 7/10"
- **Problem:** The score rests substantially on reanalysis of two previously published datasets with inevitably small non-human samples and, by the authors' own account, "much individual and cross-population variation." The headline claim (recursion is not uniquely human) is large relative to that evidence base.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10, with the same framing note about reanalysis and variation.

### Study 2: People judge exponential growth more accurately when it's bad news
**PMID:** 42837964 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Extension to compound debt sits awkwardly with the financial null** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "which has direct consequences for how epidemics, compound debt and climate feedbacks are communicated"
- **Abstract says:** "estimates did not significantly differ when mathematically equivalent numerical progressions represented personal financial losses rather than gains"
- **Problem:** The one financial framing the study tested produced no framing effect, so "direct consequences" for compound debt is the weakest of the three examples offered and partly cuts against the data. The abstract limits itself to "implications for communicating exponential-growth information."
- **Suggested fix:** "which has consequences for how epidemic and climate-feedback numbers are communicated — though notably the same framing effect did not appear for personal financial gains and losses."

**Issue B. NS fit score rests partly on exploratory analysis** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "NS fit: 7/10 — three preregistered experiments with a very large total sample and a clean, concrete result"
- **Abstract says:** The media-exposure result came from "follow-up analyses" in Experiment 3; in Experiment 2 "the interaction between context and framing was not significant."
- **Problem:** The digest's own sharpest hook ("what you've been reading changes your arithmetic") is a post hoc subgroup finding, which the caveats acknowledge but the score does not. The core infection/recovery effect is solid; the hook is not.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10, or keep 7/10 but state in the score line that the media-exposure angle is exploratory.

### Study 3: Fear of childbirth that grows through pregnancy predicts less depression afterwards
**PMID:** 42749166 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Interpretation diverges from the authors' own conclusion** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a late build-up of apprehension may be ordinary anticipatory adjustment rather than a warning sign, which matters for how antenatal screening is timed and interpreted"
- **Abstract says:** "This study ... highlights the importance of early, targeted screening and intervention to support maternal mental health and optimize birth outcomes."
- **Problem:** The "ordinary anticipatory adjustment" reading is the digest's inference, not something the abstract offers; the authors draw the opposite practical emphasis (earlier screening, because baseline fear is the harmful predictor). The inference is hedged, but a pitch should not imply the authors endorse it.
- **Suggested fix:** "It suggests the starting level and the trajectory of childbirth anxiety are doing different psychological work — though the authors' own conclusion is that early, targeted screening matters most. Worth asking them directly what a rising trajectory means clinically."

All sample, design, timepoint and directional details (N = 2617; 12/20/28 weeks and 8–10 weeks postpartum; 70/30 train-test split; baseline fear → higher postnatal depression, lower bonding, lower birth weight, more epidural; steeper growth → lower postnatal depression, higher birth weight; higher education → steeper increase) match the abstract.

### Study 4: Legalizing same-sex partnership improves relationships, but not individual well-being
**PMID:** 42839126 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues → **Significant on framing**

**Issue A. Causal headline for a cross-sectional two-society survey** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Legalizing same-sex partnership improves relationships, but not individual well-being"
- **Abstract says:** "This paper examines the association between the legal recognition of same-sex couple relationships and one's individual and relational well-being... Taiwan respondents reported better relational well-being."
- **Problem:** "Improves" is a causal verb applied to a cross-sectional comparison of two different societies. The pitch angle correctly says to "frame the legal comparison as correlational," so the headline contradicts the digest's own guidance.
- **Suggested fix:** "Where same-sex partnership is legally recognised, couples report better relationships — but not better individual well-being"

**Issue B. Wrong outcome named in "Why it matters"** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "what a person's parents think may matter more to their day-to-day well-being than what the law says"
- **Abstract says:** "parental acceptance explains much of the significant variation in relationship satisfaction, beyond living in societies with or without legal recognition"
- **Problem:** The parental-acceptance finding is tied to *relationship satisfaction*, not individual or "day-to-day" well-being — and individual well-being is precisely the outcome that showed no difference. Substituting "day-to-day well-being" misstates the outcome variable.
- **Suggested fix:** "what a person's parents think may matter more to how their relationship fares than what the law says"

### Study 5: Being bullied by peers in childhood changes how stress biology tracks brain structure
**PMID:** 42830709 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues → **Moderate on framing**

**Issue A. Causal headline for cross-sectional moderation** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Being bullied by peers in childhood changes how stress biology tracks brain structure"
- **Abstract says:** "The present cross-sectional study examined whether retrospectively reported childhood peer verbal and physical abuse moderated associations of systemic inflammatory burden and hair cortisol with subcortical brain volume."
- **Problem:** "Changes" asserts a causal effect from a cross-sectional design with retrospectively reported exposure. The pitch text explicitly warns against this reading, so the headline undercuts the entry's own framing.
- **Suggested fix:** "Childhood peer abuse is linked to a different relationship between stress biology and brain structure"

**Issue B. Implied contrast with family maltreatment was not tested** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Bullying by other children — not family maltreatment — appears to reshape the relationship between stress chemistry and brain anatomy"
- **Abstract says:** "Core findings remained significant after sensitivity adjustment for broader childhood adversity and current depression/anxiety symptoms."
- **Problem:** Adjusting for broader adversity is not the same as showing family maltreatment does *not* produce comparable moderation; no head-to-head comparison is reported. The dash construction reads as a tested contrast.
- **Suggested fix:** "Bullying by other children tracks these stress-biology–brain associations even after broader childhood adversity is statistically accounted for."

Marker panels (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-alpha, CRP; n = 292), hair cortisol subsample (n = 172), the specific moderations, FDR survival and the caudate null all match the abstract.

### Study 6: A wearable brain scanner and a word game can tell three psychiatric diagnoses apart
**PMID:** 42840883 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues → **Moderate on headline**

**Issue A. Headline asserts diagnostic capability the abstract does not quantify** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "A wearable brain scanner and a word game can tell three psychiatric diagnoses apart"
- **Abstract says:** "Classification experiments using machine learning (ML) models demonstrate the proposed method's robustness and superior diagnostic performance in binary and multiclass settings, outperforming traditional unidimensional feature approaches."
- **Problem:** No accuracy figures, no external validation cohort and no held-out test performance appear in the abstract. "Can tell three diagnoses apart" states a settled capability; what is reported is relative superiority over other feature-extraction methods within one recruited sample. The digest's caveats concede the accuracy figures are absent, which the headline contradicts.
- **Suggested fix:** "A portable brain scanner and a word game show promise at separating three psychiatric diagnoses"

**Issue B. "Wearable" is not supported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A wearable brain scanner"
- **Abstract says:** fNIRS offers "portability, motion tolerance, and balanced spatiotemporal resolution."
- **Problem:** The abstract claims portability, not wearability; these are different device categories and "wearable" implies ambulatory use not described here.
- **Suggested fix:** "A portable brain scanner"

Sample (414 participants: MDD, BD, SCZ, healthy controls), site (West China Hospital of Sichuan University, Chengdu), method (MSNF fusing temporal dynamics with graph-theoretical topology) and the group-level connectivity finding all match. Contact attribution to Changjian Qiu is correct.

### Study 7: 'Easy-to-Read' versions of stories don't actually help people understand them
**PMID:** 42839715 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues → **Moderate on headline**

**Issue A. Headline states an absolute null the study cannot support** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "'Easy-to-Read' versions of stories don't actually help people understand them"
- **Abstract says:** "E2R adaptations do not produce uniform gains in comprehension, and their outcomes vary across readers and narrative materials... both improvement and worsening occurred with nonzero probability."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports heterogeneous, non-uniform effects in 37 readers — not an absence of benefit. The digest's own caveat says "'no systematic effect' in a small sample is not proof of absence," which the headline contradicts.
- **Suggested fix:** "'Easy-to-Read' versions of stories don't reliably improve comprehension"

**Issue B. Legal and LLM claims not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Easy-to-Read guidelines are written into accessibility law and increasingly automated by language models"
- **Abstract says:** The findings "inform the evaluation of manual and automatic text adaptation approaches."
- **Problem:** The abstract supports the existence of automatic adaptation approaches but says nothing about accessibility law or language models specifically. Unverifiable from abstract only — fine as sourced background, but it must be attributed elsewhere, not to this paper.
- **Suggested fix:** "Easy-to-Read guidelines underpin accessibility practice and are increasingly applied automatically (source this separately), yet the evidence that they improve comprehension of narrative text turns out to be thin."

Design details (37 adults with intellectual disabilities, 11 texts, partially crossed unbalanced within-subject, cumulative logistic mixed-effects models, syntax vs lexis split) match the abstract.

### Study 8: Acute stress makes a smell lose its emotional punch faster
**PMID:** 42777956 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Concrete mechanism" overstates preliminary imaging results** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It offers a concrete mechanism for a familiar experience — that under pressure the sensory world goes flat — and situates it in olfactory-limbic circuitry rather than in general mood."
- **Abstract says:** The findings "indicate that acute stress alters subjective affective habituation... and modulates overall responses within olfactory-limbic regions, while providing preliminary evidence for altered hippocampal response dynamics."
- **Problem:** The abstract describes a modulation of overall regional response plus *preliminary*, uncorrected evidence on dynamics — not an established mechanism. The study also did not test general mood as a comparator, so "rather than in general mood" is not something the data adjudicate.
- **Suggested fix:** "It points at a possible mechanism for a familiar experience — that under pressure the sensory world goes flat — and locates it in olfactory-limbic regions. Early-stage, but a usable line of enquiry."

Design (24 healthy young men, SECPT plus non-stress control, two odors of differing valence, repeated online pleasantness ratings during fMRI), the accelerated affective habituation result and the reduced hippocampal/piriform/orbitofrontal responses all match. The caveat about the hippocampal dynamics failing correction is accurate and well-placed.

### Study 9: Care workers find being ignored by management more stressful than the job itself
**PMID:** 42836492 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline broadens the comparison** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Care workers find being ignored by management more stressful than the job itself"
- **Abstract says:** "Feeling thus disenfranchised seemed to cause more stress than coping with distressed behaviours."
- **Problem:** The abstract's comparator is one specific demand — coping with distressed behaviours — not "the job itself," which implies the whole of the work. The body text gets this right; the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Care workers find being ignored by management more stressful than handling distressed behaviour"

Sample (96 direct care staff, 64 women, 32 men), setting (Swedish group homes and day programs), method (problem-centred interviews with participants involved in thematic analysis) and the three subthemes (absent frontline leadership, exclusion from decision-making, management's budget focus) all match. The caveat flagging the absence of any quantitative stressor comparison is exactly right and should stay.
