### 1. One in six people excluded from stress research may not actually be stress non-responders

**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at Wayne State University measured skin conductance responses (SCRs) — a standard physiological index of stress arousal — in 269 adults during an auditory novelty task, then applied both conventional thresholds and advanced Bayesian statistical modelling to classify participants. Using the standard detection threshold, 16% of participants (about 1 in 6) would typically be labelled 'non-responders' and excluded from analyses. However, Bayesian modelling found that these individuals did show structured, stimulus-linked physiological signals at very low amplitudes, and that the broader sample fell into three continuous subgroups (low, moderate, and high reactivity) rather than a simple responder/non-responder binary.

**Why it matters:** If a sixth of participants in psychophysiology studies are routinely discarded — and may not be true non-responders — decades of stress and emotion research could be systematically biased, potentially misrepresenting who is physiologically reactive to stress.

**Story angles:**

A new study of 269 adults used Bayesian modelling to look more closely at those discarded participants and found that they were actually producing real, structured physiological signals — just quieter ones. The finding implies that the scientific picture of who is and isn't stress-reactive may have been distorted for years, with potential knock-on effects for research into anxiety disorders, PTSD, and emotional regulation. Why it fits: NS Mind readers care about the hidden assumptions baked into science; this study exposes a widespread methodological blind spot that affects how we understand individual differences in stress, anxiety, and emotional reactivity.

(2026), which had claimed that controlling for individual differences in response criterion revealed a strong positive relationship between people's ability to correctly identify face matches and mismatches — suggesting a shared underlying face-recognition ability.
