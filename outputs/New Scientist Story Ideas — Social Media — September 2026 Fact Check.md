### Study 1: Posts loaded with moral language get less engagement, not more
**PMID:** 42686956 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline reverses the paper's own terminology** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Posts loaded with moral language get less engagement, not more"
- **Abstract says:** "moral loading was positively associated with engagement (range 1.12 [0.96, 1.28] to 9.07 [8.21, 9.93], all P < 0.001). Conditional on moral loading, however, moral density was 'negatively' associated with engagement"
- **Problem:** "Loaded with" maps directly onto the paper's technical term "moral loading," which is the variable that went the *other* way. The negative result attaches to density (saturation), conditional on loading. The body text gets this right, but a reader scanning headlines would take away the opposite of the loading finding.
- **Suggested fix:** "Posts saturated with moral language get less engagement, not more"

**Issue B. "English-language platforms only" not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "English-language platforms only"
- **Abstract says:** No statement about the language of the corpus; only that 1,621,147 observations across 13 socio-political topics on Twitter, Reddit and 8chan were analyzed.
- **Problem:** Plausible but Unverifiable from abstract only. If it goes into Caveats as fact it should be checked against the methods.
- **Suggested fix:** "language coverage of the corpus not specified in the abstract — check methods before pitching"

Note: "1,621,147 posts" vs the abstract's "1,621,147 observations" is close enough not to flag, but "observations" is the safer word. NS fit of 8/10 is defensible for a 1.6-million-observation, three-platform dataset with a clean, replicated inverted-U.

### Study 2: AI influencers dent women's body image just as much as real ones
**PMID:** 42379075 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Null moderation read as a positive statement about low-comparison women** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "even women who say they rarely compare themselves weren't spared"
- **Abstract says:** "appearance comparisons and age did not moderate the relationship between the assigned condition and the outcome variables"
- **Problem:** A non-significant moderation term means no evidence that the effect *differed* by comparison tendency — it is not positive evidence that low-comparison women were affected. With N = 303 split three ways, the moderation test is underpowered to detect a difference.
- **Suggested fix:** "and the effect didn't look any weaker in women who say they rarely compare themselves — though the study wasn't powered to rule out a difference"

Study facts (N = 303, ages 18–74, Prolific, random assignment, three conditions, body dissatisfaction up, facial dissatisfaction and cosmetic surgery interest not significant) all check out, as does the journal, epub date and the expert-witness disclosure in Caveats. NS fit 7/10 is defensible for a randomized three-arm exposure experiment.

### Study 3: A dose of negative social media posts about IUDs is enough to put people off them
**PMID:** 42641053 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Exposure described as one-off when the paper frames it as repeated** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "A single scroll through negative IUD content shifted attitudes measurably" / Caveats: "one-off exposure with no follow-up"
- **Abstract says:** Title: "Impact of Repeated Negative Affective Social Media Content on Acceptability of the IUD"; "Viewing this content repeatedly may deter people from considering IUDs."
- **Problem:** The study's own framing is repeated exposure to negative content within the experimental session. Calling it "a single scroll" makes the effect sound cheaper to produce than the design supports, and the Caveat compounds it by asserting a one-off exposure.
- **Suggested fix:** Pitch angle: "One session of repeated negative IUD content shifted attitudes measurably". Caveats: "a single session of repeated exposure, with no follow-up".

**Issue B. "Single-center" is not supported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "single-center student-aged sample that may not generalize"
- **Abstract says:** "In an online experimental study, participants (N = 206, MAge=18.9 SD = 2.02) were randomized..."
- **Problem:** This was an online study; the abstract gives no recruitment site. "Single-center" asserts a design detail that isn't there.
- **Suggested fix:** "online sample with a very narrow age range (mean 18.9) that may not generalize"

**Issue C. NS fit score slightly generous** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "NS fit: 7/10"
- **Problem:** Randomization earns it credit, but the outcome is a self-reported attitude measured immediately after exposure, in 206 participants clustered around age 19, with no behavioral endpoint. That is a narrow claim on thin outcomes.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10.

Mediation order (availability heuristic → affect heuristic) and the direction of the acceptability effect are both reported correctly, and the pitch's explicit warning not to imply uptake effects is good practice.

### Study 4: Heaviest phone users either zone out completely — or barely at all
**PMID:** 42492199 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline and pitch conflate PSU severity with time spent on the phone** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Heaviest phone users either zone out completely — or barely at all" / "Two people can spend identical hours on their phones and be doing completely different things with their attention"
- **Abstract says:** "Problematic smartphone use (PSU), characterized by addictive behaviors such as neglecting other activities and persisting despite harm... a U-shaped relationship between PSU severity and the tendency to engage in mind-wandering during smartphone use"
- **Problem:** Two errors in one. PSU is a symptom-severity construct, not usage volume — nothing in the abstract equates it with hours or "heaviest" use. And the U-shape runs across the whole severity range: high mind-wandering was found at *both* the low and high ends of PSU, not within a single group of heavy users who split in two. The body text states this correctly, so the headline contradicts the entry's own copy.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Phone-use problems come in two flavours: zoned out, or gripped". Pitch: "Two people can score the same on problematic smartphone use and be doing completely different things with their attention."

**Issue B. Number of industry-affiliated authors** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "three authors are employed by KDDI Research Inc., which also provided funding for the research"
- **Abstract says:** Two authors (Miyake Y, Kobayashi N) are listed at "KDDI Research Inc., Neurotechnology Laboratory, Saitama, Japan"; the COI statement reads "Affiliation c has provided funding for this research."
- **Problem:** Two, not three. The funding point is correct and correctly disclosed.
- **Suggested fix:** "two authors are employed by KDDI Research Inc., which also funded the research"

**Issue C. NS fit score vs. what is actually known** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "NS fit: 7/10"
- **Problem:** The abstract gives no sample size, the exposure and outcome are both self-reported, and the funder employs two co-authors. The digest acknowledges the docking but still lands at 7.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10 until N is confirmed from the full text.

### Study 5: Algorithms and chatbots are distorting what we learn from each other — in opposite directions
**PMID:** 42715802 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. Framing is consistent with the abstract. The two-stage upstream/downstream structure, the opposite distortions (tails amplified vs. collapse toward the mode), the shared point that the optimization signal shapes both visibility and prestige, and the link to pluralistic ignorance and illusory consensus are all reported as the authors state them. Affiliations for Brady (Kellogg, Northwestern) and Jackson (Booth, Chicago) check out, and the entry correctly labels this a theory/review paper with no new data and untested interventions.

### Study 6: Checking your ex's Instagram is about distress, not attachment style
**PMID:** 42388174 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Omits the indirect role of anxious attachment** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The comforting story that ex-stalking is hardwired into your attachment style doesn't survive contact with the data."
- **Abstract says:** "Attachment styles showed no direct associations after emotional distress was included in the model. Emotional distress emerged as the most consistent factor associated with IES, showing patterns consistent with indirect associations involving Neuroticism and anxious attachment"
- **Problem:** The paper does not discard attachment; it relocates it. Anxious attachment (and Neuroticism) show patterns consistent with *indirect* association operating through distress — which is close to the standard mediation story rather than a refutation of it. The headline and pitch both read as a clean debunk, and the novelty tag "Overturns prior research" leans on that reading.
- **Suggested fix:** Pitch: "Attachment style drops out as a direct predictor once you account for how bad someone feels — it seems to work through distress, not around it." Consider downgrading Novelty to "Refines prior research."

Sample details (N = 232, 18–27, 84 per cent women, breakup within past year, still following the ex, first three weeks retrospectively reported), the Extraversion finding, the Agreeableness exclusion for low internal consistency, and Eichenberg's affiliation all check out.

### Study 7: Fear of missing out may be what turns disaster feeds into lasting distress
**PMID:** 42722509 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Genuinely longitudinal" overstates the design the authors claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "NS fit: 5/10 — a genuinely longitudinal design on a timely question"
- **Abstract says:** "we collected data using a semi-longitudinal design from the same participants at three-month intervals (N = 220)... a cross-lagged panel model within a semi-longitudinal mediation framework to conduct the mediation test at two time points"
- **Problem:** The authors themselves label this "semi-longitudinal" twice. Two waves three months apart is thinner than "genuinely longitudinal" implies, and the Caveats line ("only two usable time points") already sits awkwardly against it.
- **Suggested fix:** "a semi-longitudinal, two-wave design on a timely question"

N = 220, age range 18–43, mean 21.69 (digest: 21.7), 78.2 per cent women (digest: 78 per cent), the mediating role of FoMO between social media addiction and vicarious trauma, and Altundağ's department all check out. The pitch's instruction to keep causal language tight is appropriate given the design.

### Study 8: Burnt-out teenagers scroll more — and that may raise their eating disorder risk
**PMID:** 41580900 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline implies a temporal/causal sequence the data can't provide** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Burnt-out teenagers scroll more — and that may raise their eating disorder risk"
- **Abstract says:** "Structural equation modeling was employed to analyze data from 853 adolescents... significant indirect effects of school burnout on EDs were found through three pathways"
- **Problem:** All variables were measured at one time point, so "scroll more" and "raise their risk" both import a direction the design assumes rather than tests. "May" softens it, and the body and Caveats are explicit that the arrows are hypothesized — but the headline is the part that travels.
- **Suggested fix:** "Burnt-out teenagers scroll more — and report more eating disorder symptoms"

N = 853, mean age 15.72, 52.1 per cent female (digest: 52 per cent), the three significant indirect pathways with ESMU as the first link, and Chao's affiliation at Tianjin Normal University are all accurate.

### Study 9: Reddit posts reveal how people really take one of the most intense psychedelics
**PMID:** 42704210 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Clinical-trial context not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Clinical trials of 5-MeO-DMT are advancing while a substantial unsupervised community has already worked out its own harm-reduction norms" / "Before the trials report..."
- **Abstract says:** Nothing about clinical trials; it positions the work as contributing "to the literature on real-world use of psychedelics."
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only. It's likely true and is legitimate framing, but it should be sourced independently rather than presented as flowing from this paper.
- **Suggested fix:** Attribute the trial context to an external source, or write "with clinical interest in 5-MeO-DMT growing (source separately)".

**Issue B. "The drug interaction that worries pharmacologists most"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "including the drug interaction that worries pharmacologists most"
- **Abstract says:** "Users were aware of pharmacological interactions, particularly with serotonergic medications, and emphasized harm-minimization measures such as the presence of a trusted sitter."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports what *users* discussed, not a ranking of pharmacologists' concerns. The superlative is the digest's own addition.
- **Suggested fix:** "including interactions with serotonergic medications such as antidepressants"

The counts (120 posts, 24 threads, 48 codes, seven categories), the individualized-dosing and distinct-tolerance findings, the emphasis on preparation, sitter and integration, and Świeczkowski's affiliation are all accurate, and the Caveats correctly describe the limits of anonymous forum data. NS fit 5/10 is right for a qualitative, unverifiable corpus.

### Study 10: Online victimization stands out in depressed teenagers who have attempted suicide
**PMID:** 42714373 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Dropped out" is an inference, not a reported result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "while problematic social media use itself dropped out" / "The interesting detail is what *didn't* survive the model"
- **Abstract says:** "cybervictimization, and two factors of metacognitive beliefs including negative meta-worry and superstition, punishment and responsibility beliefs emerged as key determinants"
- **Problem:** The abstract names what emerged; it never states that problematic social media use was entered and became non-significant. The inference is reasonable but the entire "why it matters" hook rests on it, so it needs confirming from the results table before pitching.
- **Suggested fix:** "while problematic social media use was not among the variables the authors identify as key determinants (confirm from the regression table whether it was entered and fell away)"

Group sizes (76 / 75 / 84), the direction of the group comparisons, the two named metacognitive factors, the cross-sectional caveat, and the author names all check out. NS fit 4/10 is appropriately conservative, and the "no causal or clinical claims" instruction is well placed given the abstract's own clinical-management language.

### Study 11: Fear of missing out is tied to brooding, not to healthy self-reflection
**PMID:** 41622812 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. Framing is consistent with the abstract. N = 461 American university students, web survey in 2024, confirmatory factor analyses, FOMO significantly more related to the brooding factor than the reflection factor, and equal relations to the underlying negative affect and depression factors are all reported correctly, as is Elhai's affiliation. The entry keeps the claim descriptive and cross-sectional throughout, and NS fit 4/10 is well judged.

### Study 12: Loneliness may push people toward impulse buying online, via self-esteem and FOMO
**PMID:** 42594777 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. Framing is consistent with the abstract. The sample (311 Chinese young adults aged 18–35, recruited via social media, university student networks and commercial panels), the sequential chain (lower life satisfaction and higher loneliness → lower self-esteem → higher FoMO → higher impulsive buying tendencies), and the corresponding author's affiliation all match. The digest correctly flags that the outcome is a self-reported buying *tendency* rather than purchase records, and that the mediation order is assumed rather than tested over time. NS fit 4/10 is defensible.
