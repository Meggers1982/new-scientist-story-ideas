### Study 1: Being told you did worse than average makes you exercise harder — and feel worse about your body
**PMID:** 42805545 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Moderation attributed to the wrong outcomes** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "but also showed the largest drops in body satisfaction and the largest rises in negative mood, with both effects strongest in people who identified most closely with the comparison group."
- **Abstract says:** "Moderation analyses indicated that the effects of the norm feedback on performance (F(2, 114) = 4.55, p = .13) and body satisfaction (F(2, 114) = 7.19, p = .001) were moderated by social identification such that its effects were stronger among those who strongly identified with the norm reference group."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports moderation tests for *performance* and *body satisfaction* — not negative mood. The digest transfers the moderation to negative mood, an outcome for which no moderation result is reported. Separately, the performance moderation is printed as p = .13, i.e. non-significant as reported, so only the body-satisfaction moderation is clearly supported.
- **Suggested fix:** "but also showed the largest drops in body satisfaction and the largest rises in negative mood, with the body-satisfaction effect strongest in people who identified most closely with the comparison group."

Note: the pitch angle ("the sting is worst among people who care most about the group") should be narrowed to body satisfaction for the same reason. NS fit of 7/10 is defensible — pre-registered, randomized, with an objective performance outcome.

### Study 2: Three minutes a day beats two half-hour workouts a week
**PMID:** 42704303 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Headline and pitch omit the two 20-minute sessions in the micro-goal condition** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Three minutes a day beats two half-hour workouts a week" / "a goal of three minutes a day ends up producing *more* exercise in total than a goal built around proper workouts"
- **Abstract says:** "Participants (n = 142) were randomly assigned to a micro goal group (3 min daily plus two 20-minute sessions weekly) or a standard goal group (two 30-minute sessions weekly)."
- **Problem:** The micro-goal condition was *not* three minutes a day instead of longer workouts — it included two 20-minute sessions a week as well, so the two prescribed weekly totals were near-identical (≈61 vs 60 minutes). A reader of the headline will take away that three minutes a day alone outperformed half-hour sessions, which the study did not test.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Adding a three-minute daily goal to weekly workouts gets people exercising more." Body: "a goal that added three daily minutes to two shorter weekly sessions produced more total exercise than two 30-minute sessions, despite near-identical prescribed weekly totals."

**Issue B. "Motivation" was not tested as a mechanism** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this is a randomized test showing the mechanism is not motivation or enjoyment but sheer ease of compliance"
- **Abstract says:** "Participants tracked their daily exercise duration, enjoyment, and ease of adherence over 3 wk. The micro goal group exercised significantly more time overall than the standard goal group, with ease of adherence mediating this effect."
- **Problem:** Enjoyment was measured; motivation was not, per the abstract. The abstract also does not report a failed mediation test for enjoyment, only a successful one for ease of adherence.
- **Suggested fix:** "a randomized test in which the effect was mediated by ease of adherence rather than by enjoyment."

**Issue C. NS fit score** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — randomized, directly testable by any reader, but short and reliant on self-tracked activity."
- **Problem:** A three-week trial with entirely self-tracked exercise duration, unblinded participants, and no objective activity measure is thin ground for a 7. The score note itself concedes both weaknesses.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10.

### Study 3: We judge people's health habits as moral failings only when we think they hurt someone else
**PMID:** 41185114 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Wider angle conflates collective harm with interpersonal harm** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "campaigns that emphasize the cost of unhealthy behavior to families, the health service or society are, on this evidence, directly manufacturing moral condemnation."
- **Abstract says:** "We identified three types of harm-personal, interpersonal, and collective... Perceived interpersonal harm reliably predicted moralizations of health."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that *interpersonal* harm was the reliable predictor and the manipulated variable. Collective harm (cost to the health service or society) is a separate construct that the abstract does not report as predictive. The pitch extends the finding to a harm type the study distinguished and did not support.
- **Suggested fix:** "campaigns that emphasize the cost of unhealthy behaviour to family and close others are, on this evidence, likely to amplify moral condemnation — though the study found the effect for interpersonal, not collective, harm."

**Issue B. Sample composition** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "limited by largely online samples" / "Mostly online samples of uncertain demographic breadth"
- **Abstract says:** Nothing about recruitment platform or sample composition.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only. Reasonable as a hedge, but it should not be stated as fact.
- **Suggested fix:** "Sample recruitment and demographics are not described in the abstract and should be checked against the full text."

NS fit 7/10 is defensible: five studies, N = 2,055, with experimental manipulation in two of them.

### Study 4: Ten minutes of "exercise snacks" a day lowers office workers' blood pressure and stress
**PMID:** 42810116 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Within-group changes presented as controlled intervention effects** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "By week 12 the intervention group's systolic blood pressure had fallen 4.6 mmHg and diastolic 2.4 mmHg, with perceived stress down 2.5 points on the PSS-10."
- **Abstract says:** "At week 6, significant group × time interactions emerged for SBP and PSS-10 but not for DBP or BMI. By week 12, SBP, DBP, and PSS-10 in the intervention group decreased from baseline by 4.6 mmHg... 2.4 mmHg... and 2.5 points..."
- **Problem:** The three numbers are within-group changes from baseline, not between-group differences. The only group × time interactions the abstract reports are at week 6, and DBP was *not* significant there. The digest's framing (and the headline verb "lowers") implies all three are controlled effects; the DBP claim in particular is not supported by a reported interaction.
- **Suggested fix:** "At week 6 the intervention outperformed the control on systolic blood pressure and perceived stress but not diastolic pressure or BMI. By week 12 the intervention group's own readings were down 4.6 mmHg systolic, 2.4 mmHg diastolic and 2.5 points on the PSS-10 from baseline; BMI did not change."

**Issue B. Control condition described as "no intervention"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "or to no intervention"
- **Abstract says:** "assigning office workers to an Exercise-Snack intervention (n = 25) or control (n = 27)."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not describe what the control group received. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "or to a control condition (not described in the abstract)."

Group sizes (25 + 27 = 52) and BMI null are reported correctly. NS fit 6/10 is defensible.

### Study 5: For older adults, being shown how to exercise matters more for mood than how much they exercise
**PMID:** 42807680 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline asserts a causal ranking from observational data** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "For older adults, being shown how to exercise matters more for mood than how much they exercise"
- **Abstract says:** "PAV and EG were associated with lower depressive symptom scores in the full sample, although the association for PAV was less consistent across subgroup and moderation models."
- **Problem:** "Matters more for mood" reads as a causal effect on a causal comparison. The study is a GEE analysis of self-reported survey data; the finding is that one association was more *consistent* across models, which is not the same as one factor mattering more. The body text and caveats hedge properly, but the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "For older adults, exercise guidance is a more consistent correlate of mood than how much they exercise"

**Issue B. Number of waves** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Using three waves of the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (2020–2023)"
- **Abstract says:** "Data were drawn from the 2020 to 2023 waves of the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS)."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not state how many waves this spans. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Using the 2020–2023 waves of the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey."

N (5,643), age band (60–75) and the digital-engagement moderation are reported accurately. NS fit 6/10 is defensible.

### Study 7: Eating disorder clinicians say they lack any guidance on the compulsive exercise they treat daily
**PMID:** 41982074 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline overstates the absence of guidance and invents a treatment frequency** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Eating disorder clinicians say they lack any guidance on the compulsive exercise they treat daily"
- **Abstract says:** "Only 42% reported access to clinic-level practices/informal protocols, while 84% expressed a need for national guidance."
- **Problem:** Two problems. First, 42% *did* report access to clinic-level practice or protocol, so "lack any guidance" is wrong for a substantial minority; what is absent is *national* guidance. Second, "they treat daily" is not measured anywhere in the abstract — assessment was common, but treatment frequency was not quantified.
- **Suggested fix:** "Most eating disorder clinicians say they have no agreed protocol for the compulsive exercise they routinely assess"

**Issue B. Escalated risk language** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Compulsive exercise is one of the most common and most dangerous features of restrictive eating disorders"
- **Abstract says:** "Maladaptive exercise is a common and serious symptom in eating disorders (EDs), yet often poorly addressed in treatment."
- **Problem:** "One of the most dangerous" and the narrowing to "restrictive" eating disorders both go beyond "a common and serious symptom in eating disorders."
- **Suggested fix:** "Maladaptive exercise is a common and serious symptom in eating disorders"

**Issue C. Companion study reference** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the companion Italian work on acute 'urges to move' (PMID 42490250) gives the piece a second limb."
- **Problem:** No abstract supplied for PMID 42490250. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** Verify the second paper's content and country before including it in the pitch.

N (102), the 84% figure, the "assessment common / structured intervention rare" contrast and the Karolinska / Swedish School of Sport and Health Sciences affiliations are all correct. NS fit 5/10 is appropriate.

### Study 8: "Realistic" postpartum workout images still leave new mothers feeling worse about their bodies
**PMID:** 41724692 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Backfire" framing implies a worsening the design cannot show** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Counterintuitive... a genuinely unexpected null-plus-backfire" / "The hook is the backfire against the obvious remedy." / Headline: "still leave new mothers feeling worse about their bodies"
- **Abstract says:** "Findings suggest women experience body dissatisfaction after viewing imagery and desire 'realistic' PA imagery."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that women *experienced* body dissatisfaction after viewing the imagery — it reports no pre-exposure baseline, no control imagery and no statistical test of change. "Leave them feeling worse" and "backfire" both assert that the imagery made things worse, which this design cannot establish. The caveats flag the absent control condition, but the headline and pitch contradict that caveat rather than respecting it. The only reported null (no difference between weight-retention and weight-loss groups) concerns a different comparison entirely.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "New mothers still report body dissatisfaction after viewing 'realistic' postpartum exercise imagery." Pitch: "The hook is that women shown deliberately realistic imagery still reported dissatisfaction afterwards — and asked for imagery more realistic still."

N (33 questionnaires, 21 interviews), the <12 months postpartum window, the weight-retention null and the Fraser Valley affiliation are all accurate. NS fit 5/10 is appropriate for an exploratory mixed-methods study.

### Study 9: Replaying a relaxation exercise to sleeping nightmare sufferers changes their sleep spindles — but not their nightmares
**PMID:** 42714131 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Failed replication" is the wrong novelty label** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Failed replication"
- **Abstract says:** "its precise role remains insufficiently understood because experimental approaches to manipulate hyperarousal during sleep are limited. In the present study, we investigated whether..."
- **Problem:** This is a first experimental test of odour-cued reactivation of a relaxation exercise in frequent nightmare sufferers, not an attempt to reproduce a prior study. The body text ("this is a careful test in a clinical population") describes it correctly; the label does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** Null result / first test"

Everything else is accurate: N = 25, ≥1 nightmare a week, one-week training, randomized blinded crossover, two lab nights, no beta/gamma effect, nightmare symptoms unchanged, unexpected reduction in spindle count and density, Wilhelm at University of Luebeck. The "authors flag longer reactivation periods" caveat matches the abstract. NS fit 5/10 is appropriate.

### Study 10: Teaching exercise through "feel it" cues beats instruction by demonstration on fitness and movement quality
**PMID:** 42804848 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Analyzed sample was 61, not 63** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "63 female university students aged 18–25 were randomized to 32 sessions over 16 weeks"
- **Abstract says:** "63 volunteer female university students aged 18-25 years were randomly assigned... Two participants from the embodied cognition-based group were unable to attend the post-test assessment and were excluded from the final analyses; therefore, the final sample comprised 61 participants."
- **Problem:** The randomized N is right, but the results rest on 61. Worth stating, since two dropouts from a 32-person arm is a non-trivial share.
- **Suggested fix:** "63 female university students aged 18–25 were randomized... with 61 completing post-testing."

**Issue B. Characterization of the two instruction styles** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "does it matter whether the instructor says 'watch me' or 'notice what your ankle is doing'?" / headline "'feel it' cues beats instruction by demonstration"
- **Abstract says:** "traditional instruction and embodied cognition-based training... By emphasizing exploratory learning and sensorimotor awareness..."
- **Problem:** The abstract never describes the verbal cues or says the traditional condition worked by demonstration. The digest's caveat ("loosely defined intervention") and the instruction to "press on how the instruction scripts actually differed" partly cover this, but the headline states the contrast as established fact.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Training built on body awareness beats conventional aerobics instruction on fitness and movement quality" — and keep the query about the scripts in the angle.

The fitness outcomes, direction of effect, kinematic findings (reduced knee flexion, greater ankle range of motion) and Kharazmi University authors are correctly reported. NS fit 5/10 is appropriate.

### Study 11: Prescribing exercise for pleasure gets people back to the gym — but not via the motivations theory predicts
**PMID:** 42772728 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. N (46), mean age (32), the three-session two-week design, the FITT vs FITT + AFFECT contrast, the absence of significant group-by-time interactions, and the authors' own inference that the parent trial's frequency gain "may operate through mechanisms beyond these constructs" are all represented correctly. The framing is appropriately cautious, the caveats explicitly note that nulls from an underpowered design do not rule out real effects, and the headline correctly attributes the behavioral gain to the parent trial rather than to this analysis. NS fit of 4/10 is, if anything, conservative.

(One note, not an issue: "not powered for these outcomes" is a reasonable inference but is not stated in the abstract — unverifiable from abstract only.)

### Study 12: Team sport, solo spinning or karate — the mood lift after exercise looks much the same
**PMID:** 42801814 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Physiological arousal" was self-reported arousal** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the pleasant feeling outlasts the physiological arousal by at least an hour"
- **Abstract says:** "One hour post-exercise, arousal fell below baseline, and positive and negative affect returned to baseline, whereas feeling state remained elevated."
- **Problem:** Arousal here is a self-reported affective dimension, not a physiological measurement — no heart rate or other physiological index is reported in the abstract. The caveats correctly say "self-reported affect," so the body text contradicts the caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "the pleasant feeling outlasts the sense of arousal by at least an hour"

The N (60), the three sport categories and their interactive/coactive/hybrid labels, the time course, the absence of sport-group effects, the large-effects-only sensitivity and the failure of equivalence tests across all 24 contrasts are all accurately rendered — and the digest deserves credit for refusing to sell the null as equivalence. NS fit 4/10 is appropriate.

### Study 13: Confidence in runners builds in one direction: believing in yourself shapes what you think others think of you
**PMID:** 42722364 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. The reverse path is not explicitly reported as null** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The reverse path did not hold"
- **Abstract says:** "Results revealed a significant cross-lagged effect from self-efficacy at Time 1 to RISE at Time 2, with an indirect effect from self-efficacy at Time 1 to RISE at Time 3 via RISE at Time 2... Findings provide the first longitudinal evidence that self-efficacy may shape RISE beliefs over time, offering new insights into the bidirectional nature of efficacy development."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports only which paths were significant; it does not state that the RISE → self-efficacy path was tested and non-significant (and it oddly refers to "the bidirectional nature of efficacy development"). The inference is probably right but is not confirmable from the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Only the self-efficacy → RISE direction reached significance in the reported results."

N (350), three time points over two months, the RI-CLPM design and its description, the social-identity null and the within-time correlations, and both named authors' affiliations are all accurate. The "first longitudinal evidence" claim matches the abstract. NS fit 4/10 is appropriate.

### Study 14: Most people with hard-to-treat epilepsy or dissociative seizures do no exercise at all
**PMID:** 42700754 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. "Most... do no exercise at all" is not supported** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Most people with hard-to-treat epilepsy or dissociative seizures do no exercise at all"
- **Abstract says:** "PA was low, especially at work (60.0% without PA) and during leisure-time (41.3% without PA). PWE reported less exercise than those with FDS (no exercise: 57.2% vs. 42.6%, p = 0.038)."
- **Problem:** "No exercise" was reported by 57.2% of the epilepsy group but only 42.6% of the FDS group — so "most" is wrong for one of the two groups named in the headline, and marginal across the pooled sample (~52%). The 60% figure is absence of physical activity *at work*, a different measure. The headline merges two incompatible statistics.
- **Suggested fix:** "Around half of people with hard-to-treat epilepsy or dissociative seizures report no exercise at all"

**Issue B. Mechanism speculation presented as the hook** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the within-group difference hints that fear of seizures, not physical incapacity, may be doing the work" / "suggesting the barrier is as much perceived risk and medical caution as pathology."
- **Abstract says:** Nothing about barriers, fear of seizures, or medical advice; the study measured activity levels and instrument psychometrics only.
- **Problem:** The explanation is invented by the digest. It is hedged, but it is also the stated hook of the pitch, which puts the weight of the story on an interpretation the data do not address.
- **Suggested fix:** "the between-group difference raises the question of what drives the gap — the study did not measure barriers, so this is one for the authors."

**Issue C. Unverified background claim about exercise and seizure control** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Exercise is one of the few interventions with evidence across both seizure control and the depression that so often accompanies it"
- **Abstract says:** "This highlights the need for interventions to increase PA in these patient groups." No efficacy evidence is cited.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only; needs an independent source before it runs as a premise.
- **Suggested fix:** Attribute to a cited review or drop.

N (240), the 146/94 split, 86.6% not seizure-free, 65.4% psychiatric comorbidity ("two-thirds"), the 60%/41% activity figures, the 57.2% vs 42.6% direction and the ICC range 0.54–0.75 are all reported correctly. NS fit 4/10 is appropriate.
