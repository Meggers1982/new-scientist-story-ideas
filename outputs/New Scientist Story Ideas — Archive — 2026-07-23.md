### 1. People who lose weight before their first psychotic episode gain it back twice as fast afterwards

**PMID:** 42463299 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bmjment-2026-302720
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Counterintuitive finding (pre-illness weight loss, not just medication, drives post-psychosis weight gain) with clear clinical and societal relevance, decent real-world dataset, and robust sensitivity analyses — docked slightly for retrospective observational design and modest N.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Using UK primary care records from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink, researchers studied 369 people diagnosed with first-episode psychosis (FEP) to examine whether pre-diagnosis weight changes predicted post-diagnosis weight gain. They found that 53% of patients had lost weight before their diagnosis, and those individuals gained weight at more than twice the annual rate compared with those who had not lost weight beforehand (8.93% vs 4.07% body weight per year). Crucially, the effect was independent of which antipsychotic medication was prescribed.

**Why it matters:** Weight gain after a first psychotic episode is already a major clinical concern linked to cardiovascular risk; this study suggests that the pre-illness period — not just medication — primes the body for rapid weight gain, pointing to a window for early intervention.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "The body 'remembers' pre-psychosis weight loss — and overcorrects dramatically" Over half of people with first-episode psychosis had already lost significant weight before their diagnosis — and new data show their bodies then regain it at twice the speed, independent of any medication they are given. Rapid weight gain after a first psychotic episode has long been blamed on antipsychotic drugs, but a UK primary care study of 369 patients suggests the story starts earlier: more than half had lost weight during the untreated phase of psychosis, and those individuals subsequently gained weight at double the rate — regardless of which medication they received. The finding points to a physiological 'rebound' mechanism triggered before treatment even begins, and opens a new window for early intervention. With growing awareness of physical health inequalities in people with severe mental illness, this has a compelling public-health angle. Why it fits: NS Mind readers care about the intersection of mental and physical health, and this reframes a widely blamed side-effect of antipsychotics as partly a pre-existing biological process.
