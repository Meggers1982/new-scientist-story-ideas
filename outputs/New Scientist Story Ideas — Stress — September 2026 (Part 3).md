### 1. Anxiety disorders and PTSD are linked to a higher risk of stroke, in a pooled analysis of 11 million people

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 21 September 2026
**PMID:** 42767516 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122527
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — huge sample and a clean effect size, but observational cohorts only, so causation stays open.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Researchers pooled 23 longitudinal cohort studies covering 11,181,338 participants to test whether anxiety disorders, OCD, and PTSD predict a first-ever stroke. Clinically diagnosed anxiety disorders were associated with a 29% higher hazard of stroke, PTSD with 50%, and the association held specifically for ischemic (not hemorrhagic) stroke. Crucially, the effects persisted after adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors and for depression.

**Why it matters:** Fear- and stress-related disorders have mostly been treated as brain problems; this suggests they also travel with the vascular system, and that a psychiatric diagnosis may function as a vascular risk marker in its own right.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is that PTSD outperformed depression — the usual psychiatric suspect in cardiovascular risk — and the link survived adjustment for blood pressure, smoking and the rest. Open on someone in their forties with a long-standing anxiety diagnosis whose cardiology appointment never mentions it. Call Pankaj Sharma's group at Royal Holloway, and pair with a stroke epidemiologist who can say plainly what the residual confounding problem is. Frame it as a risk-marker question, not a "treat your anxiety to prevent stroke" claim.
- **Wider angle:** A longer feature on whether psychiatry and cardiology should share a risk chart at all — the slow merging of "mental" and "cardiometabolic" risk scoring, and what a combined clinic would actually look like. Fits a health-policy or medical-trade outlet.

**Caveats:** Observational cohorts only (cannot establish causation); substantial heterogeneity between studies; OCD evidence rested on a single study; diagnoses defined differently across cohorts.

### 2. Stress hormones show up in how other people rate your working relationships — not in how you rate them yourself

**Journal:** *Biological Psychology* | **Published:** October 2026 (online 29 August 2026)
**PMID:** 42667999 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.biopsycho.2026.109380
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — an unusually rich behavioral dataset and a genuinely uncomfortable finding; limited by the small number of professionals.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Across four waves, 46 community supervision officers provided diurnal cortisol samples while independent observers rated relationship quality in 2126 video-recorded sessions with 732 supervisees; officers also rated 432 of those relationships themselves. Within individuals, higher total daily cortisol output predicted lower observer-rated caring and fairness, and a flatter cortisol slope predicted lower observer-rated trust. No cortisol measure predicted the officers' own ratings of the same relationships.

**Why it matters:** It suggests physiological stress degrades the skilled, effortful part of professional interaction — and does so invisibly to the person doing it, because self-reports were largely trait-like and stable.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the blind spot: on your most physiologically stressed days, other people can see you being less fair and less trustworthy, and you can't. Open on a probation officer running back-to-back appointments. Call Frances Chen at Georgia State, and get a second voice on why self-report measures of relationship quality barely move within a person. Careful framing: this is covariation, not proof that cortisol causes worse behavior.
- **Wider angle:** A workplace feature on "relational labor" in high-stakes public-service jobs — probation, social work, emergency medicine — and the case for measuring quality of interaction by observation rather than staff surveys. Fits a public-administration or long-form labor outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N of professionals (46) despite thousands of sessions; observational within-person design; single occupational setting, may not generalize.

### 3. A close bond with parents fails to protect stressed teenagers' response to reward

**Journal:** *Development and Psychopathology* | **Published:** 23 September 2026
**PMID:** 42773853 | **DOI:** 10.1017/S0954579426101862
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a clear failure of a well-loved buffering hypothesis, with EEG plus behavior; effects are modest and the sample is mostly White.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** In 165 adolescents aged 9-16, researchers interviewed participants about parent-child relationship quality and exposure to peer stress, then measured reward responsiveness three ways: self-report, a behavioral reward-learning task, and the reward positivity brain response to money, social and food rewards. Contrary to prediction, teenagers with higher-quality parent-child relationships showed a *blunted* brain response to monetary reward and reduced behavioral reward sensitivity when they also reported high peer stress.

**Why it matters:** The "good parenting buffers social stress" story is close to received wisdom; this suggests it is not uniform, and that different measures of the reward system give different answers.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the reversal — warm parents were associated with a *flatter* reward response in stressed teens, the opposite of the buffering prediction. Open on the lab scene: a 13-year-old in an EEG cap winning small sums of money after a week of being frozen out at school. Call Anna Weinberg at McGill and press her on the honest interpretation, including the possibility that protective parenting reduces the need to chase rewards elsewhere. No parenting advice should be drawn from it.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-driven feature for a psychology-literate audience on why self-report, behavior and brain measures of the same construct routinely disagree — and what that means for the RDoC-style project of building psychiatry out of "units of analysis."

**Caveats:** Small-to-moderate N (165); cross-sectional; predominantly White sample, may not generalize; several interactions non-significant, so the positive finding needs replication.

### 4. Treating PTSD reduces physical pain — even when no one treats the pain

**Journal:** *European Journal of Psychotraumatology* | **Published:** December 2026 (online 21 September 2026)
**PMID:** 42766718 | **DOI:** 10.1080/20008066.2026.2727209
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — randomized parent trial and a year of follow-up, but a secondary analysis with no pain-only control arm.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** A secondary analysis of a PTSD effectiveness trial in 196 veterans randomized to Prolonged Exposure plus placebo, Prolonged Exposure plus sertraline, or sertraline with enhanced medication management. Over 52 weeks, pain severity and interference fell in all three arms — including among the 70 and 93 veterans with clinically significant baseline pain interference and severity. Prolonged Exposure plus placebo produced the largest drop in pain interference during treatment.

**Why it matters:** Chronic pain and PTSD co-occur constantly and are usually managed in separate clinics; this is the first head-to-head look at whether PTSD-focused treatment moves pain, and the psychotherapy arm did at least as well as the drug.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Sell the counterintuitive clinical shape: talking through the trauma memory did more for pain interference than the antidepressant, in people who came in for PTSD, not pain. Open on a veteran whose back pain was treated for years as an orthopedic problem. Call Sheila Rauch at Emory. Be explicit that the study cannot rule out natural improvement over 52 weeks.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on the "pain is produced by the brain under threat" model and the clinicians trying to treat chronic pain by targeting fear and prediction rather than tissue — for a health or medical-science outlet, with the opioid-sparing angle built in.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of a trial not designed for pain outcomes; no non-treatment control, so improvement over time cannot be attributed to treatment; veteran sample, may not generalize; several authors declare pharma and foundation relationships (listed in the paper).

### 5. Post-traumatic stress symptoms predict who moves from suicidal thoughts to an attempt

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 15 December 2026 (online 7 September 2026)
**PMID:** 42705422 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122476
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a prospective sample of people already having suicidal thoughts is rare and valuable; effects are modest and all measures self-reported.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** 923 Australian adults with current suicidal ideation (mean age 36, 65% women) were assessed for depression, generalized and social anxiety, obsessive-compulsive, and post-traumatic stress symptoms, then followed every six months for 18 months with 86% retention. 11.5% reported a suicide attempt. Baseline post-traumatic stress symptoms carried the strongest hazard (HR 1.26) and depressive symptoms a smaller one, while obsessive-compulsive symptoms showed a small *negative* association with attempts.

**Why it matters:** Most suicide research compares people with ideation to the general population; this tests the far more useful question of who, among those already thinking about suicide, goes on to act — and hints that some anxiety-spectrum symptoms may inhibit rather than enable action.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the OCD result running the "wrong" way, which fits the "capability for suicide" idea: harm-avoidance may hold people back. Open with the ideation-to-attempt transition as the thing clinicians most want to predict and least can. Call Philip Batterham at the Australian National University. Handle with standard suicide-reporting care, including help-line signposting, and avoid implying any symptom set is reassuring.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on why suicide prediction models have barely improved in 50 years of research, and the shift from risk factors toward theories of capability and acute state — a good fit for a psychiatry-focused or long-form science outlet.

**Caveats:** All measures self-reported including attempts; modest effect sizes; associations attenuated by lifetime attempt history; Australian community sample, may not generalize.

### 6. About half of postpartum depression cases don't lift within the first year

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 16 September 2026
**PMID:** 42749161 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122518
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — fine-grained repeated measures across 12 months is genuinely uncommon; N is moderate and single-center.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** 191 mothers were followed from childbirth through 12 months with repeated symptom measures plus assessments of mother-infant attachment and perceived stress. Roughly half of postpartum depression cases recovered within six months and the rest did not. Among initially healthy mothers, 4.7% developed major depression within the year; among those with an early adjustment disorder, 25.6% did. Persistent cases were marked by low attachment, low social support, prior depression, childhood maltreatment and premenstrual syndrome.

**Why it matters:** Postpartum depression is widely framed as a six-week window; this reframes it as a dynamic 12-month process with distinct recovery, persistence and late-onset paths — and identifies early adjustment disorder as a strong warning signal.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the timing problem: screening concentrated in the first six weeks misses the quarter of mothers with early adjustment difficulties who become depressed months later. Open on a mother cleared at her six-week check who deteriorates at month seven. Call Natalya Chechko at RWTH Aachen. Note the hormonal-sensitivity signature — baby blues plus PMS — as an observational pattern, not a test.
- **Wider angle:** A reported piece on how maternal mental health screening is scheduled across different health systems, and whether the first-year monitoring this study implies is affordable anywhere. Fits a health-service or parenting-oriented publication.

**Caveats:** Moderate N (191), single-center German cohort, may not generalize; observational; risk correlates are retrospective self-report.

### 7. Inflammation in the body changes what brain-clearance markers mean in depressed teenage girls

**PMID:** 42777933 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.biopsych.2026.09.010
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — large, well-controlled imaging cohort; but the headline result is a moderation effect in a subgroup and needs replication.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** In the sBEAD cohort, 461 adolescents with major depression and 253 healthy controls underwent multimodal MRI measures of the brain's glymphatic clearance system alongside blood markers of inflammation and oxidative stress. Depressed adolescents showed altered perivascular diffusion, higher free water and a higher systemic immune-inflammation index. Larger choroid plexus volume was associated with more severe depression only in patients with high inflammation — and only in girls.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional (cannot establish causation); the key effect is a sex-specific moderation, vulnerable to multiple-comparison concerns; single-region Chinese cohort, may not generalize; MRI glymphatic proxies remain methodologically contested.

### 8. Childhood trauma shows up in teenagers' sleep years later — and perceived stress is the route

**PMID:** 42785565 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2026.113478
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — proper longitudinal design with a physiological moderator; sex-specific effect is fragile.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** 284 adolescents reported childhood trauma and sleep quality at baseline, gave a physiological measure of vagal regulation (respiratory sinus arrhythmia suppression during a stressful speech task), and were reassessed 1.5 years later for perceived stress and sleep. Childhood trauma predicted worse sleep 18 months on even after controlling for baseline sleep, and perceived stress mediated the link. ### 9. People who had tough childhoods react hardest to stress when they quit smoking

**PMID:** 42771860 | **DOI:** 10.1097/PSY.0000000000001531

**The study:** Smokers were randomized to smoke as usual (n=49) or abstain for 24 hours (n=69), alongside 45 non-smokers. All completed two identical sessions of speech preparation, speech delivery and mental arithmetic while blood pressure and heart rate were measured repeatedly. ### 15. Feeling that your life has gone off-course drains the good feelings, but doesn't add bad ones

**PMID:** 42765820 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02699931.2026.2733889

**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI unavailable this run

**The study:** Three studies tested whether "derailment" — the sense that your identity and life direction have been disrupted — makes stress hit harder. Study 1 (N=460 undergraduates, cross-sectional) failed to find the predicted interaction with perceived stress. **Caveats:** Mostly student and online samples, may not generalize; primary hypotheses not supported in two of three studies; key positive finding is exploratory and nonlinear; self-reported affect throughout.

**The study:** 1417 participants completed up to seven annual assessments of depressive symptoms, family and friend support, self-reported stressors, and treatment use. Multilevel models separating within-person from between-person variation showed that when an individual's depressive symptoms rose above their own usual level, they were more likely to be in treatment that year and the next. Fluctuations in social support and in stressful life events were only inconsistently related to treatment use once symptoms were accounted for.

**Why it matters:** It suggests help-seeking is triggered by internal symptom change rather than by external circumstance — so people going through objectively hard periods without symptom escalation may go unserved.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the null: losing your social support or being hit by life events did not reliably send people into treatment; only feeling worse than your own baseline did. Open on someone whose annus horribilis never produced a therapy appointment. Call Thomas Olino at Temple University, and ask what this implies for services organized around life-event triggers such as bereavement or job loss.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on the mismatch between how mental health services are marketed — around crises and events — and how people actually decide to seek help, for a health-services or social-policy outlet.

**Caveats:** Observational (cannot establish causation); self-reported symptoms, stressors and treatment use; annual assessments may miss short-lived episodes; the headline within-person symptom result is confirmatory rather than novel.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- PTSD keeps behaving like a whole-body condition. The previous digest's population epidemiology, "Men recover from PTSD in about a decade — for women it takes twice as long" (PMID 42303590), flagged PTSD's links to asthma and diabetes as a wider angle; this run makes that the main event, with "Anxiety disorders and PTSD are linked to a higher risk of stroke" (PMID 42767516) and "Treating PTSD reduces physical pain — even when no one treats the pain" (PMID 42766718).
- The measurement-mode split continues. Last run it was "Brain stimulation for PTSD works on questionnaires but not on clinician interviews" (PMID 42680058) and the publication-bias reversal in bullying therapy (PMID 42658011); this run the same fault line runs through "Stress hormones show up in how other people rate your working relationships" (PMID 42667999) and "A close bond with parents fails to protect stressed teenagers' response to reward" (PMID 42773853), where self-report, behavior and EEG give three different answers about one construct.
- Adversity effects keep turning out to be conditional rather than main effects. Following "When childhood abuse happens may matter more than how much of it there was" (PMID 42683544), this run adds "People who had tough childhoods react hardest to stress when they quit smoking" (PMID 42771860), where early adversity did nothing to cardiovascular reactivity until nicotine was withdrawn, and "Childhood trauma shows up in teenagers' sleep years later" (PMID 42785565), where perceived stress, not the trauma itself, is the operative step.
- Honest reversals of favored hypotheses are becoming the batch's signature. Last run: chronic stress blunting rather than amplifying daily reactivity (PMID 42595230) and the cognitive-reserve/loneliness paradox (PMID 42687444). This run: the failed parental buffering result (PMID 42773853) and the three-study derailment package with two unsupported predictions (PMID 42765820).

**Advances**
- Suicide research moves one step down the pathway. The previous digest predicted ideation — loneliness as an independent prospective risk factor (PMID 42642073) and the partly non-replicating IMV moderation (PMID 42683538). "Post-traumatic stress symptoms predict who moves from suicidal thoughts to an attempt" (PMID 42705422) tackles the harder ideation-to-attempt transition in a sample already at risk, and finds OCD symptoms running protectively — a capability-style result the IMV work did not test.
- War as a stress exposure moves from description to intervention. Last run offered "Growing up in a war zone changes which brain circuits track a child's attachment style" (PMID 42607893) and sociopolitical climate as trauma (PMID 42658624); this run delivers treatment inside the exposure with "Group therapy run inside a war zone proves workable for Ukrainian outpatients" (PMID 42731584).
- The sport thread gains a time axis. "Children whose parents and coaches both push winning report the most stress" (PMID 42419608) was a cross-sectional profile; "Female footballers' anxiety and depression symptoms peak in the middle of the season" (PMID 42759733) gives four waves and a seasonal shape.

**Counterpoints**
- Cortisol's status shifts again. "Mindfulness courses genuinely lower levels of the stress hormone cortisol" (PMID 42691284) treated cortisol as the hard outcome that vindicates soft self-reports; "Stress hormones show up in how other people rate your working relationships" (PMID 42667999) finds cortisol tracking *observer*-rated behavior while self-reports of the same relationships stay flat. Together they suggest cortisol is informative about things people do not notice about themselves — useful nuance for anyone writing cortisol as a straightforward stress readout.

**New themes**
- A perinatal cluster with no precedent in the previous run: "About half of postpartum depression cases don't lift within the first year" (PMID 42749161) and "'Pregnancy brain' tracks mood, not pregnancy" (PMID 42764724).
- Simulation and machine-learning methods used to rank intervention targets: "Chronic stress is the single best lever against teenage self-harm" (PMID 42788378) and "Gender outranks every psychological factor in predicting teenage gaming disorder" (PMID 42732833). The previous digest's network work (PMID 42695429) was descriptive; these papers try to make networks prescriptive.
- Immune-to-brain-clearance mechanism enters the topic with "Inflammation in the body changes what brain-clearance markers mean in depressed teenage girls" (PMID 42777933).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** A striking number of studies here find that stress registers where the stressed person cannot see it. Probation officers' daily cortisol predicted how observers rated their fairness and trustworthiness but had no relationship to the officers' own ratings of the same encounters (PMID 42667999); people entered therapy when their depressive symptoms rose above their personal baseline, while losing social support or being hit by life events did not reliably send them in (PMID 42732668); feeling your life has gone off-course drained positive affect without producing the distress everyone predicts (PMID 42765820); and the forgetfulness women call "pregnancy brain" tracked mood symptoms rather than pregnancy itself (PMID 42764724). "A close bond with parents fails to protect stressed teenagers' response to reward" (PMID 42773853) makes the same point about measurement: self-report, behavior and the brain's reward response to money disagreed within the same adolescents.

**Why pitch this now:** Mental health services, workplace wellness programs and app-based screening all run on the assumption that people can report their own stress accurately and will seek help when life gets objectively hard — and this batch, arriving together, suggests both assumptions fail in specific, predictable ways.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the limits of stress introspection — what other people, hormones and reaction-time tasks know about your stress before you do, and what that means for services built around self-referral and self-report. For general readers with an interest in psychology, plus clinicians and managers who design screening around questionnaires.

**Potential headlines:**
- Stressed people rate their own relationships the same on good days and bad ones
- A terrible year won't send you to therapy — a bad month relative to your own baseline will
- The stress you can't feel is the one no service will catch
