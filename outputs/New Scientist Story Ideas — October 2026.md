### 1. Crows, monkeys and preschoolers all build mental hierarchies — just not reliably

**Journal:** *Cognition* | **Published:** 6 October 2026
**PMID:** 42837861 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cognition.2026.106732
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — rare cross-species, cross-cultural comparison on a question central to what makes human thought special; limited by reanalysis of existing datasets.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers used Bayesian modeling to compare hierarchical, recursion-like reasoning — the embedding of structures inside structures that underpins language, math and tool use — across US adults, Tsimane adults from an indigenous Bolivian community, US children aged 3 to 6, rhesus monkeys and carrion crows, pooling published and newly collected data. All groups showed some evidence of hierarchical reasoning, but only adults did so consistently; children and animals showed graded performance that depended on how complex the task was and whether the stimuli were visual or conceptual.

**Why it matters:** The long-running claim that hierarchical, recursive thought is a uniquely human all-or-nothing switch looks wrong: the data fit a picture of a capacity that scales up gradually with cognitive resources across both development and evolution.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the crow. A bird with no language at all can assemble nested structures when the task is easy enough — which suggests the thing linguists called the dividing line between humans and everything else is a dial, not a switch. Open on the crow in Andreas Nieder's Tübingen lab working through bracket-like sequences, then widen to the 3-year-olds and the Tsimane adults who show the same graded pattern. Call Jessica Cantlon at Carnegie Mellon, and Steven Piantadosi at Berkeley for the modeling side.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on the slow collapse of "uniquely human" cognition — recursion, theory of mind, tool manufacture, future planning — each one eroded by a decade of comparative work, and what's actually left. Fits a long-read in a general-interest science magazine or an anthropology/linguistics-leaning outlet.

**Caveats:** Partly a secondary analysis of previously published datasets (Ferrigno et al., 2020; Liao et al., 2022); non-human sample sizes are inevitably small; substantial unexplained individual and population variation.

### 2. People judge exponential growth more accurately when it's bad news

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** 6 October 2026
**PMID:** 42837964 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107957
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — three preregistered experiments with a very large total sample and a clean, concrete result; slightly weakened by inconsistent mechanism.
**The study:** Across three preregistered experiments with 2836 participants in total, people estimated mathematically identical exponential-growth problems that were labeled either as coronavirus infections or as coronavirus recoveries. Estimates were consistently closer to the right answer when the numbers were framed as infections. Equivalent problems framed as personal financial losses versus gains showed no such difference, and the infection advantage was clearest among people with high exposure to liberal media and low exposure to conservative media.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**Why it matters:** Exponential-growth blindness is usually treated as a fixed arithmetic limitation, but these data suggest it bends with how a problem is worded and what information happens to be sitting in memory — which has direct consequences for how epidemics, compound debt and climate feedbacks are communicated.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The same doubling sequence, two labels — and people get the sums measurably less wrong when the thing doubling is a virus rather than a recovery. Open on the familiar 2020 moment when "it's only 200 cases" became 20,000 in a fortnight, then show that the error isn't purely mathematical. The media-exposure split is the sharpest hook: what you've been reading changes your arithmetic. Call Michal Pinhas at Ariel University or Maayan Katzir at Bar Ilan.
- **Wider angle:** A practical piece for a public-health or risk-communication outlet on how to present exponential numbers so they land — doubling times versus totals, losses versus gains — drawing on the broader numerical-cognition literature. Fits a specialist health-communication or data-journalism publication.

**Caveats:** Online samples, hypothetical estimation tasks with no stakes; the proposed emotional and attitudinal mechanisms did not consistently explain the effect; the key media-exposure result came from follow-up rather than preplanned primary analyses; the Experiment 2 interaction was not significant.

### 3. Fear of childbirth that grows through pregnancy predicts less depression afterwards

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 15 December 2026 (epub 16 September 2026)
**PMID:** 42749166 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122514
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a large longitudinal cohort with train/test validation and a genuinely odd directional finding; limited by observational design.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Using 2617 women from the Dutch Brabant Study, researchers modeled fear-of-childbirth trajectories across three measurement points in pregnancy and at 8 to 10 weeks postpartum, splitting the data into training and test sets. High fear early in pregnancy predicted worse postnatal depression scores, weaker mother-infant bonding, lower birth weight and more epidural use. But a steeper *rise* in fear across pregnancy predicted the opposite: lower postnatal depression and higher birth weight. More highly educated women showed the steepest increases.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the starting level and the trajectory of childbirth anxiety are doing different psychological work — a late build-up of apprehension may be ordinary anticipatory adjustment rather than a warning sign, which matters for how antenatal screening is timed and interpreted.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Rising dread before birth turns out to be the better sign, not the worse one. Open on the standard antenatal questionnaire handed out at a single appointment and ask what a one-off score can possibly capture about a nine-month process. Lead with the split between baseline fear and rate of change. Call Lianne Hulsbosch at Tilburg University; be explicit that this is cohort data and nobody should be told to worry more.
- **Wider angle:** A reported feature on how much of obstetric mental-health screening rests on single-timepoint snapshots, and what repeated-measures cohorts are revealing — for a health-policy or midwifery-focused publication.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort, cannot establish causation; largely self-reported measures including the mood and bonding outcomes; Dutch sample that may not generalize; short postpartum follow-up (8–10 weeks); the data-informed model-building approach risks capitalizing on chance despite the held-out test set.

### 4. Legalizing same-sex partnership improves relationships, but not individual well-being

**Journal:** *Archives of Sexual Behavior* | **Published:** 6 October 2026
**PMID:** 42839126 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10508-026-03539-6
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a natural comparison between two culturally similar societies with and without legal recognition, and a null where readers expect a positive; cross-sectional.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 812 LGBQ+ people in committed same-sex relationships in Taiwan, which legally recognizes same-sex couples, and Hong Kong, which does not. Taiwanese respondents reported more parental acceptance, less couple-level stigma and better relational well-being — but individual well-being did not differ between the two societies. Parental acceptance explained much of the variation in relationship satisfaction, over and above which legal jurisdiction people lived in.

**Why it matters:** Evidence from the US and Europe has treated marriage equality as a broad mental-health intervention; this suggests that in societies organized around family harmony, what a person's parents think may matter more to their day-to-day well-being than what the law says.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Taiwan legalized same-sex partnership and Hong Kong didn't — and on individual well-being, the two populations look the same. Open on a couple in Taipei whose marriage is legally recognized but not acknowledged at the family dinner table. The hook is that the minority-stress literature built in the Global North may be importing assumptions about where recognition comes from. Call Randolph C. H. Chan at the Chinese University of Hong Kong. Frame the legal comparison as correlational, not as a policy evaluation.
- **Wider angle:** A trend piece on the quiet export of Western psychology's models of stigma and well-being into very different family cultures, and the replication problems that follow — suited to a social-science or international-affairs magazine.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational, cannot establish causation; two-society comparison confounded by everything else that differs between Taiwan and Hong Kong, including the current political climate; self-reported outcomes; convenience sampling of a hard-to-reach population.

### 5. Being bullied by peers in childhood changes how stress biology tracks brain structure

**Journal:** *European Journal of Psychotraumatology* | **Published:** 5 October 2026
**PMID:** 42830709 | **DOI:** 10.1080/20008066.2026.2730909
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — rare combination of inflammatory markers, hair cortisol and MRI in one community sample, and it holds after adjusting for other adversity; cross-sectional with modest effect sizes.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** In a community sample, researchers combined retrospective reports of childhood peer abuse with structural MRI, a composite inflammatory marker (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-alpha, CRP; n=292) and hair cortisol (n=172). Peer verbal abuse moderated the relationship between inflammatory burden and amygdala and putamen volume; peer physical abuse moderated the link between hair cortisol and amygdala volume. The effects survived false-discovery-rate correction and adjustment for broader childhood adversity and current depression and anxiety symptoms.

**Why it matters:** Research on early adversity and the brain has overwhelmingly focused on what parents do; this points to the playground as a separate biological context, and suggests the same level of circulating inflammation means different things depending on what happened to you socially as a child.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Bullying by other children — not family maltreatment — appears to reshape the relationship between stress chemistry and brain anatomy, and it does so even after you statistically strip out home adversity. The surprise is that verbal and physical peer abuse track different biological markers and different structures. Open on the methodological point: hair cortisol gives months of exposure in a single strand. Contact Li Bu at Guangdong Pharmaceutical University. Be clear this is a statistical moderation in cross-sectional data, not a demonstrated causal pathway.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on the measurement problem at the heart of childhood-adversity research — retrospective questionnaires recalling events decades earlier, used to explain brain scans taken today — for a psychology-focused or methods-literate outlet.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional, cannot establish causation; childhood abuse measured retrospectively and self-reported; modest standardized effect sizes; hair cortisol available in only a subsample (n=172); single population, may not generalize; caudate findings did not survive correction.

### 6. A wearable brain scanner and a word game can tell three psychiatric diagnoses apart

**Journal:** *Depression and Anxiety* | **Published:** 6 October 2026
**PMID:** 42840883 | **DOI:** 10.1155/da/9921579
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a decent-sized clinical sample and a genuinely hard three-way discrimination problem; drags on being a classifier paper with no external validation.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers in Chengdu recorded frontal blood-flow changes with functional near-infrared spectroscopy — a portable, motion-tolerant alternative to fMRI — while 414 participants performed a verbal fluency test. Participants included people with major depression, bipolar disorder and schizophrenia, plus healthy controls. A new feature-extraction method that fuses the time course of the signal with graph-theory measures of connectivity outperformed conventional single-dimension approaches at both two-way and three-way classification, and group-level connectivity analyses showed distinct patterns per diagnosis.

**Why it matters:** Distinguishing unipolar depression from bipolar disorder is one of psychiatry's most consequential and most error-prone judgments, often taking years; an objective physiological signal that helps would be significant, though nothing here is ready for a clinic.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the clinical reality — people with bipolar disorder are routinely treated for depression for years before the diagnosis changes, sometimes with harm. This headset-plus-word-game setup is cheap, portable and tolerates fidgeting, which is where fMRI fails. Frame honestly: good within-sample classification is a long way from a diagnostic test, and psychiatric machine-learning papers have a poor track record of replicating. Call Changjian Qiu at West China Hospital, and get an independent methodologist to assess the overfitting risk.
- **Wider angle:** An investigation into why a decade of psychiatric biomarker classifiers have produced impressive accuracy figures and almost no deployed tests — the external-validation gap. Fits a medical or health-technology publication.

**Caveats:** No external validation cohort reported in the abstract; single-center; accuracy figures not given in the abstract; classification performance in a recruited, diagnostically clean sample typically degrades badly in real-world presentation; cross-sectional.

### 7. 'Easy-to-Read' versions of stories don't actually help people understand them

**Journal:** *Journal of Intellectual Disability Research* | **Published:** 6 October 2026
**PMID:** 42839715 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jir.70189
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a well-designed within-subject null that challenges a widely mandated accessibility standard; held back by small N.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Thirty-seven Spanish-speaking adults with intellectual disabilities read 11 short stories in both their original and Easy-to-Read adapted versions, in a partially crossed within-subject design analyzed with cumulative logistic mixed-effects models. The adaptations most often left comprehension unchanged, with no systematic improvement — and both gains and losses occurred for different readers and different texts. Simplified syntax did reduce reported grammatical difficulty, but lexical problems persisted.

**Why it matters:** Easy-to-Read guidelines are written into accessibility law and increasingly automated by language models, yet the evidence that they improve comprehension of narrative text turns out to be thin — and this study suggests the benefit is at best reader- and text-specific.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Simplifying a story's grammar makes it feel easier without making it more comprehensible — the hard part stays the vocabulary. Open on an Easy-to-Read version of a well-known short story alongside the original, and ask what simplification is actually buying. The timely hook is that AI systems are now being used to generate Easy-to-Read text at scale against guidelines that may not do what they claim. Call María del Carmen Suárez-Figueroa at the Universidad Politécnica de Madrid.
- **Wider angle:** A piece for a technology or disability-policy outlet on automated text simplification: who is evaluating the output, what counts as evidence of accessibility, and whether legal compliance has substituted for measured comprehension.

**Caveats:** Small N (37), single-center, Spanish-language narrative texts only; unbalanced design; "no systematic effect" in a small sample is not proof of absence; findings may not transfer to informational or procedural text, where Easy-to-Read is most used.

### 8. Acute stress makes a smell lose its emotional punch faster

**Journal:** *Behavioural Brain Research* | **Published:** 5 January 2027 (epub 23 September 2026)
**PMID:** 42777956 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bbr.2026.116491
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a vivid, relatable effect with brain imaging to match, but a very small all-male sample and exploratory findings the authors themselves flag as needing replication.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Twenty-four healthy young men underwent the Socially Evaluated Cold Pressor Test — a standard laboratory stressor combining cold water and social scrutiny — and a non-stress control session, then rated two odors of differing pleasantness repeatedly during fMRI scanning. Under stress, affective habituation accelerated: the emotional response to both odors faded faster across repeated exposures. Imaging showed reduced overall responses in the hippocampus, piriform cortex and orbitofrontal cortex under stress.

**Why it matters:** It offers a concrete mechanism for a familiar experience — that under pressure the sensory world goes flat — and situates it in olfactory-limbic circuitry rather than in general mood. Early-stage, but a usable line of enquiry into how stress blunts everyday pleasure.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Stress doesn't just make nice things less nice — it makes them stop registering sooner. Open on the stress test itself, hand in ice water while being filmed and evaluated, then the scanner and the odor pleasantness ratings sliding toward neutral. The hook for readers is anhedonia under pressure, seen in real time in the nose and the hippocampus. Call Pengfei Han at Southwest University, and ask directly which results survived correction — several did not.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on smell as an underused window into mental health, from the olfactory losses that precede depression and Parkinson's to post-COVID anosmia and its emotional aftermath. Suits a long-form health or neuroscience outlet.

**Caveats:** Very small N (24), men only, single-center; the hippocampal dynamics finding did not survive correction across predefined regions of interest and the authors call for independent replication; key outcome is a subjective pleasantness rating.

### 9. Care workers find being ignored by management more stressful than the job itself

**Journal:** *Journal of Intellectual & Developmental Disability* | **Published:** 6 October 2026
**PMID:** 42836492 | **DOI:** 10.3109/13668250.2026.2729565
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a striking inversion of what everyone assumes burns out care staff, from a substantial interview sample; qualitative design caps how far it can be pushed.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 96 frontline staff (64 women, 32 men) in Swedish group homes and day programs for people with intellectual disabilities, using problem-centered interviews, with participants involved in the thematic analysis itself. The dominant theme was abandonment: absent frontline managers, exclusion from decisions that shaped their work, and a perceived management focus on budget. Being disenfranchised in this way appeared to generate more stress than managing distressed or challenging behavior.

**Why it matters:** Burnout interventions in care work typically target staff coping skills and behavior-management training; if the primary stressor is organizational powerlessness, those interventions are aimed at the wrong target.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Ask any care worker what's hardest about the job and you'd expect the answer to involve aggression or distress. In 96 interviews, it was the empty manager's office. Open on a shift where a staff member handles a crisis alone and then can't reach anyone afterwards. The broader hook is the occupational-health literature on control and demand — Karasek's model playing out in a sector built on emotional labor. Call Petra Björne at Lund University.
- **Wider angle:** A reported piece on the social-care workforce crisis across Europe, framed around autonomy rather than pay — why staff leave jobs they describe as meaningful. Fits a current-affairs or health-service publication.

**Caveats:** Qualitative, no quantitative comparison of stressor magnitude despite the headline claim; participants self-selected; Swedish public-sector services may not generalize to privatized or lower-resourced systems; participant involvement in the analysis is a strength for validity but reduces analytic independence.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Interventions and institutions that move something other than the intended target.** This was the dominant thread last run — "A self-help app for trauma in Arabic reduced sadness, but did not raise happiness" (PMID 42747789), "Sending clowns into dementia wards changes how families experience visiting" (PMID 42749305), "Mental health parity laws and bigger state budgets barely move what patients actually get" (PMID 42750086) — and it recurs cleanly here. "'Easy-to-Read' versions of stories don't actually help people understand them" (PMID 42839715) cuts reported grammatical difficulty while leaving comprehension flat, and "Legalizing same-sex partnership improves relationships, but not individual well-being" (PMID 42839126) finds the legal change registering on couple-level stigma and relational well-being but not on the individual outcome everyone assumes it buys.
- **Useful nulls against established expectations.** Last run's "Boys and girls with autism turn out to look developmentally alike — with one exception" (PMID 42748595) and the parity-law null (PMID 42750086) are joined by two more: the Easy-to-Read null (PMID 42839715), against a standard written into accessibility law, and "Crows, monkeys and preschoolers all build mental hierarchies — just not reliably" (PMID 42837861), which dissolves the all-or-nothing recursion claim rather than confirming it.
- **Stress effects that depend on where the person starts.** "Your baseline cortisol decides whether stress wrecks your memory" (PMID 42749210) argued that identical stress produces opposite cognitive outcomes depending on resting hormone levels. "Acute stress makes a smell lose its emotional punch faster" (PMID 42777956) extends the same logic to affect, and "Being bullied by peers in childhood changes how stress biology tracks brain structure" (PMID 42830709) is the structural version: the same circulating inflammation maps onto amygdala volume differently depending on childhood social history.
- **Single-center psychiatric classifiers, still unvalidated.** The previous run's precision-psychiatry readiness audit and EEG prognostic work (PMIDs 42657684, 42663449 — carried in topic memory) are directly echoed by "A wearable brain scanner and a word game can tell three psychiatric diagnoses apart" (PMID 42840883): strong within-sample three-way discrimination, no external validation cohort, one site.

**Advances**

- **Perinatal risk moves from level to trajectory.** Last run established that postpartum depression is substantially heritable ("Postpartum depression is strongly heritable, Danish twin study finds", PMID 42648551), which reframes who is at risk. "Fear of childbirth that grows through pregnancy predicts less depression afterwards" (PMID 42749166) adds the temporal dimension the screening system lacks — baseline fear and rate of change point in opposite directions, so a single antenatal score cannot do the work asked of it.
- **Emotion decay gets a second measurement route.** "Anxious people don't get the usual relief of bad imagined futures fading" (PMID 42750110) tracked affective decay over a week in imagination; the odor-habituation study (PMID 42777956) tracks it over minutes in the scanner with piriform, hippocampal and orbitofrontal correlates. Together they make the rate at which feeling drains out of a stimulus a measurable individual trait rather than a metaphor.

**Counterpoints**

- **Is rising anxiety a warning sign or an adjustment?** The fading-affect work (PMID 42750110) reads persistent, undecaying dread as the anxious phenotype. The childbirth cohort (PMID 42749166) finds that fear which *climbs* across pregnancy predicts lower postnatal depression and higher birth weight, with the highest-educated women climbing fastest. The two can be reconciled — level versus slope, pathological versus anticipatory — but the tension is itself reportable.
- **Where recognition has to come from.** The Arabic PTSD Coach trial (PMID 42747789) raised the question of exporting Western self-help tools; the Taiwan–Hong Kong comparison (PMID 42839126) sharpens it into a result, with parental acceptance explaining more variance in relationship satisfaction than legal jurisdiction. Minority-stress models built in the Global North may be assuming the wrong source of legitimation.

**New themes**

- **Comparative and cross-cultural cognition.** Nothing in the previous digest addressed what is specifically human about human thought; PMID 42837861 pools US adults, Tsimane adults, 3–6-year-olds, rhesus monkeys and carrion crows to argue hierarchical reasoning is a graded capacity.
- **Numerical cognition and risk communication.** "People judge exponential growth more accurately when it's bad news" (PMID 42837964) opens a framing-and-arithmetic strand absent from the last run, with a media-exposure moderator attached.
- **Disability services as a research setting.** Two items from intellectual-disability journals — the Easy-to-Read null (PMID 42839715) and "Care workers find being ignored by management more stressful than the job itself" (PMID 42836492) — bring accessibility standards and the care workforce into this digest for the first time.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Three studies in this batch independently show that what looks like a fixed cognitive limit is actually a product of how the task is posed. People solve mathematically identical doubling problems more accurately when the numbers are labeled infections rather than recoveries, and the effect tracks which media they consume (PMID 42837964); hierarchical, recursion-like reasoning shows up in crows, monkeys and preschoolers but only when the task is simple enough and the stimuli are the right kind, making it a dial rather than a species switch (PMID 42837861); and Easy-to-Read adaptations lower reported grammatical difficulty without improving comprehension, with gains and losses depending on which reader met which text (PMID 42839715). The odor study adds a state-dependent version of the same point — the same stimulus loses its emotional charge faster when the person is stressed (PMID 42777956).

**Why pitch this now:** Large language models are being used to simplify public information at scale against guidelines these data suggest don't deliver, and health agencies are again writing exponential-growth messaging without evidence on what makes the arithmetic land — both practices rest on a model of comprehension as a fixed individual capacity.

**Angle:** A reported feature on comprehension as a property of the encounter between reader and text rather than a property of the reader, built around the three paradigms and the people now automating simplification — aimed at general readers who have been handed a "plain language" leaflet or a case-doubling graph and at the communicators producing them.

**Potential headlines:**
- What people can grasp depends on how the question is put
- Simplifying the grammar made the story feel easier and no easier to understand
- The wording of a warning decides whether the arithmetic lands
