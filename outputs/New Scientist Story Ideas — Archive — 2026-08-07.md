### 1. One in 19 US parents now uses cannabis daily — four times more than a decade ago

**Journal:** *Addictive Behaviors* | **Published:** 2026 Aug
**PMID:** 41904830 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.addbeh.2026.108654
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 9/10 — Large nationally representative US sample, striking counterintuitive magnitude of change (4x increase), strong societal hook around parenting and child welfare, clean survey design with trend data — minor caveat only for self-report.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers analysed data from 481,858 US residents in the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (2012–2024), comparing cannabis use trends among parents of children under 18 (N = 169,926) versus non-parents (N = 311,932). Daily cannabis use among parents quadrupled between 2012 and 2024, rising from roughly 1 in 80 to 1 in 19, and cannabis use disorder among parents increased 1.5-fold since 2020. The authors estimate that at least 5.9 million US children now live with a parent who uses cannabis on most days.

**Why it matters:** The rapid normalisation of daily parental cannabis use — now affecting millions of children's home environments — makes understanding its effects on parenting behaviour and child development an increasingly urgent public health question.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Daily cannabis use among US parents has quadrupled in a decade — and millions of children are growing up in those homes" Just twelve years ago, a US parent who used cannabis every day was a rare exception; today, it's one in nineteen. A massive survey of nearly half a million Americans spanning 2012 to 2024 reveals that daily cannabis use among parents has quadrupled, and cannabis use disorder among parents has grown 50% since 2020 alone. The researchers calculate that at least 5.9 million US children now live with a frequently using parent — a figure that would have been almost unthinkable before legalisation spread. The study doesn't show harm to children, but it sets up one of the most important unanswered questions in family psychology: what does a daily-using parent actually look like to a child? Why it fits: Parenting, child development, and the social consequences of drug policy are exactly the kind of societal-science story NS Mind readers engage with most.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** self-reported outcomes. Drug use data are self-reported, which likely underestimates true prevalence; study is descriptive and cannot establish whether parental cannabis use harms children; US-specific policy context (state-by-state legalisation) shapes the trends and limits direct comparison with other countries

### 2. People with OCD-like symptoms smoke for different reasons than other smokers — and nicotine withdrawal hits them harder

**Journal:** *Experimental and Clinical Psychopharmacology* | **Published:** 2026 Aug
**PMID:** 41843456 | **DOI:** 10.1037/pha0000843
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Counterintuitive result (nicotine patches work less well for OCS smokers) from a well-designed experimental study with a clear societal hook around smoking cessation and mental health comorbidity.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers recruited 129 adult smokers and assessed them for clinically significant obsessive-compulsive symptoms (OCS), then put them through four overnight tobacco-deprivation sessions in a randomised within-subject design that independently varied the ritual of smoking (very-low-nicotine cigarettes vs. no smoking) and nicotine itself (patch vs. placebo). Those with OCS got greater relief from urges through the act of smoking and greater pain relief from nicotine — but, contrary to predictions, nicotine actually suppressed their withdrawal symptoms less effectively than in smokers without OCS.

**Why it matters:** The finding suggests OCS-positive smokers may be harder to treat with standard nicotine-replacement therapies and could need interventions targeting the compulsive ritual of smoking separately from its pharmacological effects.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "People with OCD-like symptoms smoke for different reasons than other smokers — and nicotine withdrawal hits them harder" For smokers with obsessive-compulsive symptoms, nicotine patches may actually do a worse job of taming withdrawal — suggesting the standard quit-smoking toolkit could be failing a sizeable group. A laboratory experiment at USC gave 129 smokers either nicotine patches or placebos, and either very-low-nicotine cigarettes or nothing, in every combination — letting researchers pull apart the ritual of smoking from the drug itself. For people with significant OCS, the physical act of smoking suppressed urges more powerfully than it did for others, and nicotine blunted pain more strongly too. But the twist: nicotine patches did a worse job of easing withdrawal symptoms in the OCS group. That upends the assumption that nicotine replacement works the same way for everyone, and raises urgent questions about why millions of people with anxiety-related conditions find it so hard to quit. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are increasingly aware of mental health comorbidities shaping everyday behaviour; the idea that OCS rewires why people smoke — and makes standard treatments less effective — is a fresh, actionable insight.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** self-reported outcomes, single-center. Self-reported outcome measures for urge and withdrawal; single-centre US sample; OCS assessed dimensionally (yes/no threshold) not as a formal OCD diagnosis; several hypothesised interactions were non-significant, so findings are partial *Editor's fact-check note: Abstract confirms N=129, within-subject 2×2 randomised factorial design. The counterintuitive finding — that nicotine suppressed withdrawal symptoms less in OCS participants — is explicitly noted as 'counter to hypotheses' in the abstract. No corrections needed.*

### 3. How securely a parent is attached themselves predicts their child's attachment — and this holds equally for lesbian, gay, and heterosexual parents

**Journal:** *Attachment & Human Development* | **Published:** 2026 Aug
**PMID:** 38078384 | **DOI:** 10.1080/14616734.2023.2292053
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — Compelling finding that the intergenerational transmission mechanism is universal across family types, with decent combined sample and clear societal relevance to family diversity debates; docked for single-country sample, observational design, and small child N.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Italian researchers examined attachment patterns in 60 lesbian mothers, 50 gay fathers, and 42 heterosexual parents formed via assisted reproduction, alongside their 76 children aged 6–12 years, using the Adult Attachment Interview and the Friends and Family Interview. The distribution of attachment patterns was similar across all three family types and consistent with international norms. Mediation models showed that, regardless of family type, parents with greater coherence of mind had higher reflective functioning, which in turn predicted greater attachment security in their children.

**Why it matters:** The finding that the psychological mechanism transmitting attachment across generations operates identically in lesbian, gay, and heterosexual ART families suggests that parental sexual orientation is not a meaningful variable in children's emotional development during middle childhood.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "The way attachment passes from parent to child works the same in gay and lesbian families as in heterosexual ones" A study of Italian families formed through assisted reproduction finds that the psychological chain linking a parent's own attachment security to their child's is identical regardless of whether parents are lesbian, gay, or heterosexual. For decades, researchers have known that a parent's own attachment security predicts their child's — but this had rarely been tested in same-sex parent families. This Italian study finds the mechanism is the same: parents who can think coherently about their own attachment histories show higher reflective functioning, and their children bond more securely. Sexual orientation adds nothing to the equation. The finding matters because it removes one more theoretical basis for treating same-sex parent families as developmental risk factors. Why it fits: NS Mind readers interested in parenting, relationships, and child development will find the universality of this mechanism both reassuring and scientifically significant.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** small sample (N<100 children), observational design, single-center (Italy), sample limited to ART families. All families are Italian and formed through ART, limiting generalisability; the child sample (N=76) is relatively small; the study is observational so causation cannot be established; the paper was published online in 2023 but assigned to a 2026 issue *Editor's fact-check note: Abstract states 60 lesbian mothers, 50 gay fathers, and 42 heterosexual parents with 76 children — these numbers are reproduced accurately. Note that the PMID and publication date suggest the article was published online in December 2023 but assigned to the August 2026 journal issue; this is flagged for editorial awareness.*

### 4. Older adults are better at being happy — and younger people can learn their trick

**Journal:** *Personality and Social Psychology Review* | **Published:** 2026 Aug
**PMID:** 41178172 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10888683251384840
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — The counterintuitive framing — that older adults, despite more losses, are better happiness-pursuers because their goals align better — is exactly NS Mind's tone, and the practical takeaway is clear; score tempered because there is no new empirical data.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at the Chinese University of Hong Kong propose a theoretical 'intergoal compatibility account' to explain two longstanding puzzles: why people generally fail to achieve happiness despite trying (the paradox of pursuing happiness), and why older adults tend to be happier than younger adults despite facing more life losses (the emotion paradox of aging). The paper is a theoretical review, not an empirical study with participants. The account argues that happiness pursuit succeeds when it is compatible with other personal goals, and that older adults achieve this naturally because their life-stage goals are less likely to conflict with happiness, while younger adults' career and educational demands create friction.

**Why it matters:** The framework suggests a practical lever — aligning happiness-seeking with other personal goals rather than treating it as a separate pursuit — that could inform psychological interventions and self-help strategies across the lifespan.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Older adults are better at being happy not despite their losses, but because their goals finally align" A new psychological theory argues that the reason older people are happier isn't wisdom or acceptance — it's that their life goals stop fighting with each other. Psychologists have long puzzled over two paradoxes: why trying hard to be happy often backfires, and why older adults — who face more loss — report greater wellbeing. A new theoretical account from Hong Kong proposes a single answer: it's all about whether happiness fits with your other goals. For younger adults, career and educational demands are in constant tension with simply enjoying life. For older adults, those conflicts ease, making happiness pursuit more effective. The practical implication: you don't need to age into happiness — you can redesign your goals now. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are drawn to counterintuitive findings about emotion and cognition, and the idea that younger adults' unhappiness is structurally built into their life-stage goals is a fresh and actionable insight.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** observational design, no empirical data (theoretical/review paper only), no participant sample, based on researchers from Hong Kong SAR (consider cultural context of happiness research). This is a theoretical framework paper with no new empirical data; all predictions await experimental testing. Researchers are based in Hong Kong SAR, and cultural assumptions about happiness and aging goals may not translate universally — this should be acknowledged *Editor's fact-check note: No participant sample exists; this is a theoretical framework paper. Summary and caveats corrected accordingly. The paper draws on existing literature rather than collecting new data.*

### 5. Getting psychosis treatment faster is linked to better recovery — but immigrant patients are still being left behind

**Journal:** *Schizophrenia Research* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 31
**PMID:** 42537444 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.schres.2026.07.017
**Novelty:** Included for relevance
**NS fit:** 6/10 — Solid real-world cohort with a meaningful equity angle, but the regional Italian healthcare context limits direct generalisability to US/global audiences, and the core findings (treat early, treat specifically) are not strongly counterintuitive — partially offset by the immigrant disparity finding which has broader resonance.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers evaluated a multicomponent early intervention program for first-episode psychosis (FEP) across the Emilia-Romagna region of Italy, following 1,085 patients aged 18–35 over a mean of 2.5 years (2013–2019). They found that shorter delays before treatment and receiving more than 36 FEP-specific interventions (such as CBT and psychoeducation) were both associated with better clinical outcomes, with the largest improvements occurring in the first six months. However, patients with foreign citizenship showed higher symptom severity at both entry and follow-up, suggesting persistent equity gaps in access and outcomes.

**Why it matters:** The findings suggest that how quickly someone receives specialist psychosis care — and how much of it — shapes long-term recovery prospects, while also flagging that immigrant and lower-educated patients may be systematically under-served by existing early intervention services.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Immigrant patients with first-episode psychosis recover less well even in a dedicated early-intervention programme" Even in a purpose-built early psychosis programme designed for fairness, patients with foreign citizenship consistently showed worse outcomes — suggesting systemic barriers that specialist care alone doesn't fix. A large Italian real-world study of over 1,000 young adults with first-episode psychosis found that getting into treatment quickly and receiving more intensive specialist interventions were linked to better recovery. But the study also revealed a stubborn equity gap: immigrant patients entered the programme sicker and remained sicker at follow-up, raising uncomfortable questions about whether early intervention services are truly reaching everyone who needs them — a question with resonance far beyond Italy. Why it fits: NS Mind readers care about mental health equity, immigration, and whether public health programmes deliver on their promises — this study puts numbers to a gap many assume has been closed.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** observational design, single-center (single region), short follow-up (mean 2.5 years), self-reported outcomes not specified but naturalistic cohort. Results come from a single Italian region with a specific healthcare model that may not translate to other systems; the observational design means causation cannot be assumed; the study does not fully explain why immigrant patients fared worse, leaving open questions about language barriers, cultural factors, or delayed help-seeking *Editor's fact-check note: Abstract states mean follow-up of 2.5 years and N=1,085; both verified. The 'single-center' flag is applied loosely — this is a single-region (Emilia-Romagna, Italy) multi-site study, which limits generalisability similarly. No industry funding declared. Substance use finding (associated with improvement in behavioural symptoms) is counterintuitive but included as stated in abstract.*

**The study:** Italian researchers conducted a critical review of preclinical evidence on classic psychedelics — such as psilocybin and LSD — in the context of autism spectrum disorder (ASD), examining studies on social interaction, communication, repetitive behaviours, anxiety, and cognition. ### 7. Non-musicians automatically sort musical notes into categories in their brains — without even trying

**Journal:** *Brain and Cognition* | **Published:** 2026 Aug 3
**PMID:** 42546554 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bandc.2026.106461
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — Counterintuitive finding about non-musician pitch processing scores well conceptually, but the very small sample (N=26), single-center design, and the fact that results are specific to Mandarin-speaking participants (whose tonal language experience may drive the finding) limit generalisability and NS Mind appeal.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers recorded brainwave responses (specifically the mismatch negativity, or MMN) in 26 Mandarin-speaking non-musicians while they passively listened to musical tone sequences — without being asked to actively categorise anything. The study found that the brains of non-musicians automatically distinguished between tones that crossed a musical pitch boundary more strongly than tones within the same category, even at a pre-attentive, unconscious stage of processing. This suggests that the brain's ability to categorise musical pitch does not require formal musical training and may be partly shaped by language experience.

**Why it matters:** The finding suggests that musical pitch perception is not a specialist skill reserved for trained musicians but may be a more universal feature of the human auditory brain, potentially shaped by the tonal properties of one's native language — broadening our understanding of the relationship between language and music.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "You don't need music training for your brain to hear like a musician" Even people who have never studied music automatically sort musical notes into categories in their brains — without consciously paying attention. A new EEG study found that non-musicians' brains automatically fire differently when a musical tone crosses a category boundary — a response previously thought to require years of formal training. The twist: all participants spoke Mandarin, a tonal language, suggesting that language itself may train the brain to hear music categorically. This raises the provocative idea that growing up speaking a tonal language could give you a hidden musical edge. Why it fits: NS Mind readers curious about perception, music, and the hidden workings of the brain will be intrigued by evidence that musical ability may be less about training and more about the language we grew up speaking.
- **Wider angle:** Not generated by the previous pipeline.

**Caveats:** small sample (N<100), single-center, observational design, study population specific to Mandarin-speaking participants in China. Very small sample (N=26); all participants were Mandarin-speaking, so the tonal-language effect may not generalise to speakers of non-tonal languages like English; single lab in China; passive EEG cannot capture conscious experience of music *Editor's fact-check note: Abstract confirms N=26, passive oddball ERP paradigm, Mandarin-speaking non-musicians. Direction of effect (larger MMN for between-category deviants) verified. No corrections needed.*

**PMID:** 42091833 | **DOI:** 10.3758/s13415-026-01449-7
**Novelty:** Included for relevance
**NS fit:** 5/10 — Clinically relevant topic (trauma memory) with a novel framework, but it is a theoretical review with no new human data, limiting NS Mind appeal.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at Chinese universities conducted a narrative review synthesizing experimental findings across multiple neurotransmitter systems — dopamine, norepinephrine, acetylcholine, glutamate/GABA, and endocannabinoids — to propose a unified framework called the PE-Novelty-Neurotransmitter Network. The framework explains how surprise (prediction error) and novelty trigger the temporary destabilization of consolidated memories, making them susceptible to updating. The authors argue this midbrain-locus coeruleus-cortex circuit model could inform strategies for modifying maladaptive memories, such as those underlying PTSD or phobias. This is a theoretical review, not an original empirical study.

**Why it matters:** Understanding the conditions under which memories become malleable could eventually inform more targeted therapeutic approaches for trauma and anxiety disorders. The framework offers a unifying lens for a field that has historically studied neurotransmitters in isolation.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Your brain can rewrite bad memories — and scientists now have a map for how to trigger it" Surprise turns out to be the key that unlocks a memory and makes it rewritable — and researchers now think they know the neural network that controls that process. Scientists have long known that memories become temporarily editable when retrieved, but the mechanism has been murky. A new integrative review proposes a unified brain-circuit model — spanning dopamine, norepinephrine, and the endocannabinoid system — that explains when and why memories open up for revision. The surprising implication: it's novelty and violated expectations, not emotional intensity per se, that trigger this window. That reframes how we think about therapeutic interventions for PTSD and phobias. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are deeply interested in how memory, emotion, and trauma intersect, and a new framework tying neurotransmitters to memory flexibility has clear resonance.
