**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at Florida State University conducted a comprehensive theoretical review re-evaluating the 'fleeing famine hypothesis' (FFH) of anorexia nervosa, which proposes that the disorder's paradoxical behaviours — continued food restriction, denial of illness, and increased activity despite starvation — are triggered by an evolutionarily conserved survival programme originally designed to drive animals away from local food shortages. The team assessed nine empirical predictions made in the hypothesis's 2003 formulation against evidence accumulated over two decades. **Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers randomly assigned 196 adults (98 with ADHD, 98 neurotypical controls) to one of three mental encoding strategies before attempting to remember self-assigned everyday tasks across three online sessions. Contrary to predictions, outcome-focused episodic future thinking — vividly imagining the end result of completing an intention — produced the strongest memory improvements overall, while process-focused thinking (imagining the steps involved) offered only moderate benefit for the ADHD group. The study provides early evidence that a simple, low-cost mental imagery technique can meaningfully reduce the prospective memory failures that commonly disrupt daily life for people with ADHD.

**Why it matters:** Prospective memory failures — forgetting to carry out intended actions — are a major source of daily difficulty for adults with ADHD, and this study suggests a brief mental imagery strategy could serve as a practical, drug-free aid to functioning.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Picturing success — not the steps — is what helps ADHD brains remember plans" When scientists tested two mental imagery techniques to help adults with ADHD remember their intentions, the one that logic said should work best came second. A randomised study of nearly 200 adults found that vividly imagining the end result of completing a task — not rehearsing the steps to get there — led to the biggest improvement in everyday memory performance for people with ADHD. This flips the conventional wisdom from productivity advice and behavioural therapy, which typically emphasises process planning. The finding opens a new question about why outcome visualisation works, with implications for how clinicians and coaches help ADHD adults manage daily life. Why it fits: NS Mind readers with or without ADHD will immediately recognise the frustration of forgetting what you meant to do, and a simple, free mental trick to fix it is exactly the kind of practical insight they seek.

**Why it matters:** The finding illustrates that the brain recruits sound to compensate for degraded visual information during memory encoding, which could have implications for how learning environments, media design, and assistive technologies are structured.

The result neatly demonstrates the brain's 'inverse effectiveness' principle: cross-sense integration kicks in hardest when one channel is weak. The finding raises questions about how classrooms, e-learning platforms, and even social media could harness audio cues when visuals are unclear or glanced at quickly.
