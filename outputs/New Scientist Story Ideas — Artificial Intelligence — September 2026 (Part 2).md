### 1. Saying "we" instead of "I" makes people more afraid of AI

**Journal:** *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* | **Published:** 31 July 2025 (issue Sep 2026)
**PMID:** 40745650 | **DOI:** 10.1177/01461672251356107
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a tiny linguistic lever moving a big public attitude, backed by corpora plus four experiments, though effect sizes and real-world reach are unclear
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers at Beijing Normal University analyzed two large corpora of AI-related text and ran four experiments (N = 1,451) with English- and Chinese-speaking participants. People who used first-person plural pronouns ("we", "us") rather than singular ones ("I", "me") when thinking or writing about AI reported greater perceived AI threat. The proposed mechanism is "regulatory scope": "we" widens the mental frame to collective, long-range concerns, and the effect was strongest when the threat was psychologically distant and among English speakers.

**Why it matters:** It suggests public alarm about AI is partly a product of how the conversation is phrased — commentary, campaigning and policy documents overwhelmingly speak in the collective voice — rather than of the technology itself.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a sentence from a typical AI op-ed — "we need to ask what this means for humanity" — and show that simply swapping the pronoun changes how threatening readers find the same technology. The hook is that the most-studied predictor of AI anxiety here isn't knowledge, age or job exposure but a grammatical choice. Call Jingyuan Dang or Li Liu at Beijing Normal University; worth pairing with a psycholinguist on construal-level theory for an independent read on the regulatory-scope mechanism.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a politics or communications outlet on the rhetoric of existential risk — how "we" became the default pronoun of AI-safety discourse, and whether collective framing inflates distant risks while leaving concrete, near-term harms (bias, surveillance, layoffs) feeling comparatively minor.

**Caveats:** Student and online participant samples; cross-sectional corpus analyses cannot establish causation, though the experiments can; self-reported threat perception only; effects differed by language, so generalization beyond English and Chinese is untested.

### 2. Chatting with an AI that plays a foreigner softens prejudice — but only on the surface

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** 20 September 2026
**PMID:** 42763964 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107846
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a genuinely new use for chatbots with a clean control condition, held back by small N and pilot status
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Sixty-one Japanese adults were assessed for attitudes toward Chinese people, then a month later either did nothing (pure control) or spent 15 minutes exchanging text messages with a conversational AI presenting itself as a Chinese person, introducing themselves and discussing joint work toward the Sustainable Development Goals. The contact group showed increased positive attitudes and intergroup empathy and reduced negative attitudes relative to controls. Intergroup anxiety and implicit attitudes measured with the Implicit Association Test did not budge.

**Why it matters:** Intergroup contact is one of social psychology's most replicated prejudice-reduction effects, but it requires an actual out-group member; this is early evidence that a synthetic stand-in can carry some of the same benefit — and equally clear evidence that it does not reach the automatic layer of bias.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the oddity — a 15-minute text chat with a machine pretending to be Chinese shifted Japanese participants' stated attitudes, while their implicit responses stayed exactly where they were. The piece opens on the ethics of a chatbot performing an ethnicity it does not have, then asks what "contact" even means if no one from the out-group is present. Call Ryosuke Takamatsu at Institute of Science Tokyo; pair with a researcher in the e-contact tradition for a check on whether explicit-only gains persist.
- **Wider angle:** A longer piece for an education or international-affairs outlet on AI-mediated "virtual exchange" programs replacing school and university partnerships between countries with strained relations — cheaper and safer than the real thing, and possibly a substitute that lets institutions avoid the harder work.

**Caveats:** Small N (61), single country, pilot study, self-reported explicit measures, very short exposure, no follow-up to test durability, and a pure control rather than an active comparison (e.g. chatting with an in-group AI).

### 3. AI spots hidden brain disorganization in epilepsy tissue that looks normal to pathologists

**Journal:** *Brain Pathology* | **Published:** 26 March 2026 (issue Sep 2026)
**PMID:** 42767799 | **DOI:** 10.1111/bpa.70096
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a concrete demonstration that machine analysis sees structure human eyes miss, with a clear clinical payoff; retrospective and single-center
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Mayo Clinic researchers applied automated neuron detection and spatial-statistical modeling to whole-slide images from 83 focal cortical dysplasia regions and 19 neurologically normal autopsy controls, extracting features such as neuronal clustering, distribution inhomogeneity and nuclear shape. A regularized regression classifier reached 88.2% accuracy at specimen-level diagnosis. Crucially, tissue taken from areas with abnormal EEG activity but no visible dysplasia under the microscope showed measurable architectural disorganization resembling tissue adjacent to confirmed lesions — and clearly different from control brain.

**Why it matters:** It gives physical substance to the long-standing puzzle of "MRI-negative, histology-negative" epilepsy, suggesting the seizure-generating cortex is genuinely abnormal at a scale below what a pathologist can perceive — and could eventually sharpen surgical targeting.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the frustrating conversation a neurosurgeon has with a family — we removed the tissue where the seizures start, but the pathologist found nothing wrong with it. Then the twist: run the same slides through a spatial-statistics pipeline and the "normal" tissue isn't normal at all. Call Aditya Raghunathan or Lily Wong-Kisiel at Mayo Clinic. Avoid implying this changes surgical practice yet.
- **Wider angle:** A specialist-medicine or *Nature*-style feature on computational pathology as a new kind of microscope — how quantifying spatial arrangement rather than appearance is turning "normal-looking" tissue into a measurable category across epilepsy, dementia and oncology.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, single-center, modest sample of specimens; 70.6% subregion accuracy is far from clinical-grade; neuron detection carried a 12% false-positive rate; no outcome data linking detected disorganization to surgical success; no external validation.

### 4. Feeling good at using AI doesn't make students lean on it — trusting it does

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** 28 September 2026
**PMID:** 42804850 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107880
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a useful correction to a common assumption about AI literacy, but cross-sectional and self-reported
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Survey data from 518 Chinese university students were analyzed with both structural equation modeling and fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis. AI-related self-efficacy — feeling skilled and confident with generative AI — showed no direct association with how much students actually delegated thinking and task completion to it. Trust in the system was the decisive variable: the configurational analysis found a single route to heavy reliance, combining social motivation with trust, and no stable route without trust. Students with instrumental, task-focused motives were, after accounting for self-efficacy, somewhat less trusting.

**Why it matters:** Universities are responding to AI by teaching students to use it more skillfully, on the assumption that competence and over-reliance are the same dial; this suggests they are separate, and that calibrating trust is the lever that matters.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the mismatch between what universities are doing and what predicts offloading: the confident student is not the dependent one, the trusting one is. Open on a student who knows exactly how a large language model fails and uses it anyway — versus one who doesn't and doesn't. Call Yufei Du at Chongqing University of Technology; pair with a researcher on cognitive offloading for the memory and reasoning implications.
- **Wider angle:** A workplace-and-technology piece for a business or education outlet on trust calibration as the new digital literacy — drawing the parallel with decades of automation research in aviation and driving, where the danger was never operator incompetence but miscalibrated confidence in the machine.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational (cannot establish causation); single-country, single-institution student sample; all measures self-reported, including "reliance"; no behavioral or performance outcomes.

### 5. Two-thirds of HIV patients at a Nigerian clinic are already asking chatbots about their treatment

**Journal:** *International Journal of Behavioral Medicine* | **Published:** 14 September 2026
**PMID:** 42736503 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s12529-026-10488-w
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — surprising real-world uptake in a setting where it is rarely measured; descriptive design limits it
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 427 adults on antiretroviral treatment attending a large tertiary HIV clinic in Kano, northern Nigeria. Three-quarters were aware of AI chatbots and 72.6% had used one; 66.5% had used one for HIV-related questions — general treatment information, checking side effects, or preparing questions to ask their clinician. Use was predicted by internet access (aOR 6.67), younger age, post-secondary education, smartphone ownership, English proficiency and having comorbidities.

**Why it matters:** General-purpose chatbots have quietly become a first-line health information source in a setting with severe clinician shortages and heavy stigma around disclosure — largely outside the view of the health system that is supposed to be informing these patients.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a patient in Kano typing a question about a side effect into a chatbot at 11pm rather than waiting weeks for a clinic appointment. The surprise is scale: this is not a pilot program, it is unsanctioned, unmonitored, majority behavior. Note the equity twist — English proficiency and internet access gate who benefits, so the tool is most available to those who need it least. Call Zubairu Iliyasu at Bayero University Kano.
- **Wider angle:** A global-health feature on AI as informal infrastructure — the way chatbots are filling gaps in low- and middle-income health systems ahead of any regulation, and what happens when the same model that answers an antiretroviral question also hallucinates a drug interaction. Fits a global-health or development-focused outlet.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational; single tertiary center in one city, so not representative of rural or less-connected populations; self-reported use with no assessment of what the chatbots actually told people or whether it was accurate; interviewer-administered questionnaire risks social desirability effects.

### 6. People can't tell AI-generated faces from real photographs — and researchers are exploiting it

**Journal:** *Behavior Research Methods* | **Published:** 22 September 2026
**PMID:** 42773386 | **DOI:** 10.3758/s13428-026-03156-0
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — the "indistinguishable from real" result is the story, but this is fundamentally a stimulus-validation paper
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers at the University of Haifa generated and validated photorealistic AI face stimuli that independently varied emotional expression (happiness, sadness, neutrality, anger) and body weight, using the same synthetic "character" across conditions to control for individual facial features. Across three studies combining automated emotion classification with human ratings, the manipulations worked as intended — and participants could not reliably distinguish the generated images from real photographs.

**Why it matters:** Emotion research has been constrained for decades by small sets of posed photographs of real actors; being able to hold a face constant while changing one attribute at a time removes a confound that has dogged studies of weight bias, racial bias and age bias — while also confirming how thoroughly synthetic faces now pass as real.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the failure to tell fake from real, then pivot to the unexpected upside: the same technology powering deepfakes lets psychologists run an experiment they could never run before — the identical person, heavier or lighter, angry or sad, with nothing else changed. Open on the classic problem of confounded face databases. Call Shlomo Hareli at the University of Haifa; all stimuli and code are open on OSF, so the images are usable.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-and-ethics piece for a science-policy or research-integrity outlet on synthetic stimuli entering the behavioral science pipeline — how generative models inherit the biases of their training data, and what it means when the "faces" used to measure human bias were themselves produced by a biased system.

**Caveats:** Validation study, not a test of a psychological hypothesis; participant samples are online panels; realism judgments depend heavily on presentation format and viewing time; generated faces may carry training-data biases that the validation framework does not detect.

### 7. Sleep doctors watching the same airway video often disagree — and off-the-shelf AI doesn't fix it

**Journal:** *Sleep and Breathing* | **Published:** 21 September 2026
**PMID:** 42766212 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11325-026-03807-8
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a sobering look at diagnostic subjectivity in sleep apnea surgery, though small and retrospective
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Twelve clinicians (7 junior, 5 senior) and one general-purpose AI model with no task-specific training independently assessed 20 drug-induced sleep endoscopy recordings using the standard VOTE classification and proposed treatments. Human agreement on obstruction grading was poor to fair (weighted κ 0.255–0.425); pattern classification was somewhat better. Seniors agreed with expert consensus 70–90% of the time versus 35–85% for the AI, and exact agreement on treatment recommendations was 70% for seniors, 55% for juniors and 50% for the AI.

**Why it matters:** Sleep apnea surgery is planned on the basis of these recordings, and the study shows the underlying judgment is substantially operator-dependent — while a generic AI model, given no training on the task, already performs in the range of junior clinicians.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a patient about to have airway surgery, and the fact that which operation they get depends partly on who watched their video. The hook is the uncomfortable comparison: an untrained general-purpose model landed roughly where a junior specialist did. Frame it as a question about the reference standard rather than about AI replacing anyone. Call Federico Leone at Istituto Auxologico Italiano in Milan.
- **Wider angle:** A broader feature for a medical or health-policy outlet on inter-rater reliability as medicine's quiet crisis — the many diagnoses built on subjective visual judgment, and how AI benchmarking is exposing how shaky the human ground truth has always been.

**Caveats:** Small N (20 recordings, 12 raters), retrospective, single consortium of Italian centers; "expert consensus" reference standard is itself human-derived; the AI was a general-purpose model without task-specific training, so its performance says little about purpose-built systems; no patient outcomes.

### 8. Brain waves may be the reason AI can't be conscious, argues a leading philosopher of mind

**Journal:** *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* | **Published:** 17 September 2026
**PMID:** 42750504 | **DOI:** 10.1017/S0140525X25103567
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a sharp, quotable new line in the machine-consciousness debate, but it is a short commentary with no data
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** In a commentary on Anil Seth's target article on biological naturalism and conscious AI, Ned Block (New York University) advances a speculative argument that oscillatory brain activity generated by electrochemical processing may be necessary for consciousness. If so, consciousness would be unavailable not only to digital AI systems but potentially to some invertebrates as well. The same issue carries opposing commentaries from Blake Richards and Blaise Agüera y Arcas (Mila/Google) and from Lenore and Manuel Blum (Carnegie Mellon), both arguing that prediction, homeostasis and autopoiesis are inherently computational and that AI consciousness is therefore plausible.

**Why it matters:** The question of what physical substrate consciousness requires is moving from abstract philosophy to something with legal and ethical consequences, as people increasingly form attachments to systems that behave as though they have inner lives.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the specificity of Block's claim — not "machines lack souls" but "consciousness may require brain waves", a testable-in-principle criterion that would exclude silicon and possibly also some animals we assume are sentient. Open on the awkwardness of the invertebrate implication. Call Ned Block, and get the counterpunch from Blake Richards at Mila, whose commentary in the same issue argues the opposite from computability theory.
- **Wider angle:** A debate-format feature or podcast for a general-interest outlet built around the full set of commentaries on Seth's article — the rare moment when philosophers, theoretical computer scientists and industry AI researchers are arguing in the same forum, with Google-affiliated authors on the pro-machine-consciousness side and academic philosophers on the other.

**Caveats:** Commentary, not an empirical study; explicitly labeled speculative by its author; no new data or testable protocol offered; represents one position in an active and unresolved debate.

### 9. Chatbots are being told about suicidal thoughts — and they don't push people toward help

**Journal:** *Psychiatric Services* | **Published:** 16 June 2026 (issue 1 Sep 2026)
**PMID:** 42298343 | **DOI:** 10.1176/appi.ps.20250521
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — identifies a specific, fixable design gap in a high-stakes area, but it is an argument piece rather than new evidence
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Ball and Kleiman at Rutgers argue that general-purpose large language models have become a common place for people to first disclose suicidal thoughts, and that while these systems are getting better at detecting risk, they are not built to do the one thing that most reliably improves safety: prompting the user to tell a real person. They propose integrating disclosure-focused interventions — an existing, empirically supported target in suicide prevention research — directly into conversational AI.

**Why it matters:** The framing reorients a debate currently stuck on whether chatbots give harmful advice, toward what a chatbot should actively try to achieve in a crisis conversation.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the asymmetry: a person types something to a chatbot at 3am that they have never said to a friend, a parent or a clinician — and the conversation ends there. The hook is that detection is the part AI is getting good at and disclosure is the part that actually saves lives, and no major system is designed around it. Call Evan Kleiman at Rutgers, whose group works on real-time suicide risk monitoring. Do not frame chatbots as a clinical intervention.
- **Wider angle:** A regulation and product-design story for a technology or policy outlet on who sets the safety specification for crisis conversations — comparing the current patchwork of model guardrails with the standards applied to crisis text lines and helplines, and asking whether "route the user to a human" should be a legal requirement.

**Caveats:** Commentary/open forum piece with no new data; recommendations are untested in deployed systems; evidence base on whether AI-prompted disclosure produces the same benefits as spontaneous disclosure does not yet exist.

### 10. A questionnaire for spotting risky student drinking was built with help from ChatGPT

**Journal:** *International Journal of Methods in Psychiatric Research* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42473812 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mpr.70096
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a genuinely novel use of a language model in psychometrics, undercut by circular validation and no external sample
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (1 hits)

**The study:** Researchers at Çanakkale Onsekiz Mart University surveyed 599 university students with a 59-item pool covering behavioral, psychological, social and academic aspects of alcohol use, then used ChatGPT-assisted algorithms to select 15 items for a new instrument (SARAS-15). Machine learning classifiers applied to the resulting scores performed well (logistic regression: 93.5% accuracy; AUC 0.88–0.96 across three risk tiers), internal consistency was acceptable (α = 0.811), and scores correlated strongly with the established AUDIT (r = 0.861).

**Why it matters:** Item selection is one of the slowest, most judgment-laden steps in building a psychological measure; this is an early test of whether a language model can shortcut it — and a test case for how much scrutiny such instruments should get before use.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the provocation — a mental health screening tool whose questions were chosen by a chatbot. Open on the decades-long, expert-panel-heavy process of building something like the AUDIT, then ask what is lost when a model does the shortlisting. The honest tension is that the headline accuracy figures come from models tested on the same dataset that generated the items. Call Şükrü Alperen Korkmaz; get an independent psychometrician to assess the validation design.
- **Wider angle:** A research-methods feature for a specialist or academic-publishing outlet on language models entering the instrument-design pipeline across psychology and medicine — the coming wave of AI-assisted scales, and the absence of any agreed standard for reporting how a model was involved.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional, single-university Turkish student sample; machine learning performance metrics derived from the same dataset used for item selection, so accuracy is likely optimistic; no independent validation cohort; self-reported alcohol use; convergent validity against self-report instruments only, with no clinical diagnostic anchor.

### 11. Office ergonomics AI works well in the lab — then loses accuracy on new people

**Journal:** *Ergonomics* | **Published:** 30 August 2026
**PMID:** 42669165 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00140139.2026.2710143
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 4/10 — external validation studies are rare and important, but this one is small and the topic sits at the edge of the Mind desk
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Queensland University of Technology researchers took six machine learning models previously trained on 810 office workers to predict work-related musculoskeletal disorder risk and tested them on an independent, globally recruited sample of 74 participants. Generalization varied sharply by body region: moderate for high-prevalence areas such as neck and lower back, substantially worse for low-prevalence regions. Simplifying inputs from Likert scales to yes/no answers barely hurt performance.

**Why it matters:** It is a concrete example of the gap between reported and real-world performance in health prediction models, and a mildly encouraging finding that much simpler data collection loses little accuracy.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the deflation — a model that looked strong on its training population degrades when it meets a different set of workers, and does so unevenly depending on which body part you ask about. Frame it as a rare case of researchers testing their own tool and publishing the shortfall. Call Nektarios Karanikas at QUT.
- **Wider angle:** A workplace-technology investigation for a business or labor outlet on AI-driven "wellbeing" and injury-risk scoring being sold to employers — what happens when a model with region-specific blind spots is used to triage who gets a workstation assessment, and whether workers can see or contest their scores.

**Caveats:** Small external validation sample (N = 74), self-reported symptom and exposure data, heterogeneous global recruitment, no clinical confirmation of musculoskeletal disorders, and no prospective outcome data.

### 12. A new theory says hidden depression leaks out through your phone at night

**Journal:** *Asian Journal of Psychiatry* | **Published:** 10 September 2026
**PMID:** 42753424 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ajp.2026.105143
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a striking, testable idea with a strong cultural argument, but it is a narrative review with no new data
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** This narrative review proposes the "Digital Leakage Effect": a three-stage model in which daytime effort to conceal distress depletes prefrontal control resources, evening circadian decline further weakens inhibition, and the resulting loss of self-monitoring shows up overnight in passive smartphone signals — sleep fragmentation, keystroke kinematics, and divergence between what a person writes and when. The authors map all nine DSM-5 depression criteria onto passive digital markers and argue that models trained on Western samples risk misreading East and Southeast Asian emotional restraint as pathological masking.

**Why it matters:** It offers a specific, falsifiable mechanism for why concealed depression might be detectable at night but not during the day — relevant given that a large share of people who die by suicide deny suicidal ideation at their final clinical contact.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the statistic that haunts the field: up to 60% of people who die by suicide said no when asked, shortly before. Then the proposal — that the effort of hiding is itself measurable, and that it fails predictably after dark. Be explicit that this is a hypothesis awaiting data. Call Chunlin Fu at the Zhejiang Research Institute of Education Science, and get a digital-phenotyping researcher to say whether the nocturnal signal survives contact with real data.
- **Wider angle:** A privacy and ethics feature for a technology or civil-liberties outlet on passive mental health sensing — the proposal's own answer is federated learning and dynamic consent, but the underlying premise is continuous keystroke and sleep monitoring on personal phones, which is a story in itself.

**Caveats:** Narrative review and theoretical framework only — no empirical test, no validation cohort; DSM-5-to-sensor mappings are proposed rather than demonstrated; passive-sensing literature it draws on is heterogeneous and largely small-scale; the cultural-calibration claims are argued, not measured.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Chatbots as an unsanctioned first-line health service.** "Two-thirds of HIV patients at a Nigerian clinic are already asking chatbots about their treatment" (PMID 42736503) supplies real-world uptake data for exactly the behavior the previous digest could only infer from content audits — "ChatGPT's abortion advice is usually acceptable and almost never complete" (PMID 40550012) — and from stated preferences in "The people most likely to accept an AI therapist are the ones who struggle most with human relationships" (PMID 41549774). The Kano study shows the 3am chatbot query is majority behavior, not a hypothetical.
- **Disclosure to machines that never reaches a human.** "Chatbots are being told about suicidal thoughts — and they don't push people toward help" (PMID 42298343) extends the previous digest's developmental framing in "Chatbots are becoming part of how teenagers form an identity" (PMID 42664282) and the judgment-free-confidant mechanism in "People feel less judged when comfort comes from a machine" (PMID 42691276): the absence of a judging mind is precisely what makes the conversation a dead end.
- **AI as an audit of shaky human judgment.** "Sleep doctors watching the same airway video often disagree" (PMID 42766212) and "AI spots hidden brain disorganization in epilepsy tissue that looks normal to pathologists" (PMID 42767799) both use machine analysis to expose the limits of expert visual perception, continuing the line opened by "ChatGPT spots eating disorders that human clinicians miss in higher-weight patients" (PMID 42068161).

**Advances**

- **From prediction-model inflation to an actual external test.** The previous digest flagged overclaiming in "Machine learning can guess who will still be depressed after eating disorder treatment" (PMID 42117565), which lacked external validation. "Office ergonomics AI works well in the lab — then loses accuracy on new people" (PMID 42669165) is the missing experiment: the same team testing its own models on an independent sample and publishing the shortfall, with degradation concentrated in low-prevalence outcomes.
- **Language as measurement moves into passive sensing and into item writing.** "Describing your mood in your own words predicts sick leave better than a questionnaire does" (PMID 40974258) is advanced on two fronts: "A new theory says hidden depression leaks out through your phone at night" (PMID 42753424) pushes the signal from volunteered text to involuntary keystroke and sleep data, and "A questionnaire for spotting risky student drinking was built with help from ChatGPT" (PMID 42473812) puts the model on the other side of the instrument, choosing the items rather than reading the answers.
- **Synthetic social partners produce shallow but real effects.** "Chatting with an AI that plays a foreigner softens prejudice — but only on the surface" (PMID 42763964) gives an empirical depth-limit to the previous digest's finding that AI can substitute for a human in emotionally loaded interaction (PMID 42691276): explicit attitudes moved, the Implicit Association Test did not.
- **Trust, not competence, as the lever.** "Feeling good at using AI doesn't make students lean on it — trusting it does" (PMID 42804850) sharpens "Giving students an AI tutor does nothing much — unless you teach them how to study with it" (PMID 42600450); both point away from tool access and skill training and toward the routine and the calibration wrapped around the tool.

**Counterpoints**

- On how far off-the-shelf models get you: the previous digest's clinical-notes study found general-purpose LLMs underperforming a small purpose-built model (PMID 42671866), whereas an untrained general-purpose model landed in the range of junior specialists on drug-induced sleep endoscopy grading (PMID 42766212). The reconciliation is probably the quality of the human reference standard, which is itself poor in the sleep study.

**New themes**

- **Attitudes to AI as a target of manipulation in their own right.** "Saying 'we' instead of 'I' makes people more afraid of AI" (PMID 40745650) has no analogue in the previous digest, which studied what AI does to people rather than what framing does to AI perception.
- **Machine consciousness as a substrate argument.** "Brain waves may be the reason AI can't be conscious" (PMID 42750504) opens a philosophical strand absent last time.
- **AI as research apparatus.** "People can't tell AI-generated faces from real photographs — and researchers are exploiting it" (PMID 42773386), alongside PMIDs 42473812 and 42767799, marks a shift from AI as object of study to AI as the instrument doing the measuring; the previous digest's social-listening analysis (PMID 42673770) now reads as an early case of the same thing.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** In this batch, AI has stopped being the thing under the microscope and become the microscope. Psychologists are now running bias experiments on faces a generative model invented, which participants cannot distinguish from photographs (PMID 42773386); a screening instrument for student drinking had its items shortlisted by ChatGPT (PMID 42473812); spatial statistics applied to whole-slide images are redefining which epilepsy tissue counts as "normal" (PMID 42767799); an untrained general-purpose model is being benchmarked against clinicians whose own agreement is poor to fair (PMID 42766212); and a theoretical paper proposes mapping all nine DSM-5 depression criteria onto passive phone signals (PMID 42753424). The validation machinery has not kept pace: the drinking scale's accuracy figures come from the same dataset that produced its items, and the one study that did test its models on fresh people watched performance fall (PMID 42669165).

**Why pitch this now:** Debate about AI in science is fixed on chatbot-written papers and peer review, while the quieter change — models generating the stimuli, writing the items, defining the ground truth and setting the reference standard — is already in print across pathology, psychometrics and behavioral science with no agreed way to report or check it.

**Angle:** A reported piece on the validation gap: what it takes to certify a measuring instrument, how each of these papers falls short of that bar, and what a reporting standard for AI-built measures would have to require. For readers who follow how science actually gets made — researchers, clinicians, research-integrity and methods people, and the general science audience that will eventually be scored by these tools.
