### 1. Excess covid deaths among people with severe mental illness did not shrink after vaccines arrived

**Journal:** *The Lancet Psychiatry* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42586081 | **DOI:** 10.1016/S2215-0366(26)00195-1
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — whole-country data with a genuinely uncomfortable result; limited only by the fact that covid stories are a crowded field.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers linked electronic health records for essentially the entire primary-care population of England and analyzed 13,463,945 people who caught SARS-CoV-2 between January 2020 and June 2023, of whom 160,190 had a severe mental illness (schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, bipolar disorder, or other psychosis). People with severe mental illness were about 56% more likely to die after infection overall — and the gap was *smaller* in the first, pre-vaccine wave (adjusted hazard ratio 1.27) than during (1.56) and after (1.55) the vaccine roll-out. Vaccine uptake started higher in this group than in the general population but ended lower (79.4% versus 87.2% fully vaccinated).

**Why it matters:** The tools that closed the mortality gap for most of the population failed to close it here, and the early advantage in vaccination was lost — pointing to a system that reaches people with psychosis at the start of a campaign and then loses them.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The intuitive story is that vaccines leveled the playing field; the data say the opposite — the survival penalty attached to a psychosis diagnosis got *wider* once vaccines were available. Open on the reversal in the vaccination figures: this group was ahead in early 2021 and eight points behind by 2023. Call Jayati Das-Munshi at King's College London, who led the work and convened a patient and public involvement panel; ask what "reached first, then dropped" says about how mental health care and physical health care fail to talk to each other. Observational data — frame as inequality, not as a causal claim about vaccines.
- **Wider angle:** A health-policy feature for a general outlet on the "campaign cliff edge": public health drives that front-load outreach to vulnerable groups and then quietly stop, using severe mental illness as the case study alongside learning disability and homelessness registers. Fits *The Guardian*'s health desk or *BMJ* analysis.

**Caveats:** Observational design, cannot establish causation; diagnoses drawn from routine records so severe mental illness may be under-captured; England-only, so generalization to other health systems is uncertain.

### 2. Semaglutide beats every other drug tested for antipsychotic weight gain

**Journal:** *JAMA Psychiatry* | **Published:** 1 September 2026
**PMID:** 42418179 | **DOI:** 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2026.1814
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 8/10 — the first full network comparison putting GLP-1 drugs against the older options in this population; small trial counts for semaglutide temper it.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** A systematic review and network meta-analysis pooled 95 randomized trials (N=5898) of 39 different drugs given to reduce weight in people with schizophrenia spectrum disorders who were taking antipsychotics. Semaglutide came out far ahead (mean difference −10.98 kg versus placebo, moderate certainty), followed by liraglutide (−5.43 kg), topiramate (−3.95 kg), metformin (−3.86 kg) and exenatide (−2.97 kg). Only semaglutide and metformin produced clinically meaningful weight change of 5% or more, and dropout rates did not differ notably between treatments.

**Why it matters:** Antipsychotic-induced weight gain is one of the biggest drivers of the roughly 15-20 year life expectancy gap in schizophrenia, and until now the evidence base was a scatter of small trials with no way to rank the options.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the number: 11 kg, in the group of patients for whom weight gain is not vanity but a lifespan issue. The hook is that the GLP-1 gold rush has largely bypassed psychiatry, where the metabolic harm is iatrogenic — caused by the treatment itself. Call Margaret Hahn or Sri Mahavir Agarwal at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health in Toronto. Note the honest limitation on air: only three semaglutide trials, and the field needs bigger ones.
- **Wider angle:** A specialist feature on the ethics and economics of who gets GLP-1 drugs — people with schizophrenia have among the strongest medical cases and among the weakest positions in the queue for a rationed, expensive drug. Suits *STAT* or a health-economics outlet.

**Caveats:** Only 2-5 trials each for the top-ranked drugs; short follow-up in most; several authors report consulting or speaker fees from pharmaceutical companies (Boehringer Ingelheim, Bristol Myers Squibb, HLS Therapeutics, Lundbeck, Otsuka and others), and one author holds a biotech patent — all disclosed in the paper.

### 3. A stress-hormone gene is switched up in schizophrenia — and aging pushes it the same way

**Journal:** *American Journal of Psychiatry* | **Published:** 26 August 2026
**PMID:** 42642806 | **DOI:** 10.1176/appi.ajp.20250473
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — postmortem work with two independent cohorts and single-nucleus validation; abstract mechanism is a harder sell to general readers.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using DNA methylation arrays, genotyping and bulk, single-nucleus RNA and chromatin-accessibility data from postmortem prefrontal and orbitofrontal cortex across two cohorts (378 people who had lived with schizophrenia, depression or bipolar disorder; 209 controls), researchers mapped the epigenetic switches controlling *FKBP5*, a gene that regulates the brain's response to cortisol. They found specific sites in the gene's proximal enhancer where less methylation means more gene expression, in all major cell types. People with schizophrenia had significantly lower methylation at exactly those sites — and age and the common risk variant rs1360780 pushed in the same direction.

**Why it matters:** It offers a concrete molecular route by which stress biology, genetic risk and aging converge on the same regulatory switch in the frontal cortex, and *FKBP5* blockers are already in early psychiatric trials.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is the convergence: three separate risk factors — a diagnosis, a genotype, and simply getting older — all land on the same handful of chemical tags on one gene in the frontal cortex. Open with what a brain bank actually is and what it takes to read a switch this small in donated tissue. Call Natalie Matosin at the University of Sydney or Elisabeth Binder at the Max Planck Institute of Psychiatry in Munich, whose group has driven the *FKBP5* story for two decades. Do not imply anyone should be tested or treated on this basis.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on whether "epigenetic aging" of the brain is the missing link between chronic stress and psychiatric illness — and why postmortem brain banks are becoming the field's most valuable and most fragile infrastructure. Fits *Nature* news features or a science-magazine long read.

**Caveats:** Postmortem and correlational; secondary analysis of previously generated datasets; cause and effect cannot be separated (illness, medication and lifestyle can all alter methylation); no living-participant validation.

### 4. Trauma therapy for psychosis works on questionnaires — but not on people's phone diaries

**Journal:** *Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics* | **Published:** 27 August 2026
**PMID:** 42658758 | **DOI:** 10.1159/pps/aemag010
**Novelty:** Failed replication
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a rare, honest measurement-mismatch result inside a positive RCT; the story is about how we measure minds, which is squarely NS Mind territory.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** In a subsample of the STAR randomized trial, 153 adults with both PTSD and a schizophrenia-spectrum diagnosis were assigned to trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy for psychosis plus usual care (n=73) or usual care alone (n=80), and reported intrusive memories, hyperarousal, dissociation, avoidance and trauma-related thoughts several times a day for six days via a smartphone app, at baseline and nine months. The trial's headline benefit, measured retrospectively by clinician interview, was not reproduced in the momentary data: there were no between-group differences at nine months, only weaker interaction effects showing faster decline in hyperarousal, negative cognitions and avoidance in the therapy arm.

**Why it matters:** It suggests recalled symptoms and moment-to-moment symptoms are partly different things — which matters for every trial in psychiatry that rests on a questionnaire administered after the fact.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the awkward gap: the same trial, the same patients, two ways of asking — and only one shows the therapy clearly working. Open on what an experience-sampling week feels like from the inside: a phone beeping at you eight times a day asking whether the memory is here right now. Call Filippo Varese or Amy Hardy from the STAR team, and ask the blunt question — which measure should regulators believe? Frame as a measurement problem, not as evidence the therapy fails.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-and-trust feature for a specialist audience on experience sampling as psychiatry's replication stress test, alongside similar mismatches in depression and anxiety trials. Fits *Nature Human Behaviour* commentary or a research-integrity outlet.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of an RCT subsample; self-reported momentary outcomes; six-day sampling windows may miss slower-moving change; possible reduced power relative to the parent trial.

### 5. Almost a quarter of older people with psychosis have dementia

**Journal:** *Asia-Pacific Psychiatry* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42615098 | **DOI:** 10.1111/appy.70033
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a striking pooled figure, but very high heterogeneity means the number itself is soft.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** A systematic review and meta-analysis pooled prevalence and incidence estimates of dementia among people with schizophrenia and other primary psychotic disorders. After excluding one outlier, pooled dementia prevalence in older adults was 24.3% (95% CI 20.5-28.5%), with very high between-study heterogeneity (I²=84.7%) driven by setting, region, age and how prevalence was defined. Incidence estimates varied too widely to pool meaningfully.

**Why it matters:** Cognitive decline in later-life schizophrenia has long been described as part of the illness rather than as dementia; a one-in-four figure reframes it as a service-planning problem sitting at the junction of two specialties that rarely share patients.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is a diagnostic borderland: when an older person with a 40-year history of schizophrenia starts losing memory, who decides whether that is the illness or Alzheimer's — and does anyone even look? Open on the fact that psychiatric and dementia services are usually separate buildings with separate records. Call Chuan Shi's co-authors at Shanghai Mental Health Center, or Stefan Leucht at the Technical University of Munich, a co-author with a long track record in psychosis evidence synthesis. Be explicit that the pooled number is heterogeneous.
- **Wider angle:** A health-services piece on aging with severe mental illness — the first large cohort of people to live decades on antipsychotics is now reaching their seventies, and no one designed a system for them. Suits a specialist aging or health-policy outlet.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of heterogeneous observational studies (I²>84%); definitions of dementia and of psychosis vary between sources; few population-based studies, which the authors themselves flag; incidence data too inconsistent to pool.

### 6. People who see hallucinations face a choice between explanations that convince and ones they can live with

**Journal:** *Psychology and Psychotherapy: Theory, Research and Practice* | **Published:** 21 August 2026
**PMID:** 42627099 | **DOI:** 10.1111/papt.70074
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a genuinely fresh psychological insight with a strong human core; qualitative and small.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Twelve people with psychosis who had recently experienced visual hallucinations gave semi-structured interviews, analyzed with reflexive thematic analysis. Participants consistently lacked an explanation that was both believable and bearable. The explanations offered to them — stress, poor sleep, "your brain generating it" — felt acceptable but unconvincing, because they failed to account for how detailed and lifelike the visions were. The explanations that felt convincing ("it's real", "it's here to harm me") were the distressing ones, and which explanation held sway shifted with mood and with whether a vision was present.

**Why it matters:** It identifies a specific therapeutic target — supplying an account of visions that is credible enough to compete with the threatening one — and it puts visual hallucinations, usually treated as a footnote to hearing voices, at the center.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Most readers assume the reassuring explanation is the helpful one. Here it is the opposite: telling someone their vision was stress does not survive contact with how real the vision looked, so the frightening explanation wins by default. Open on one participant's line — "I just want an explanation." Call Robert Dudley in York or Felicity Waite at Oxford, who works on sleep and psychosis, and ask what a genuinely convincing non-threatening explanation of a lifelike vision would even sound like.
- **Wider angle:** A features piece on visual hallucinations across conditions — Parkinson's, Charles Bonnet syndrome, bereavement, psychosis — and why the explanation a person is given shapes how frightening the experience becomes. Fits a general-interest magazine or a psychology outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (12), qualitative and not designed to generalize; single research group; participants recruited via UK NHS services, so cultural framing of hallucinations may not transfer.

### 7. Personality traits predict who develops three major psychiatric disorders — but not schizophrenia

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 1 September 2026
**PMID:** 42680113 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122460
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a well-powered family study with a clean negative result that cuts against a popular assumption; self-report measures limit it.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers assessed adult temperament and childhood adversity in 1162 people from 393 Indian multiplex families affected by schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder or alcohol use disorder — 532 affected individuals, 457 unaffected first-degree relatives and 173 controls. Each disorder had a distinctive temperament configuration, but adverse childhood experiences were significantly associated only with alcohol use disorder. In an independent validation cohort, the temperament model predicted alcohol use disorder (AUC 0.822), bipolar disorder (0.742) and OCD (0.752), and completely failed for schizophrenia (AUC 0.526 — no better than chance).

**Why it matters:** The idea that a certain temperament, or a rough childhood, sets someone up for psychosis is widely assumed; in this large family sample neither predicted schizophrenia at all, while both mapped onto other diagnoses.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the flat line: a model that spots alcohol use disorder, bipolar disorder and OCD in an independent sample can't tell schizophrenia from health. The hook is that adverse childhood experiences — currently a near-universal explanatory frame in mental health — showed a significant association with only one of the four disorders here. Call Biju Viswanath or Srinivas Balachander at NIMHANS in Bangalore about the ADBS multiplex-family design and why family-based sampling gives a different answer from case-control studies.
- **Wider angle:** A critical trend piece on the reach of the "ACEs" framework in public health and schools — a genuinely large study where childhood adversity did not predict three of the four disorders examined. Fits a policy or social-science outlet.

**Caveats:** Self-reported temperament and retrospectively reported childhood adversity; cross-sectional and observational; recruited from multiplex families at a single Indian tertiary center, so the sample is high genetic loading and may not generalize; null AUC could partly reflect measurement insensitivity rather than absence of effect.

### 8. A brain response was supposed to be weaker in people at risk of psychosis — it turned out stronger

**Journal:** *The World Journal of Biological Psychiatry* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42274463 | **DOI:** 10.1080/15622975.2026.2684757
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a directional reversal in a well-known biomarker, honestly reported; small sample keeps it from scoring higher.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Thirty-six antipsychotic-naive young people at clinical high risk for psychosis and 21 healthy controls had EEG recorded while listening to click trains at 40 Hz, a standard test of the brain's gamma-frequency auditory steady-state response — a signal known to be *reduced* in established schizophrenia. Over two years of follow-up, the high-risk participants whose symptoms did not remit showed *larger* 40 Hz evoked power than controls; those who remitted did not differ. The authors had predicted the opposite and suggest the enlargement reflects a phase of cortical hyperexcitability before illness consolidates.

**Why it matters:** If the same measure runs high before psychosis and low after, then the trajectory — not the snapshot — is the biomarker, which changes how these signals should be used in prediction models.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is a sign flip: a brain signal textbooks describe as blunted in schizophrenia was *amplified* in the young people whose symptoms persisted. Open on the clinic problem this is trying to solve — a teenager with early symptoms, and no way to say who will recover. Call Michael Kiang at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health in Toronto, or Gregory Light in San Diego, who has spent a career on this EEG measure. Emphasize that the sample is antipsychotic-naive, which makes the result unusually clean.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on the excitation/inhibition-imbalance model of psychosis: does the brain overshoot before it undershoots, and does that open a treatment window? Fits a neuroscience-focused specialist outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (36 at-risk, 21 controls), single center; contrary to the pre-stated hypothesis, so needs independent replication before use; remission defined clinically over two years, and follow-up may be too short to capture later transitions.

### 9. Fidgety, restless movements in early psychosis foreshadow memory problems two years on

**Journal:** *Schizophrenia Bulletin* | **Published:** 20 August 2026
**PMID:** 42640953 | **DOI:** 10.1093/schbul/sbag128
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — prospective multicenter data on an overlooked and easily observed sign; effect sizes are modest.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** In a multicenter naturalistic Spanish cohort, 159 people with a first episode of psychosis were assessed clinically, cognitively and functionally at two months and again at two years, with motor abnormalities scored on hypokinetic (slowed) and hyperkinetic (excess movement) dimensions derived from the PANSS. Hyperkinetic severity at two months predicted worse vocabulary, verbal memory and global cognition at two years (r=−0.28 to −0.37), and growth mixture modeling identified sustained high-hyperkinetic trajectories that were linked to poorer verbal memory and worse overall functioning at two years.

**Why it matters:** Motor signs are cheap and observable without a scanner, and this suggests they track something meaningful about cognitive trajectory in early psychosis — though whether they are cause, consequence, or medication effect remains open.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The pitch is the return of an old clinical idea: before antipsychotics existed, psychiatrists described psychosis as a disorder of movement as much as thought, then attributed all the movement to the drugs. Here movement patterns visible in the first weeks track cognitive decline over two years. Open on what a hyperkinetic presentation actually looks like in a clinic room. Call Manuel J. Cuesta in Pamplona or Miquel Bernardo in Barcelona from the PEPs group. Observational — no clinical action implied.
- **Wider angle:** A history-of-medicine feature on how the drug era erased motor symptoms from the description of schizophrenia, and what digital gait and movement sensing is now recovering. Fits a long-form science or medical-history outlet.

**Caveats:** Observational, cannot establish causation; motor dimensions derived from a symptom rating scale rather than instrumented measurement; antipsychotic exposure is a plausible confounder; N=159 with attrition over two years; naturalistic treatment.

### 10. Clinicians' suicide risk ratings miss most of the people who go on to attempt

**Journal:** *Journal of Affective Disorders* | **Published:** 1 September 2026
**PMID:** 42044698 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.121863
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — enormous sample and a sobering result about expert judgment; the population is specific to US veterans.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using clinician-administered suicide risk assessments extracted from electronic medical records, researchers examined 249,011 US veterans experiencing criminal-legal involvement, homelessness, or both. One in seven reported suicidal ideation at assessment and one in 32 was rated high acute risk; within 12 months, one in 37 had a documented suicide attempt. Attempts were most common in people with bipolar disorders, psychotic disorders other than schizophrenia, and personality disorders. Crucially, clinician-identified risk was highly specific but had low sensitivity — most people who later attempted had not been flagged.

**Why it matters:** Structured clinical risk assessment is the backbone of suicide prevention in most health systems, and at this scale it caught only a minority of subsequent attempts.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the arithmetic of a false reassurance: a quarter of a million risk assessments, and the people who went on to attempt suicide were mostly not the ones marked high risk. The hook is that "risk stratification" may be the wrong paradigm entirely. Open on the assessment form itself — a handful of questions, minutes long, carrying enormous weight. Call Emily Edwards at the VISN 2 MIRECC in the Bronx, or Lisa Brenner in Colorado. Present as a critique of prediction, not of clinicians.
- **Wider angle:** A piece for a health-systems or investigative outlet on the shift from predicting individuals to changing environments — means restriction, housing, legal diversion — as the intervention that actually moves suicide rates in marginalized populations.

**Caveats:** Retrospective analysis of routinely collected records; outcomes limited to *documented* attempts, so undercounting is likely; US veterans experiencing criminal-legal involvement or homelessness is a specific population; no control group of non-assessed patients.

### 11. Schizophrenia leaves an audible mark on speech — its milder cousin does not

**Journal:** *Schizophrenia Research* | **Published:** 2 September 2026
**PMID:** 42685439 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.schres.2026.08.016
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a clean dissociation across the schizophrenia spectrum with a computational-speech hook; small groups.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Automated acoustic analysis was applied to recordings of open-ended interviews with 27 people with schizotypal personality disorder, 36 with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder, and 44 healthy controls, alongside diagnostic interviews, symptom ratings and social cognition tasks. The schizophrenia group showed altered articulatory control, spectral stability and temporal coordination; the schizotypal group was acoustically indistinguishable from controls. Across the two clinical groups, flatter acoustic features tracked more severe negative symptoms and poorer social cognition.

**Why it matters:** Schizotypal personality disorder sits on the same spectrum but these participants are typically antipsychotic-naive and never hospitalized — so the absence of acoustic change in that group is informative about what the schizophrenia signal actually reflects.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The interesting bit is the negative half: if voice analysis is going to be a diagnostic tool, it apparently detects schizophrenia but is silent on the spectrum condition next door. Open on the practical scene — a microphone, a conversational interview, and software that finds things a listener cannot name. Call Samantha Fradkin or Erin Hazlett at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, and press them on the confound they cannot rule out: medication.
- **Wider angle:** An investigation for a technology or civil-liberties outlet into voice biomarkers being commercialized for mental health screening, using this study's spectrum-specificity finding to show how far the science is from the marketing.

**Caveats:** Small N per group (27/36/44), single site; cross-sectional; antipsychotic exposure differs systematically between the clinical groups and could account for the acoustic difference; one speech task only, as the authors note.

### 12. Leading researchers argue the dopamine theory of schizophrenia can no longer stand alone

**Journal:** *JAMA Psychiatry* | **Published:** 1 September 2026
**PMID:** 42418174 | **DOI:** 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2026.1684
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a field-defining reframing by an unusually senior author list, but it is an evidence review rather than new data.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Twelve senior schizophrenia researchers synthesized neuroimaging, postmortem, genetic, pharmacological-challenge and clinical-trial evidence from 1980 to 2025 to test whether schizophrenia and related disorders converge on a "final common pathway" of dopamine dysregulation. They conclude it does not: about a third of patients show treatment resistance with no elevation in striatal dopamine synthesis capacity, and the efficacy of the muscarinic agonist xanomeline-trospium, which does not block D2 receptors, shows psychosis can be reduced through non-dopaminergic routes. They propose a pluralistic model involving glutamate, GABA, acetylcholine, serotonin, endocannabinoid and opioid systems plus oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation.

**Why it matters:** The dopamine hypothesis has organized both drug development and public understanding of psychosis for 50 years; this is the field's most senior voices saying it explains one symptom domain in some patients, not the disorder.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is a textbook being rewritten by the people who wrote it. Lead on the drug that broke the model — a muscarinic agent that reduces hallucinations and delusions without touching dopamine receptors — then explain why a third of patients were never fitted by the theory in the first place. Call Matcheri Keshavan at Beth Israel Deaconess, or Anissa Abi-Dargham at Stony Brook, whose imaging work established the striatal dopamine finding now being bounded.
- **Wider angle:** A feature on how a single-neurotransmitter story takes hold — dopamine for psychosis, serotonin for depression — and what fragmentation into subgroups means for patients waiting for the next drug. Fits a general-interest science magazine or *Mosaic*-style long read.

**Caveats:** Narrative evidence synthesis, not a systematic review; studies identified partly through "expert knowledge" and appraised qualitatively; no new data; authors include many with extensive pharmaceutical industry relationships and long-standing theoretical commitments.

### 13. A drug that raises prolactin sharply does not harm sexual function more than one that doesn't

**Journal:** *The World Journal of Biological Psychiatry* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42439529 | **DOI:** 10.1080/15622975.2026.2697979
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — cleanly contradicts a standard prescribing assumption, but it's a secondary analysis with modest power.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using data from the ACLAIMS trial, 175 participants (paliperidone palmitate n=86; haloperidol decanoate n=89) were followed for 24 months with repeated Arizona Sexual Experience Scale assessments and prolactin measurements. Paliperidone produced significantly higher prolactin at every timepoint, yet sexual function trajectories did not differ between the two drugs, and an exploratory late window even favored paliperidone. The counterintuitive line is that the hormone everyone blames tracks *change within a person* but does not distinguish between drugs. Open on how rarely this side effect gets asked about in a psychiatric appointment. Call Alessandro Serretti at the Kore University of Enna. Frame as complicating prescribing rationale, not as advice.
- **Wider angle:** A health-reporting piece on sexuality as psychiatry's neglected outcome — undermeasured in trials, rarely raised in clinics, and a leading unstated reason people quit medication. Fits a consumer-health or long-form magazine.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of a completed trial; self-reported sexual function; N=175 with repeated-measures attrition; the significant late-window comparison is explicitly exploratory; single author.

### 14. People with schizophrenia living at home function better — and are at higher risk of suicide

**Journal:** *Australasian Psychiatry* | **Published:** 11 August 2026
**PMID:** 42581341 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10398562261476921
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a sharp, socially loaded trade-off, but small and cross-sectional.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** In a cross-sectional Turkish study of 68 clinically stable people with schizophrenia — 34 living in institutions and 34 living at home — researchers compared symptoms, functioning, childhood trauma, depression and suicidal ideation. Institutionalized patients had poorer functioning and higher PANSS symptom scores; home-living patients had significantly higher suicide risk (p=0.014) despite similar levels of depressive symptoms. **Why it matters:** Deinstitutionalization is usually framed as unambiguously good; these data suggest the two settings carry different risks — functional decline in one, suicidality in the other — which is an argument about what community care must supply, not against it.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the trade-off no one plans for: the group doing better on every functioning measure was the group with the higher suicide risk, and their depression scores were no worse. - **Wider angle:** An international comparison feature on what happened after the asylums closed in different countries, and why "community care" means a supported flat in one place and a family's spare room in another. Fits a global-health or social-affairs outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (68), single region in Türkiye, cross-sectional and observational so causal direction is indeterminate (people who function better may be the ones placed at home); retrospective self-reported childhood trauma; suicide risk measured by scale, not outcome.

### 15. Waiting to prescribe clozapine costs patients function they don't get back

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychiatric Research* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42155318 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2026.05.019
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — clinically consequential and a good news peg on clozapine reform; retrospective with subjective outcome scales.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (1 hits)

**The study:** Records of 150 Japanese patients with treatment-resistant schizophrenia started on clozapine were analyzed retrospectively, split into "early" and "late" treatment-resistance subtypes and stratified by dopamine supersensitivity psychosis status. The late-onset resistance group improved significantly more on global functioning, and dopamine supersensitivity — more common in that group — was associated with greater functional gain. Regression identified baseline functioning, age at resistance designation, and the *delay* between being recognized as treatment-resistant and actually starting clozapine as significant predictors; in the supersensitivity subgroup, longer delay correlated with less improvement.

**Why it matters:** Clozapine is the only drug licensed for treatment-resistant schizophrenia and is chronically underused; this offers a mechanism-flavored reason why the delay itself, not just the diagnosis, shapes outcome.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is timing: within a group of people who all eventually got the same drug, the ones who waited longer recovered less function. Add the twist that patients whose resistance emerged late — arguably the ones clinicians least expect to respond — did best. Open on the paperwork and monitoring wall between a patient and this prescription, newly relaxed in the US in 2025. Call Nobuhisa Kanahara at Chiba University. Observational data — no dosing or timing advice.
- **Wider angle:** A regulatory feature on the loosening of clozapine monitoring rules and what that has meant in practice, drawing on the parallel argument that decades of blood-test requirements suppressed prescribing far beyond what the agranulocytosis risk warranted. Fits a health-policy or pharmacy-specialist outlet.

**Caveats:** Retrospective chart review; no randomization or control group, so delay may be a marker of illness severity or service disorganization rather than a cause; outcome measures (GAF, SOFAS, CGI) are clinician-rated; several authors report honoraria from antipsychotic manufacturers including Otsuka, Sumitomo, Janssen and Meiji Seika; Japanese services may not generalize.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Six studies in this batch converge on the physical body of the person with psychosis rather than the mind, and they point the same uncomfortable way: the excess-mortality gap in severe mental illness got *wider* after covid vaccines became available (PMID 42586081), roughly a quarter of older people with psychosis now meet criteria for dementia (PMID 42615098), and the metabolic harm that drives much of the life expectancy gap is treatment-caused — with semaglutide the first drug to shift it meaningfully (−10.98 kg, PMID 42418179) while a fashionable alternative, probiotics, fades by week four (PMID 42596905). Alongside these sit two studies about neglected bodily outcomes and delayed access: sexual function barely tracks the prolactin logic used to justify prescribing choices (PMID 42439529), and the delay between being labeled treatment-resistant and actually starting clozapine predicts how much function is never recovered (PMID 42155318). Together they describe a population whose new tools exist but arrive late, unevenly, or not at all.

**Why pitch this now:** The GLP-1 story has been told through obesity and diabetes for three years and has almost entirely skipped the group with the strongest medical case and the weakest place in the queue — arriving at the same moment as whole-country evidence that the pandemic's health inequalities in psychosis did not close, and the first pooled estimate of how many of these patients are now aging into dementia.

**Angle:** Follow the physical health of people with schizophrenia across a lifetime — weight gain in the first year on an antipsychotic, decades of undiscussed side effects and delayed clozapine, then dementia and early death in a service system where psychiatry and physical medicine occupy different buildings and different records. For a general science and health readership that knows the "15-year life expectancy gap" as a statistic but not as a sequence of specific, fixable failures.

**Potential headlines:**
- One in four older people with psychosis has dementia, and no service is looking for it
- The mortality gap in severe mental illness got wider after the vaccines arrived
- Growing old with schizophrenia in a system built for crisis, not for decades
- The obesity drugs that psychiatry never got round to prescribing

**Potential outlets:**
- **New Scientist Features** — the batch supplies the hard numbers (England-wide records, a 95-trial network meta-analysis) NS needs to run a structural health story rather than a single-finding item.
- **STAT** — owns the GLP-1 access-and-rationing beat; the argument that iatrogenic weight gain gives psychiatric patients an unusually strong claim on a scarce drug is squarely theirs.
- **The Guardian** science/health desk — the covid mortality analysis is NHS primary-care data with a policy failure attached ("reached first, then dropped"), and they cover health inequality as a running story.
- **Undark** — suits the longer investigative treatment of a population that falls between two specialties, including the clozapine delay and the dementia diagnostic borderland.
