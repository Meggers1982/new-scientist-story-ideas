### 1. Hostile immigration policies erode children's mental health advantage — even for US-born Asian kids

**PMID:** 42545350 | **DOI:** 10.1037/ort0000952
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 9/10 — Highly counterintuitive racial spillover finding, large nationally representative US sample, clean survey design with policy climate index, strong societal hook around immigration and child mental health, and clear relevance to NS Mind's broad readership.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Using data from the 2023 National Survey of Children's Health (a large, nationally representative US dataset) and the 2022 Immigration Policy Climate index, researchers at Rutgers University examined whether state-level immigration policy hostility was associated with changes in child mental health among children with immigrant versus US-born parents. Survey-weighted logistic regression found that children with immigrant parents generally had better mental health than those with US-born parents — consistent with the 'healthy immigrant paradox' — but this advantage modestly but significantly diminished in states with more hostile immigration policies. Critically, a 'racial spillover' effect emerged: Asian and Pacific Islander children faced increased odds of mental health problems as state policy hostility rose regardless of whether they themselves had immigrant parents.

**Why it matters:** The finding suggests that punitive immigration policies may damage the mental health of racially minoritised children who are themselves citizens, extending harm well beyond immigrant families and raising questions about the broad societal costs of restrictive immigration environments.

**The study:** Researchers analysed longitudinal cognitive ability data collected prospectively from 1957 to 1999, examining IQ test scores of 1,187 children and adolescents aged 7–15 years (89% White, 9% Black, 52% female) across three versions of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children. **Why it matters:** Understanding that distraction suppression is an active cognitive process rather than a structural filter has implications for research into attention disorders and for designing environments that reduce unwanted cognitive interruption.

This reframes 'staying focused' not as a passive state we slip into but as a continuous mental effort — which may help explain why concentration is so exhausting and why attention disorders are so disruptive.
