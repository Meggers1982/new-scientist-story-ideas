### 1. Posts loaded with moral language get less engagement, not more

**Journal:** *Nature Human Behaviour* | **Published:** 2 September 2026
**PMID:** 42686956 | **DOI:** 10.1038/s41562-026-02560-y
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — 1.6 million posts across three very different platforms, a clean inverted-U, and it contradicts the "moral outrage always wins" story everyone now repeats.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 1,621,147 posts covering 13 socio-political topics on Twitter (n=530,104), Reddit (n=1,048,653) and 8chan (n=42,390), using word embeddings scored against an expert-validated moral dictionary. They separated a post's overall moral relevance ("moral loading") from how concentrated moral words were across the text ("moral density"). More moral relevance did predict more engagement — but holding that constant, higher moral density predicted sharply *less* engagement, with a peak at density 0.30 and roughly two- to three-fold drops on either side.

**Why it matters:** The dominant account of online discourse is that moralized, outraged language is rocket fuel for virality. This suggests there is a ceiling: saturate a post with moral words and audiences disengage, which reframes moralization as something readers can be over-served rather than endlessly rewarded.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Everyone "knows" outrage wins online — this says over-moralizing is self-defeating, and there is a measurable sweet spot. Open on a campaigner or poster who ratchets up the moral intensity of their message and watches engagement fall off a cliff. Call Cristian Candia at Universidad del Desarrollo, or Mohammad Atari at UMass Amherst on the moral-language measurement. Note it is observational engagement data, not an experiment in persuasion.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a politics or media outlet on the professionalization of moral rhetoric — how charities, activist groups and political campaigns calibrate outrage, and what an "overmoralization penalty" means for messages that are genuinely urgent. Fits a long read in a current-affairs or communications-focused publication.

**Caveats:** Observational design — cannot establish causation, and engagement metrics are platform artefacts rather than measures of persuasion or attitude change; dictionary-based text analysis may miss sarcasm and context; English-language platforms only; 8chan sample much smaller than the others.

### 2. AI influencers dent women's body image just as much as real ones

**Journal:** *Body Image* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 30 June 2026)
**PMID:** 42379075 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bodyim.2026.102130
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — randomized exposure experiment with a decent sample and a clear, quotable null: knowing an influencer is fake doesn't protect you.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** 303 women aged 18-74, recruited via Prolific, were randomly assigned to view Instagram images of human influencers, virtual (digitally created) influencers, or architectural images as a control. Both influencer conditions increased body dissatisfaction relative to control, and the human and virtual conditions did not differ from each other on any outcome. Facial dissatisfaction and interest in cosmetic surgery were not significantly affected, and neither age nor habitual appearance comparison moderated the effects.

**Why it matters:** Virtual influencers are sometimes framed as a harmless alternative to real bodies, since no human is being edited. This early evidence suggests the psychological hit lands the same way regardless of whether the face on the screen belongs to anyone.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead on the surprise that a body which never existed makes women feel as bad as one that does — and that even women who say they rarely compare themselves weren't spared. Open on the growing roster of brand-owned virtual influencers with millions of followers. Call Jennifer Harriger at Pepperdine University, and flag her disclosed role as a plaintiffs' expert witness in social media litigation.
- **Wider angle:** A business and regulation story on synthetic influencers: advertising-disclosure rules were written for humans, and no regulator currently requires a virtual model to be labelled as such. Fits a technology or advertising-industry outlet.

**Caveats:** Single brief laboratory-style exposure with self-reported outcomes; online convenience sample of women only; no follow-up, so nothing is known about cumulative effects; author has served as an expert witness for plaintiffs in litigation against social media companies (the work was not funded by that litigation).

### 3. A dose of negative social media posts about IUDs is enough to put people off them

**Journal:** *The Journal of Sex Research* | **Published:** 25 August 2026
**PMID:** 42641053 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00224499.2026.2718502
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a randomized experiment that pins down *how* contraception scare content works, though the sample is small and very young.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** 206 participants (mean age 18.9) were randomly assigned to view negative social media content about intrauterine devices or a control condition, then reported how acceptable they found the IUD, how much dread they felt about it, and how likely they thought a bad experience would be. Those exposed to the negative content rated IUDs significantly less acceptable, and the effect ran serially through two classic cognitive shortcuts: the availability heuristic (inflated perceived likelihood of a bad outcome) and then the affect heuristic (dread).

**Why it matters:** It moves the conversation about contraception misinformation from "people see scary videos" to a testable mechanism, which is what any counter-messaging intervention would need to target.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** A single scroll through negative IUD content shifted attitudes measurably — and the researchers traced the route it took through the mind, from "this seems common" to "this feels dreadful". Open on the flood of IUD-pain content that has reshaped a generation's view of a highly effective device. Call Anna R. Beacham or Sarit A. Golub at Hunter College / CUNY Graduate Center. Do not imply anything about actual contraceptive uptake — this measured attitudes only.
- **Wider angle:** A health-policy feature on why clinicians lost the credibility contest over IUD pain, and whether belated acknowledgement of inadequate pain control has left an opening that unmoderated content now fills. Fits a health or women's-health specialist outlet.

**Caveats:** Small N (206), single-center student-aged sample that may not generalize, self-reported attitudes rather than behavior, one-off exposure with no follow-up.

### 4. Heaviest phone users either zone out completely — or barely at all

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 23 July 2026)
**PMID:** 42492199 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107458
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — three months of real-time sampling plus device logs, and a U-shape that splits "phone addiction" into two different things; docked for industry involvement and unclear sample size.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using three months of ecological momentary assessment — real-time prompts about whether participants' minds were wandering — combined with smartphone screen on/off logs, researchers tested how problematic smartphone use relates to mental drift during phone use. The relationship was U-shaped rather than linear: both the least and most severely affected users reported high mind-wandering, with a trough in between. The authors read this as evidence for two distinct states, "ritualized" use (habitual, purposeless, device-focused) and non-ritualized use driven by specific content.

**Why it matters:** If problematic phone use is really two phenomena wearing one label, then studies treating it as a single dimension — and interventions treating everyone the same way — may be averaging over people who need opposite advice.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Two people can spend identical hours on their phones and be doing completely different things with their attention: one absent, one gripped. Open on the pocket-check reflex nobody can account for afterwards. Call Issaku Kawashima or Saori C. Tanaka at ATR in Kyoto. Be explicit that this is correlational and that participants rated their own mind-wandering.
- **Wider angle:** A design story for a technology outlet on "ritualized" interaction as a product feature — the unlock-scroll-lock loop that carries no content at all — and how habit-breaking apps target the wrong behavior when they count minutes rather than episodes.

**Caveats:** Observational; self-reported mind-wandering and self-reported problematic use; sample size not stated in the abstract; three authors are employed by KDDI Research Inc., which also provided funding for the research; population is likely a Japanese convenience sample and may not generalize.

### 5. Algorithms and chatbots are distorting what we learn from each other — in opposite directions

**Journal:** *Current Opinion in Psychology* | **Published:** 9 September 2026
**PMID:** 42715802 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.copsyc.2026.102412
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a synthesis rather than new data, but the framing is genuinely clarifying and unusually well suited to a 600-word explainer.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Synthesizing work across psychology, cultural evolution and computational social science, the authors argue that human social learning has two stages — "upstream" selection of what becomes visible, and "downstream" copying biases such as conformity and prestige — and that our downstream biases only work well when the visible sample reflects reality. Engagement-based algorithms, they argue, inflate the tails of the distribution by surfacing rare, extreme content, while generative AI collapses it toward the mode and erases the surrounding diversity. In both cases the optimization signal shapes not just visibility but apparent prestige, which they link to pluralistic ignorance and illusory consensus.

**Why it matters:** It offers a single mechanism that explains two complaints usually treated separately — that social media makes the world look more extreme than it is, and that AI makes everything sound the same — and suggests interventions should target what gets shown, not just how people react to it.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Two technologies, two opposite distortions of the same ancient learning system: the feed shows you the freaks, the chatbot shows you the average, and neither shows you the spread. Open on someone forming a belief about "what people think" entirely from a feed and a chatbot. Call William Brady at Northwestern's Kellogg School or Joshua Conrad Jackson at Chicago Booth. Frame clearly as a theoretical framework, not new evidence.
- **Wider angle:** A cultural-evolution feature for a general-interest science outlet on whether a generation that learns partly from language models will produce a measurable narrowing of ideas — and what an empirical test of that would even look like.

**Caveats:** Review and theory paper with no new data; interventions proposed are untested; mechanisms are inferred from a literature spanning very different methods.

### 6. Checking your ex's Instagram is about distress, not attachment style

**Journal:** *Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 2 July 2026)
**PMID:** 42388174 | **DOI:** 10.1177/21522715261466015
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — highly relatable and it dents a favourite explanation, but cross-sectional and retrospective.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** 232 people aged 18-27 (84 per cent women) who had broken up within the past year and still followed their ex on Instagram retrospectively reported their monitoring behavior during the first three weeks after the split, alongside measures of attachment, personality and breakup distress. Earlier Facebook-era work pointed to anxious attachment as the driver; here, attachment style showed no direct association once emotional distress was in the model. Emotional distress was the most consistent correlate, with extraversion the only personality trait directly linked to more monitoring.

**Why it matters:** It suggests post-breakup surveillance is a state — a coping response to how bad you currently feel — rather than a trait belonging to "anxiously attached people", which is a different target for anyone thinking about how to help.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The comforting story that ex-stalking is hardwired into your attachment style doesn't survive contact with the data. Open on the three weeks after a breakup, when the app is the only remaining window. Call Christiane Eichenberg at Sigmund Freud Private University Vienna. Avoid clinical prescriptions — this is a snapshot, not a trial.
- **Wider angle:** A design-and-ethics piece for a technology outlet on why platforms make it so easy to keep watching someone you have ended things with, and what "breakup mode" features would need to do to actually help.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and retrospective (recalling behavior up to a year later), observational design cannot establish causation, self-reported, overwhelmingly female sample of 18-27 year olds, Agreeableness dropped due to poor internal consistency.

### 7. Fear of missing out may be what turns disaster feeds into lasting distress

**Journal:** *The Journal of General Psychology* | **Published:** 10 September 2026
**PMID:** 42722509 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00221309.2026.2731892
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a genuinely longitudinal design on a timely question, but small, young and self-reported throughout.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** 220 participants aged 18-43 (mean 21.7; 78 per cent women) were surveyed at three-month intervals and analyzed with a cross-lagged panel model. Fear of missing out acted as a longitudinal mediator between social media addiction and vicarious trauma — symptoms of distress arising from witnessing wars and disasters through a screen rather than in person.

**Why it matters:** It puts a candidate mechanism behind doomscrolling: the compulsion to keep up may be what converts repeated remote exposure to atrocity into symptoms, which would make "fear of missing out" a more useful intervention target than screen time alone.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** You didn't go to the disaster — the disaster came to you, several hundred times, because you couldn't bear to be out of the loop. Open on someone who cannot stop refreshing coverage of a war they are thousands of miles from. Call Yavuz Altundağ at Abant İzzet Baysal University. Keep the causal language tight: cross-lagged does not mean proven causation.
- **Wider angle:** A journalism-and-ethics feature on graphic conflict footage in algorithmic feeds — how newsrooms and platforms handle content that reaches viewers with no warning and no context. Fits a media or press-freedom publication.

**Caveats:** Small N (220), self-reported measures throughout, only two usable time points three months apart, predominantly young female Turkish university sample that may not generalize, no clinical assessment of trauma symptoms.

### 8. Burnt-out teenagers scroll more — and that may raise their eating disorder risk

**Journal:** *Journal of Health Psychology* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 24 January 2026)
**PMID:** 41580900 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13591053251406971
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — decent adolescent sample and a novel starting point (school burnout), but cross-sectional modeling of a well-trodden pathway.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Structural equation modeling of 853 adolescents (mean age 15.72, 52 per cent female) tested whether school burnout is linked to eating disorder symptoms via excessive social media use, thin-ideal internalization and appearance comparison. All three indirect pathways were significant, with excessive use consistently the first link in the chain.

**Why it matters:** It ties academic pressure — usually studied for its effects on mood and sleep — to body image outcomes, suggesting that exhausted students may be scrolling their way into a specific kind of harm rather than simply escaping.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The story starts in the classroom, not on the phone: students worn down by school appear to turn to feeds that then feed them a body ideal. Open on an exam-season evening and the scroll that follows it. Call Miao Chao at Tianjin Normal University. Be clear this is a correlational model — the arrows are hypothesized, not demonstrated.
- **Wider angle:** An education feature on academic burnout as a mental health exposure in its own right, and whether schools ratcheting up workload are inadvertently increasing screen time and its downstream effects. Fits an education or parenting-focused outlet.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional — mediation direction is assumed, not tested over time; all measures self-reported; single-country adolescent sample; effects are statistical associations, not diagnoses.

### 9. Reddit posts reveal how people really take one of the most intense psychedelics

**Journal:** *Journal of Psychoactive Drugs* | **Published:** 7 September 2026
**PMID:** 42704210 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02791072.2026.2727030
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — rich, timely window onto real-world use of a drug now entering trials, but qualitative and unverifiable.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers thematically analyzed 120 purposively selected Reddit posts from 24 discussion threads about 5-MeO-DMT, generating 48 codes across seven categories. Users described individualized dosing and a tolerance profile distinct from other psychedelics, framed experiences as introspective and transformative, and placed heavy emphasis on preparation, a trusted sitter and integration afterwards. They also showed awareness of interactions with serotonergic medications — a real risk for people already on antidepressants.

**Why it matters:** Clinical trials of 5-MeO-DMT are advancing while a substantial unsupervised community has already worked out its own harm-reduction norms; documenting those norms is useful to researchers designing protocols and to clinicians whose patients are using it anyway.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Before the trials report, an online community has already written its own safety manual for one of the fastest and most overwhelming psychedelics — including the drug interaction that worries pharmacologists most. Open on a Reddit thread advising a poster on SSRIs. Call Damian Świeczkowski at the Medical University of Gdańsk. No efficacy claims: these are anonymous self-reports.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a drug-policy or health outlet on peer-generated harm reduction as a data source — what researchers can and cannot learn from anonymous forums, and whether regulators should be reading them.

**Caveats:** Qualitative analysis of 120 self-selected anonymous posts; no verification of substance, dose or identity; survivorship bias (bad outcomes are less likely to be posted); no control group or quantitative outcomes; not generalizable to any defined population.

### 10. Online victimization stands out in depressed teenagers who have attempted suicide

**Journal:** *Archives of Suicide Research* | **Published:** 9 September 2026
**PMID:** 42714373 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13811118.2026.2724425
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — clinically recruited three-group comparison is a strength, but cross-sectional, self-reported and modest in size.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers compared 76 adolescents with major depressive disorder and no suicide attempt history, 75 with MDD and a history of attempt, and 84 controls on problematic social media use, metacognitive beliefs and cybervictimization. All three were elevated in the attempt group. In a logistic regression within the depressed sample, cybervictimization and two metacognitive factors — negative meta-worry, and superstition/punishment/responsibility beliefs — were the variables most strongly associated with a history of attempt, while problematic social media use itself dropped out.

**Why it matters:** The interesting detail is what *didn't* survive the model: total problematic use mattered less than being targeted online and holding particular beliefs about one's own worrying — a more specific picture than "screen time and suicide risk".

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Among depressed teenagers, how much time they spend on social media looked less informative than whether they were being victimized on it, and what they believed about their own thoughts. Open on the gap between a parent counting hours and a clinician asking a different question. Contact the corresponding team via Pınar Ünal-Aydın / Orkun Aydın. Be firm: cross-sectional, so no causal or clinical claims.
- **Wider angle:** A mental-health services piece on metacognitive therapy — a treatment aimed at beliefs about worrying rather than the worries themselves — and where it currently sits in adolescent care. Fits a health or psychotherapy-focused outlet.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional — cannot establish causation or direction; suicide attempt history recorded retrospectively; all measures self-reported; group sizes modest and recruitment likely single-center; population may not generalize.

### 11. Fear of missing out is tied to brooding, not to healthy self-reflection

**Journal:** *Anxiety, Stress, & Coping* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 1 February 2026)
**PMID:** 41622812 | **DOI:** 10.1080/10615806.2026.2624434
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a neat, specific distinction with adequate N, but student sample, self-report and low news drama.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** 461 American university students completed web-based measures of fear of missing out, rumination, negative affect and depression in 2024. Confirmatory factor analyses showed that FOMO tracked significantly more closely with the *brooding* component of rumination — repetitive, self-critical dwelling — than with the *reflection* component, which is generally considered the more adaptive form. It was equally related to the underlying factors of negative affect and depression.

**Why it matters:** FOMO is often treated as one undifferentiated bad feeling; this suggests it sits specifically with the maladaptive half of rumination, which is a more precise target for anyone testing interventions.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Fear of missing out isn't thoughtful self-examination in disguise — it lines up with the corrosive kind of rumination instead. Open on the 1am scroll through other people's weekends. Call Jon D. Elhai at the University of Toledo, who has built much of the FOMO measurement literature. Keep it descriptive: cross-sectional survey, no causal claim.
- **Wider angle:** A psychology explainer for a general-interest outlet on why rumination splits into two kinds, why one predicts depression and the other doesn't, and how badly popular "self-reflection" advice conflates them.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional, observational; self-reported throughout; single-country university student sample that may not generalize; latent-factor distinctions are statistical and modest in practical terms.

### 12. Loneliness may push people toward impulse buying online, via self-esteem and FOMO

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** September 2026 (online 13 August 2026)
**PMID:** 42594777 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107599
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — very relatable subject matter and a coherent model, but cross-sectional survey data on a fairly intuitive chain.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** 311 Chinese adults aged 18-35, recruited via social media, student networks and commercial panels, completed measures of life satisfaction, loneliness, self-esteem, fear of missing out and impulsive buying in social commerce settings. Structural equation modeling supported a sequential chain: lower life satisfaction and higher loneliness predicted lower self-esteem, which predicted higher FOMO, which predicted more impulsive buying.

**Why it matters:** It frames the shoppable feed as something closer to a mood-repair device than a marketplace — which, if it holds up in longitudinal or behavioral data, matters for how consumer protection thinks about live-shopping and limited-time offers.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The "buy now" button may be catching people at a very specific psychological moment — lonely, low on self-worth, and afraid the deal will vanish. Open on a late-night live-shopping stream. Contact the corresponding author Sadiq at Shandong Xiehe University. Note the study measured self-reported buying tendency, not actual spending.
- **Wider angle:** A consumer-affairs investigation into social commerce mechanics — countdown timers, scarcity cues, livestream drops — and whether regulators treating these as ordinary advertising are missing a targeted psychological design. Fits a business or consumer-rights outlet.

**Caveats:** Small-to-moderate N (311), cross-sectional and observational — the mediation order is assumed, not tested over time; self-reported buying tendency rather than purchase records; convenience sample of young Chinese adults may not generalize.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Exposure shapes body image regardless of who is looking — or whether the body is real.** The new randomized test showing that virtual influencers hurt women's body image as much as human ones ("AI influencers dent women's body image just as much as real ones", PMID 42379075) lines up with last month's experience-sampling finding that feed content moves body image while body image barely steers viewing (PMID 42537570), and with the prospective nurses study (PMID 41968956). In both digests, moderators that ought to protect people — habitual appearance comparison, gender, health literacy — fail to do so. The new school-burnout chain ("Burnt-out teenagers scroll more", PMID 41580900) re-runs the thin-ideal internalization pathway from a new starting point.
- **Forums as an unlicensed clinical knowledge base.** The Reddit analysis of 5-MeO-DMT use norms (PMID 42704210) extends the thread opened by the r/AnorexiaRecovery "extreme hunger" study (PMID 41983301) and the edtwt/Ozempic netnography (PMID 42604953): patient and user communities documenting phenomena and safety practices ahead of, or in the absence of, professional guidance.
- **Problematic use is a poor explanatory variable.** The U-shaped mind-wandering result splitting phone use into "ritualized" and content-driven states (PMID 42492199) and the finding that cybervictimization and metacognitive beliefs, not problematic use, tracked suicide attempt history in depressed teens (PMID 42714373) continue the previous digest's relocation of the problem away from quantity: overestimated self-control beating content pull (PMID 42608804) and initiatory self-control as the transdiagnostic thread (PMID 42285011).
- **Engagement metrics do not mean what platforms assume.** The inverted-U for moral density across 1.6 million posts (PMID 42686956) sits well beside last month's "counter-valenced liking" package, in which a like can be an act of cruelty (PMID 42424914). Both say the signal recommender systems are trained on is being misread.
- **State beats trait.** Post-breakup Instagram monitoring tracked current emotional distress rather than attachment style (PMID 42388174), echoing the previous digest's finding that comparison orientation is cultivated by online group membership rather than fixed (PMID 42546360).

**Advances**

- The algorithm/AI social-learning synthesis (PMID 42715802) generalizes the "synthetic consensus" idea flagged in last month's four-stage radicalization framework (PMID 41869924) from extremism to ordinary belief formation, and adds a second, opposite distortion — generative AI collapsing the distribution toward the mode.
- The IUD experiment (PMID 42641053) advances the previous digest's descriptive work on how health messaging lands on platforms (Facebook school-meals campaign, PMID 42283650) by randomizing exposure and tracing a specific two-step cognitive route.

**Counterpoints**

- The moral-density result (PMID 42686956) complicates the virality assumptions underpinning both the radicalization framework (PMID 41869924) and the new social-learning synthesis (PMID 42715802): if algorithms inflate the extreme tail, audiences appear to disengage from the most saturated moral content rather than reward it without limit.

**New themes**

- **Fear of missing out becomes a hub construct**, absent from the previous digest and present in three unrelated new studies: as the longitudinal route from social media addiction to vicarious trauma (PMID 42722509), as a construct that maps onto brooding rather than adaptive self-reflection (PMID 41622812), and as the final step from loneliness to impulsive buying (PMID 42594777).
- **Social commerce** enters the batch for the first time (PMID 42594777), shifting the harm question from mood and body image toward money.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Across this batch, "how much you use it" keeps losing to "what state you are in while you use it." Fear of missing out is the mediator in three unrelated outcomes — vicarious trauma from disaster feeds (PMID 42722509), depressive brooding (PMID 41622812) and impulse buying in social commerce (PMID 42594777) — while total problematic use drops out of the model entirely once cybervictimization and metacognitive beliefs are entered in depressed adolescents (PMID 42714373), splits into two opposite attentional states in real-time sampling (PMID 42492199), and gives way to current emotional distress rather than attachment style in post-breakup monitoring (PMID 42388174).

**Why pitch this now:** Phone bans, screen-time limits and age gates are all built on hours-per-day as the exposure variable, at precisely the moment researchers are quietly abandoning it for specific psychological states that a clock cannot capture.

**Angle:** A piece for policy-literate general readers on what replaces screen time as the thing worth measuring — following FOMO, ritualized use and online victimization as candidate successor variables, and being honest that most of this evidence is still cross-sectional and self-reported. Frame it around the gap between what schools and legislators are counting and what researchers now think matters.

**Potential headlines:**
- Fear of missing out shows up in doomscrolling, rumination and late-night shopping alike
- Researchers are finding that hours on your phone is the least informative thing about it
- The phone rules in schools count minutes — the research has moved on to something harder to measure

**Potential outlets:**
- **New Scientist Mind** — a construct-led reappraisal of FOMO suits the section's appetite for psychology that reframes a familiar worry.
