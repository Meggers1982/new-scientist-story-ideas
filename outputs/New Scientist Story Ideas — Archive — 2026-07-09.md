### 1. Children born later in the school year are more likely to be taken to the doctor for behavioural concerns

**PMID:** 42398270 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.braindev.2026.104568
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Large human cohort with a clear counterintuitive societal hook (birth month driving medical referrals), loses one point each for observational design, self-reported outcomes, and single-country generalisability, but strong topic fit for NS Mind's cognition and child development audience.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers analysed a nationwide Japanese birth cohort (N=47,015) comparing children born in January — the relatively younger end of the school year — with those born in July. Children born in January were 18% more likely to have caregiver-reported medical consultations for developmental and behavioural concerns between ages 7 and 12, and were also more often described as 'restless' at age 9. The study suggests relative birth month, rather than genuine developmental disorder, may be driving some of these consultations, though whether this reflects true clinical need or misattribution of normal immaturity remains unclear.

**Why it matters:** Millions of children worldwide may be flagged for developmental concerns simply because they are younger than classmates, raising important questions about how school-entry age cutoffs shape medical decision-making and potential over-referral.

**Story angles:**

A nationwide Japanese study of over 47,000 children found that those born at the younger end of the school-year intake were significantly more likely to receive medical consultations for behavioural and developmental concerns. The finding suggests that relative immaturity — not genuine disorder — may be triggering referrals in many cases. With ADHD diagnoses rising globally, the idea that a child's birthday shapes their medical trajectory is both counterintuitive and unsettling. Why it fits: NS Mind readers — many of them parents — will find this immediately relevant to how society medicalises normal childhood variation, a theme the section covers well.

**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers conducted a prospective non-randomised clinical trial comparing the biopsychosocial impact of conventional IVF and in vitro maturation (IVM) — a minimal-stimulation alternative — in 149 women with Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (formerly PCOS; IVF: n=74, IVM: n=75). Anxiety, depression, and fertility-related quality of life were assessed at three time points: baseline, egg retrieval, and end of cycle, with propensity score matching used to balance groups. Counterintuitively, there were no significant differences between IVF and IVM in anxiety, depression, or overall quality of life. However, women undergoing IVF reported significantly more adverse physical symptoms, more post-retrieval pain, worse couple relationship quality, and a trend toward lower treatment tolerability.

**Why it matters:** With PCOS affecting roughly one in ten women of reproductive age, these findings suggest that IVM — which carries lower physical burden — does not compromise psychological wellbeing compared with conventional IVF, information that could meaningfully inform shared decision-making for patients navigating fertility treatment.

In vitro maturation — a gentler alternative to IVF that avoids intensive hormonal stimulation and eliminates the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome — is increasingly offered to women with PCOS. A new prospective study of 149 women found that those choosing IVM did experience significantly less pain, fewer physical side effects, and better couple relationship quality. But here's the unexpected part: their anxiety, depression, and overall quality of life scores were statistically indistinguishable from women who underwent conventional IVF. The finding challenges the assumption that lowering physical burden translates directly into psychological relief — and raises questions about what is really driving emotional distress during fertility treatment. **The study:** Researchers conducted a randomised crossover trial among 180 Chinese adults with hypertension, comparing a sleep sound analysis smartphone app (Snail Sleep) against the standard STOP-BANG questionnaire for detecting obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) at home. **Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers equipped 536 Chinese adolescents with accelerometers for seven consecutive days to objectively measure physical activity and sedentary behavior, while sleep was recorded via diary and self-harm assessed using the Self-Injury Behavior Scale. Compositional data analysis found that moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) was negatively associated with non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) scores, while sedentary behavior was positively associated. Isotemporal substitution modelling suggested that replacing 15 minutes of sedentary time with MVPA was associated with a 0.37-unit reduction in NSSI scores, with the largest effects seen up to a 20-minute substitution threshold. A sex-specific interaction was found for sleep proportion.

**Why it matters:** Non-suicidal self-injury is common among adolescents and difficult to treat; these findings suggest that relatively small increases in vigorous daily movement may be worth exploring as part of broader prevention strategies.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Just 20 minutes of vigorous exercise a day is linked to lower self-harm scores in teenagers" Replacing as little as 20 minutes of sitting with brisk activity is associated with meaningfully lower self-injury scores in adolescents — and the benefit doesn't grow much beyond that threshold. A study of 536 teenagers using wrist accelerometers found that swapping sedentary time for moderate-to-vigorous activity was associated with lower scores on a self-harm scale — but the effect plateaued after about 20 minutes, suggesting diminishing returns. The finding is counterintuitive because most public health messaging focuses on long bouts of exercise, yet here a modest daily reallocation appeared to matter most. With self-harm rates rising in adolescents globally, understanding cheap, scalable protective factors like movement patterns is urgently relevant. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are deeply interested in adolescent mental health, and a finding that links everyday movement habits to self-harm risk — with a specific, actionable time window — will resonate widely.
