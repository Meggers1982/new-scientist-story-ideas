### 1. Negative thinking traps the mind in a self-reinforcing loop — and rumination is the key driver

**Journal:** *Emotion* | **Published:** 2026 Aug 10
**PMID:** 42574125 | **DOI:** 10.1037/emo0001715
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Strong topic fit for NS Mind (emotion, cognition, mental health), decent sample with a rigorous EMA design, preregistered, counterintuitive asymmetry (dampening does not feed back into negative interpretation the way rumination does), and clear everyday-life relevance; minor deductions for observational design and self-report measures.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers from Tilburg University and KU Leuven followed 179 adults with varying levels of depression and social anxiety over 14 days using ecological momentary assessment (12,950 completed surveys), asking them to complete a novel mobile cognitive task measuring how rigidly they held negative interpretations of ambiguous situations, alongside self-reported rumination and positive-emotion dampening. Multilevel and dynamic structural equation analyses found that when people became more locked into negative interpretations momentarily, they were more likely to ruminate and dampen positive emotions shortly after — and rumination (but not dampening) in turn increased negative interpretation inflexibility. These reciprocal effects were not stronger in people with higher depression or anxiety.

**Why it matters:** Pinpointing negative interpretation inflexibility as both a cause and consequence of rumination in everyday life suggests it could be a specific target for just-in-time digital interventions aimed at breaking harmful thought cycles.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "Rumination keeps negative thinking alive — but suppressing joy does not" A 14-day daily-life study found that getting stuck in negative interpretations fuels rumination — but only rumination, not dampening of positive emotions, feeds the cycle back. Researchers pinged 179 adults up to 12 times a day for two weeks, slipping a cognitive test into the notifications to catch the exact moment someone got stuck interpreting an ambiguous situation negatively. They found a self-reinforcing loop: negative interpretive rigidity predicts rumination, and rumination predicts more negative interpretive rigidity. The surprise? Dampening positive emotions — a common companion to depression — did not meaningfully perpetuate the cycle. That asymmetry has real implications for therapy: tackling rumination may be more important than targeting positivity-suppression. Why it fits: NS Mind readers fascinated by the mechanics of mental health will be drawn to a study that tracked thought patterns moment-to-moment and found an asymmetry most therapists might not expect.
