### 5. Children and adults believe objects 'belong' wherever they were first found — even after being moved

**NS fit:** 8/10 — Large sample across two age groups, counterintuitive finding with a clear societal hook around belonging and ownership cognition, clean experimental design, and strong topic fit for NS Mind's social behaviour coverage.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** Researchers at the University of Waterloo ran five experiments with N=951 children aged 3–7 and N=1,238 adults in Canada and the United States, presenting scenarios in which objects were moved from their original location. Both children and adults consistently judged that objects 'belong' in the location where they were first encountered, a tendency the researchers call the 'first location heuristic.' The effect was weaker in adults when objects were moved by humans rather than natural forces, and children applied it to both indoor artifacts and outdoor natural objects.

**Why it matters:** This heuristic may underlie everyday intuitions about ownership, tidiness, and even broader moral or territorial judgements about where people and things 'should' be, with implications for understanding how both children and adults reason about belonging and displacement.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "People think objects 'belong' wherever they first appeared — not wherever they end up" Ask a child or an adult where a moved object belongs, and they'll point to where it started — not where it is now. A new study of nearly 2,200 people across Canada and the US finds that humans have a deep-seated instinct to treat an object's first location as its 'rightful' home, even when it has long since been moved. The tendency appears as early as age three and persists into adulthood. Researchers suggest this 'first location heuristic' could explain why we feel unsettled when things are out of place — and may connect to broader intuitions about origins and belonging. Why it fits: NS Mind readers fascinated by social cognition and hidden mental rules will recognise this heuristic at work in arguments about tidy spaces, returned possessions, and even territorial disputes.
