### 1. People feel less judged when comfort comes from a machine than from another human

**Journal:** *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* | **Published:** 3 September 2026
**PMID:** 42691276 | **DOI:** 10.1037/pspa0000494
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — six studies, mostly preregistered, N=3,341, with a result that flips the usual "AI can't really care" assumption; limited by minor stressors rather than serious distress.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Across a pilot and six studies (five preregistered; total N=3,341), participants who had just experienced a minor social sting — rudeness, a slight — received support either from a human or from an AI, in both simulated and real interactions. Because people expect AI to have little capacity for experience, they reported lower fear of negative evaluation after AI support, which improved well-being outcomes; separately, they judged the AI as exceeding their low expectations for empathy, which drove perceptions that the support worked.

**Why it matters:** It reframes the appeal of AI comfort as being about the absence of a judging mind rather than the presence of a caring one — which means the effect could evaporate as people's expectations of AI empathy rise.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on someone venting to a chatbot after a humiliating work meeting and feeling oddly better than after telling a friend. The surprise: the machine helps *because* we assume it can't really feel anything, so there is nobody there to think less of us. Worth calling Roshni Raveendhran at UVA's Darden School on whether the benefit is self-cancelling once chatbots are marketed as empathetic.
- **Wider angle:** A feature for a business or workplace outlet on the "expectation economy" of AI support tools — why HR chatbots and AI wellness apps may be exploiting a psychological loophole that vendors' own hype is closing.

**Caveats:** Focuses on minor aversive events, not clinical distress; largely self-reported well-being outcomes; short-term single-encounter designs with no follow-up.

### 2. ChatGPT spots eating disorders that human clinicians miss in higher-weight patients

**Journal:** *International Journal of Eating Disorders* | **Published:** September 2026 (epub 2 May 2026)
**PMID:** 42068161 | **DOI:** 10.1002/eat.70128
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — a clean benchmark against published human data showing AI lacked the weight bias clinicians displayed; limited by vignettes rather than real patients.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Researchers gave ChatGPT-5.4 two clinical vignettes — one restrictive eating disorder presentation with body weight varied across low, average and higher conditions, one binge-eating disorder — repeating each prompt 20 times, and compared responses with published benchmark data from clinicians and community samples. ChatGPT identified an eating disorder in 90–100% of cases regardless of the patient's weight and recommended specialist treatment every time; clinicians identified a disorder in 47%, 21% and 16% of the low-, average- and higher-weight versions and recommended specialist care 35%, 19% and 17% of the time. Gemini-3 and Claude-4.5 behaved similarly to ChatGPT.

**Why it matters:** The weight bias that stops eating disorders being diagnosed in people who are not visibly underweight is a documented clinical failure, and here the language models simply did not show it — a rare case of AI looking less prejudiced than the professionals.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the numbers: a chatbot flagged anorexia-type presentations in higher-weight patients five times more often than clinicians did. Open on the classic patient who is told she is "not thin enough" to be ill. Call Jake Linardon at Deakin University, and pair it with a clinician who can explain why vignette performance is not the same as recognizing a disorder in a real consultation.
- **Wider angle:** A specialist health-policy or medical-education piece on using AI outputs as a mirror for clinician bias — auditing where human decisions diverge from an unbiased-by-default baseline, and what that reveals about training.

**Caveats:** Vignette study, not real patients; human comparison data came from previously published samples rather than a head-to-head design; structured prompts may flatter AI performance; author is an associate editor of the publishing journal (recused from decisions).

### 3. The people most likely to accept an AI therapist are the ones who struggle most with human relationships

**Journal:** *Psychotherapy Research* | **Published:** September 2026 (epub 19 January 2026)
**PMID:** 41549774 | **DOI:** 10.1080/10503307.2026.2615388
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — large sample and a genuinely paradoxical pattern; weakened by cross-sectional self-report and hypothetical acceptance rather than real treatment.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Survey data from 1,612 potential patients and clinicians recruited via Prolific were clustered into three psychological profiles: "Avoidant-not trusting," "Secure-trusting-healthy," and "Young-anxious-ambivalent-symptomatic." The two more vulnerable clusters — higher symptoms, insecure attachment, lower epistemic trust — showed significantly greater acceptance of AI avatars and chatbots, while the securest, healthiest group most preferred human teletherapy. Having experience of therapy, as patient or provider, predicted lower AI acceptance overall.

**Why it matters:** AI therapy tools may reach exactly the people who avoid human help — while denying them the relational repair that psychotherapy is partly thought to work through.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is a double edge: the more your difficulties are about trusting other people, the more appealing a therapist that isn't a person becomes. Open on a young, anxiously attached user who has never booked a session but talks to a chatbot nightly. Call Vera Békés at McGill on whether AI is a bridge into care or a very comfortable dead end.
- **Wider angle:** A longer piece for a health or social-affairs outlet on triage: if commissioners deploy chatbots as the front door to mental health services, they are routing the hardest-to-reach patients away from the human contact that may be the active ingredient.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational — cannot establish causation; measures hypothetical acceptance, not uptake or outcomes; online convenience sample; self-reported symptoms.

### 4. Describing your mood in your own words predicts sick leave better than a questionnaire does

**Journal:** *Assessment* | **Published:** September 2026 (epub 20 September 2025)
**PMID:** 40974258 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10731911251364022
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 7/10 — preregistered models tested on a fresh prospective sample, and it challenges the primacy of the rating scale; small prospective N and a founder conflict of interest hold it back.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Participants described their depression and worry using four response formats — selecting words from lists, writing descriptive words, phrases, or free text — alongside standard rating scales. Language was converted into word embeddings and machine-learning models trained on a development sample (N=963), then preregistered before testing on a prospective sample (N=145). All formats predicted rating-scale scores accurately (r = 0.60–0.79), and free text correlated with self-reported sick leave and healthcare visits as strongly as or more strongly than the rating scales in nine of 12 comparisons.

**Why it matters:** If a few sentences in someone's own words carry as much clinically relevant signal as a validated symptom scale, the tick-box questionnaire that underpins most mental health measurement stops being the obvious default.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the reversal — the free-text box, usually the bit clinicians ignore, outperformed the numbered scale on real-world outcomes. Open on the familiar "on a scale of 0 to 3, how often have you felt hopeless?" and ask why we ever thought that was the best available instrument. Call Oscar Kjell at Lund University, and flag his start-up interest in the piece.
- **Wider angle:** A cautionary feature for a technology or privacy outlet: once written language becomes a validated mental health measure, everything you type — in an app, an HR survey, a customer service chat — becomes potential psychometric data.

**Caveats:** Prospective validation sample under 100 for some comparisons (N=145 overall); outcomes self-reported; two authors co-founded a company commercializing computational language assessment; validation against rating scales rather than clinical diagnosis.

### 5. Asking an AI to summarize medical notes destroys the information needed to predict violence

**Journal:** *The Canadian Journal of Psychiatry* | **Published:** 31 August 2026
**PMID:** 42671866 | **DOI:** 10.1177/07067437261479118
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a useful, well-quantified negative result about LLM summarization; the clinical framing is technical and the design retrospective.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using 277 documented inpatient violence events and 2,758 matched control encounters at Harborview Medical Center (2021–2023), researchers compared risk prediction from original clinical notes with prediction from AI-generated summaries of the same notes. Direct classification of raw notes performed best (F1 = 0.776, AUROC = 0.959); replacing them with general or clinician-guided summaries slashed performance (F1 = 0.465 and 0.510). Fine-tuned Llama-3.1-8B and MedGemma-27B underperformed the smaller purpose-built model, and zero-shot prompting was poor. Accuracy was lower for patients with a documented mental health flag.

**Why it matters:** Hospitals are enthusiastically inserting LLM summarization between clinicians and the record; this suggests summaries quietly strip out exactly the messy, repeated, contextual details that carry predictive signal.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is that even summaries explicitly steered toward clinical risk factors made things worse — a bigger model was not a better model. Open on the ward round where nobody reads the notes any more, just the AI digest. Call Nicholas Dobbins or Trevor Black at the University of Washington on what a summary loses: timing of agitation, intoxication, social context.
- **Wider angle:** A broader technology story on "summarization debt" across professions — law, insurance, journalism — where compressing documents with LLMs removes the low-salience details that decisions actually turn on.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, single-center; case-control design with matched controls; no prospective or external validation; ethically fraught outcome (violence risk labels) that the authors themselves warn against deploying.

### 6. Psychopathy predicts who will use generative AI to bully people, six months in advance

**Journal:** *Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking* | **Published:** September 2026 (epub 30 June 2026)
**PMID:** 42374878 | **DOI:** 10.1177/21522715261465484
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — first longitudinal look at an emerging harassment behavior with a decent sample; self-report and only two waves.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** A two-wave study of 1,019 participants measured Dark Tetrad traits (Machiavellianism, narcissism, psychopathy, sadism) and generative-AI-involved cyberbullying six months apart. Psychopathy and being male predicted AI-involved cyberbullying at Time 2 even after controlling for the same behavior at baseline. Sadism predicted it in the baseline model but dropped out once prior behavior was accounted for; Machiavellianism, narcissism and age showed no effect.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the people weaponizing image generators and chatbots against others are not a new population created by the technology but a familiar one handed a more powerful tool — which shapes where prevention should aim.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the trait sorting: sadism looked like the culprit until prior behavior was controlled, and callous psychopathy was what actually persisted. Open on a school or workplace dealing with an AI-generated smear campaign. Call Ruoyu Sun at Texas A&M on whether AI lowers the effort threshold enough to pull in people who would never have bullied by hand.
- **Wider angle:** An investigative or education-focused piece on how schools detect and adjudicate AI-generated harassment — deepfaked images, fabricated chat logs — when the evidentiary rules were written for text messages.

**Caveats:** Self-reported perpetration (likely under-reported); two waves only, six-month interval; observational — cannot establish causation; sample composition and recruitment platform may not generalize.

### 7. A home video of a toddler at play, plus machine learning, flags autism with 91% accuracy

**Journal:** *Autism* | **Published:** 4 September 2026
**PMID:** 42695515 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13623613261480732
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a culturally-specific tele-screening validation with strong headline metrics, but modest N and no comparison against clinical diagnosis.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Parents of 170 Chinese toddlers aged 16–30 months recorded ordinary parent-child play at home; trained coders scored the videos using the Social Communication Scale, and results were compared with ADOS-2 classifications of elevated versus low autism likelihood. Inter-rater reliability was good (ICC = 0.87). A random forest model using scale items plus demographic variables reached AUC 0.91 with 91% sensitivity and 91% specificity — better balanced than a simple total-score cutoff or logistic regression.

**Why it matters:** Most tele-assessment tools for autism were built and validated in Western settings; this is an attempt to show the approach transfers, and that machine learning adds real accuracy over a summed score.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is how little material is needed — a short phone video of a parent and child playing, no clinic, no specialist. Open on a family hundreds of kilometers from the nearest developmental pediatrician. Call Patrick C. M. Wong at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, and ask directly what a 91% specificity means for false positives at population scale.
- **Wider angle:** A global-health feature on the diagnostic bottleneck in autism — years-long waiting lists in high-income countries, near-total absence of services in many others — and whether remote video screening genuinely widens access or just relocates the queue.

**Caveats:** N=170, single research program; validated against ADOS-2 classification rather than confirmed clinical diagnosis; no independent replication sample; model performance likely optimistic without external validation; population-specific.

### 8. Giving students an AI tutor does nothing much — unless you teach them how to study with it

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** September 2026 (epub 14 August 2026)
**PMID:** 42600450 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107642
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a decent-sized controlled comparison isolating the pedagogy rather than the tool; quasi-experimental with class-level assignment and a narrow outcome.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** In a seven-week quasi-experiment, 266 industrial engineering undergraduates at a Chinese university were assigned at class level either to a structured self-regulated learning program — goal setting before AI use, real-time self-monitoring, reflective evaluation afterwards — or to using the same AI tools autonomously. The scaffolded group significantly outperformed the control group on technical English vocabulary tests and reported a meaningful increase in enjoyment, while the autonomous-AI group showed no enjoyment change. The gap was largest among students who started out enjoying the subject least.

**Why it matters:** The active ingredient here is the routine wrapped around the AI, not the AI itself — which is awkward for the assumption that handing learners a chatbot is an intervention.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the control group: students given free access to AI learned no more and enjoyed it no more than before. Open on the undergraduate who has "studied" for three hours by asking a chatbot to explain things. Call Guanyao Xu at Xihua University on whether the scaffolding transfers beyond vocabulary to reasoning-heavy tasks.
- **Wider angle:** An education-policy feature for a general-interest or specialist education outlet on the AI procurement rush — schools and universities buying tools when the evidence points to teaching metacognition instead.

**Caveats:** Quasi-experimental with intact classes rather than individual randomization; single institution and single discipline; enjoyment self-reported; seven weeks with no follow-up; may not generalize beyond vocabulary learning.

### 9. ChatGPT's abortion advice is usually acceptable and almost never complete

**Journal:** *Culture, Health & Sexuality* | **Published:** September 2026 (epub 23 June 2025)
**PMID:** 40550012 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13691058.2025.2517289
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a striking accuracy-versus-completeness gap in a high-stakes information domain; small, qualitative, and tested on an older model.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Researchers put ten fact-based and ten clinical-scenario abortion questions to ChatGPT-3.5 and had three complex family planning physicians grade each response as acceptable or unacceptable and complete or incomplete, against ACOG and Society of Family Planning guidance and PubMed-indexed evidence. Some 65% of responses were rated acceptable, but only 8% were rated complete. Factual questions fared better than clinical scenarios.

**Why it matters:** The failure mode is not obvious falsehood but confident partial answers — the hardest kind for a frightened person navigating a shifting legal landscape to detect.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The number to lead on is 8%: nearly two-thirds of answers were acceptable, but almost none were complete. Open on someone in a state with restrictive laws typing a question into a chatbot at 2am because there is no one else to ask. Call Melody Xu at Jersey Shore University Medical Center, and note this tested ChatGPT-3.5 — worth asking whether newer models close the completeness gap.
- **Wider angle:** A reported piece for a health or politics outlet on AI as the de facto reproductive health advice service in jurisdictions where clinical information has been legally restricted, and who bears liability for an incomplete answer.

**Caveats:** Only 20 questions; three graders at a small number of centers; tested an outdated model version; qualitative judgment-based outcomes; no patient involvement.

### 10. Chatbots are becoming part of how teenagers form an identity — and nobody is tracking it

**Journal:** *Perspectives on Psychological Science* | **Published:** 28 August 2026
**PMID:** 42664282 | **DOI:** 10.1177/17456916261460771
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a strong, well-argued developmental framing of a live public worry, but it is a synthesis with no new data.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** An interdisciplinary synthesis argues that adolescence — with its sensitive-period work on social belonging, emotion regulation, autonomy and identity — is the life stage where generative AI chatbots matter most. The authors identify plausible benefits (low-stakes social rehearsal, scaffolded emotion regulation, safe identity exploration) alongside specific risks: displaced developmental experiences, eroded distress tolerance, distorted expectations of human relationships, and compromised identity and purpose formation. They call for longitudinal, use-case-specific research and youth-involved study design.

**Why it matters:** It shifts the adolescent-AI debate from screen time and content moderation to developmental tasks — a framework that generates testable predictions rather than moral panic.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The reframe is the story: the question is not whether chatbots expose teenagers to bad content but whether they quietly do the developmental work — the awkward conversation, the tolerated rejection — that adolescents need to do themselves. Open on a 15-year-old rehearsing a breakup message with an AI. Call Anna Maheux at UNC Chapel Hill on what the first good longitudinal study would need to measure.
- **Wider angle:** A policy and regulation feature for a general-interest outlet on why age-verification and content filters are the wrong instruments if the harm is developmental displacement, and what a design standard grounded in developmental science would look like.

**Caveats:** Review and theoretical agenda-setting, not primary research; no empirical test of the proposed mechanisms; longitudinal evidence the authors call for does not yet exist.

### 11. AI helps you generate more ideas — and makes everyone's ideas look the same

**Journal:** *Current Opinion in Psychology* | **Published:** 7 September 2026
**PMID:** 42705114 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.copsyc.2026.102425
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — homogenization is the counterintuitive cost readers can feel in their own work; review-level evidence only.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** A review of experimental work on human-AI creative collaboration across writing, design and product ideation separates outcome effects from process effects. Generative AI reliably increases the number of ideas, can raise their rated quality, speeds up generation and lowers cognitive load — but is also associated with idea homogenization across people, reduced feelings of authenticity and ownership, less independent creative effort, and weaker team collaboration. Four factors determine which way it goes: when in the process AI enters, how the interaction is designed, what the task is, and how expert the user is.

**Why it matters:** Individual creative output goes up while collective diversity goes down — an aggregation effect that no single user experiences and that most productivity studies are not designed to detect.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead on the paradox: every writer gets better ideas, and the pool of ideas gets narrower. Open on two strangers using the same tool for the same brief and producing near-identical work. Call Sara Caprioli at TUM School of Management on the timing finding — whether bringing AI in after your own first draft protects originality.
- **Wider angle:** A media and culture feature on cultural convergence: measuring homogenization across advertising, pop songwriting or academic prose as AI assistance saturates creative industries, for an arts or business outlet.

**Caveats:** Narrative review, not new data or meta-analysis; underlying studies are mostly short lab tasks; no evidence yet on long-term effects on creative skill development.

### 12. Telling epilepsy patients about the risk of sudden death does not appear to harm them

**Journal:** *Epilepsy & Behavior* | **Published:** 31 August 2026
**PMID:** 42673770 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.yebeh.2026.111276
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 5/10 — directly contradicts the clinical rationale for silence, using an unusually large trawl of patient voices; the data are unsolicited social media posts analyzed with proprietary tools.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using AI and natural language processing to analyze online epilepsy communities from September 2020 to November 2024, researchers screened posts from 23,584 authors and closely examined 1,381 posts by 789 people who explicitly mentioned Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy. Most had learned about SUDEP through their own online searching, often years after diagnosis, and described fear, shock and — where clinicians had never raised it — betrayal and mistrust. Knowing about SUDEP was associated with better medication adherence, lifestyle changes and use of seizure alarms and anti-suffocation pillows, with no sign of lasting psychological harm.

**Why it matters:** Clinicians commonly withhold SUDEP information to avoid frightening patients; the patients' own accounts suggest the withholding is what damages trust, while the knowledge drives protective behavior.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is the reversal of protective silence: people found out anyway, alone, at 3am, from a search engine — and wished a doctor had told them at diagnosis. Open on that moment of discovery. Call Christian Brandt at the Bethel Epilepsy Center, and be explicit that this reads patients' public words rather than measuring anxiety.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-forward feature for a health or technology outlet on "social media listening" as pharmaceutical and clinical research infrastructure — who consents, who profits, and what happens when patient forum posts become industry evidence.

**Caveats:** Retrospective and observational; unsolicited online posts, so self-selected and skewed toward the engaged and distressed; psychological harm inferred from text, not measured; three authors are employed by Semalytix GmbH, which sells the social-listening tools used, and the analysis relies on their proprietary platforms.

### 13. Machine learning can guess who will still be depressed after eating disorder treatment — but not who will fail to improve

**Journal:** *International Journal of Eating Disorders* | **Published:** September 2026 (epub 12 May 2026)
**PMID:** 42117565 | **DOI:** 10.1002/eat.70129
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a preregistered, transparently reported partial failure in a field prone to overclaiming; the story is technical and the sample is single-provider.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Routine electronic health record data from 1,412 people in inpatient or day-hospital eating disorder treatment were used to build preregistered machine-learning models (elastic net and extreme gradient boosting) predicting two outcomes at admission. Nonimprovement of depressive symptoms was predicted poorly (AUC 0.64–0.65); residual depression at discharge was predicted adequately (AUC 0.73–0.77). Important predictors were phobic anxiety, resilience, life satisfaction and baseline depression — and a substantial share of patients left treatment still notably depressed.

**Why it matters:** It draws a useful line between predicting a state (who will still be unwell) and predicting a change (who will not respond) — the second is what personalized care actually needs, and it is the one the models could not do.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the asymmetry that keeps tripping up clinical AI: models look impressive when they are largely restating how ill someone already is, and collapse when asked who will change. Open on a patient discharged as "recovered" from an eating disorder who is still profoundly depressed. Call Nicolas Leenaerts at KU Leuven on why preregistering prediction models matters.
- **Wider angle:** A critical feature for a specialist medical or data-science outlet on prediction-model inflation in psychiatry: how many published AUCs are essentially baseline severity in disguise, and what reporting standards would expose that.

**Caveats:** Retrospective analysis of routinely collected records from a single clinic group; no external validation; observational design; moderate performance by the authors' own account; self-reported symptom measures.

### 14. When AI helps you choose dinner, the values being optimized may not be yours

**Journal:** *Appetite* | **Published:** 7 September 2026
**PMID:** 42705481 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.appet.2026.108784
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a sharp conceptual reframing with named mechanisms and a domain everyone lives in daily; no new data.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** This review argues that research on AI and food has been stuck on one question — does AI give better recommendations? — while AI has moved along a continuum from advisor, to decision partner that helps construct the choice, to ambient influence shaping what looks desirable before deliberation starts. Drawing on consumer psychology, automation research and cognitive science, the authors specify four process mechanisms: preference offloading, responsibility reallocation, confidence calibration, and food-decision skill erosion. Because eating decisions are frequent and habitual, they argue these effects compound.

**Why it matters:** It moves the debate about AI and diet from recommendation accuracy to loss of agency — and points out that food-decision skills, like navigation skills, may atrophy with delegation.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is skill erosion: we already know GPS blunted our sense of direction, so what happens when an assistant decides what you eat several times a day for a decade? Open on a person who no longer knows what they feel like eating without asking. Call Isaac Cheah at Curtin University on which of the four mechanisms is most testable first.
- **Wider angle:** A consumer-affairs investigation for a food or business outlet into who pays for placement in AI meal recommendations — the ambient-influence end of the continuum, where retailers and delivery platforms set the option set before a "choice" is made.

**Caveats:** Conceptual review and research agenda; the authors note key boundary conditions are untested in food contexts; no empirical results.

### 15. Negotiating through a screen — or an AI — makes it easier not to notice you are behaving badly

**Journal:** *Current Opinion in Psychology* | **Published:** 25 August 2026
**PMID:** 42697057 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.copsyc.2026.102415
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — a coherent psychological account of why AI-mediated deals may go crooked, but explicitly acknowledges the evidence base is thin.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** The authors review evidence on "ethical fading" — the process by which situational features stop people recognizing the moral dimension of what they are doing — and apply it to technology-mediated negotiation. They propose that email, video conferencing and AI each amplify three cognitive processes: psychological distance from the counterpart, cognitive offloading of the reasoning to the tool, and displaced accountability for the outcome. Together, these are argued to make misrepresentation and hardball tactics feel like technical choices rather than ethical ones.

**Why it matters:** If AI delegation reliably dulls moral awareness rather than just improving analysis, then "the model recommended it" becomes a general-purpose excuse across hiring, pricing and pay negotiations.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with displaced accountability — the person who would never lie to a landlord's face but lets an AI agent do the haggling and never asks what it said. Open on an AI negotiation agent closing a deal on someone's behalf. Call McKenzie Rees at Brigham Young University on the experiments needed to show ethical fading actually happens with AI counterparts, not just email.
- **Wider angle:** A legal and governance feature for a business or law-focused outlet on accountability gaps as AI agents start transacting autonomously: who is liable when the misrepresentation was made by a model acting on your instructions?

**Caveats:** Conceptual review with no new data; the authors state that research linking technology to unethical negotiating behavior remains limited; mechanisms are proposed rather than demonstrated for AI specifically.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Taken together, this batch suggests that AI's most measurable psychological effects are subtractions — things removed from a process — rather than capabilities added. Removal is sometimes the benefit: people felt better after AI comfort precisely because there was no judging mind present (PMID 42691276), and chatbots flagged eating disorders in higher-weight patients because they lacked the weight bias clinicians showed (PMID 42068161). But the same logic runs the other way in the rest of the batch: LLM summaries stripped the messy contextual detail that carried predictive signal in clinical notes (PMID 42671866), AI-assisted ideation raised individual output while narrowing the collective pool of ideas (PMID 42705114), and three reviews model AI delegation as the loss of food-decision skill (PMID 42705481), moral awareness in negotiation (PMID 42697057), and the awkward developmental experiences adolescents need to have themselves (PMID 42664282). The education study is the clean test case: students handed AI tools with nothing removed and nothing added learned no more than before, while the ones taught a monitoring routine did (PMID 42600450).

**Why pitch this now:** Coverage of AI in health, education and creative work is still organized around whether the output is accurate or the productivity gain is real, while this cluster of new work is measuring what disappears from the process — a cost that no individual user experiences and that most benchmark and productivity studies are not designed to detect.

**Angle:** Frame it as an accounting problem: AI's ledger has a subtraction column that almost nobody is auditing, and the same mechanism — taking a human out of the loop — explains both its best results and its worst. Written for a general science-literate audience that already uses these tools daily at work and is being sold them for their children's schooling and their own health care.

**Potential headlines:**
- The evidence is stacking up that AI's biggest effects are things it takes away
- The comfort of a chatbot works because nobody is really there
- Detail, diversity, judgment: what you lose when you let a model do the compressing
- The skills you hand to an AI may not be waiting when you come back
