### Study 1: Professionals on TikTok push repressed-memory claims more than laypeople do
**PMID:** 42847121 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Sample (394 videos), hashtags, the three top-endorsed beliefs and their percentages (24.1/9.1/7.9 vs. 24.11/9.14/7.87), the professional-vs-layperson difference, journal, date and the Perez/Arcadia attribution all check out against the abstract. The Caveats correctly flag that this is a content analysis and that professional status was self-presented.

**Issue A. NS fit score of 7 on descriptive, uncontrolled data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — vivid, timely and genuinely surprising about who is spreading memory myths, though it is a content analysis rather than a study of what viewers come to believe."
- **Abstract says:** A descriptive coding study of 394 videos; creator status is "identified as laypeople or professionals" with no verification described; the conclusion is only that findings "confirm concerns about social media platforms serving as a possible source of misinformation about memory."
- **Problem:** The score rests entirely on an uncontrolled content analysis in which the key grouping variable (professional vs. layperson) is self-presented and unverified, and in which no viewer outcome is measured. The fit note itself concedes the main limitation, which argues against a 7.
- **Suggested fix:** "**NS fit:** 6/10 — vivid and timely, and the professional/layperson split is a genuine surprise, but it is an uncontrolled content analysis with self-presented creator credentials and no measure of what viewers take away."

### Study 2: Letting your mind wander after studying does not seem to harm what you remember
**PMID:** 42771053 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N (197), preregistration, online format, 1-back/3-back for 15 min, thought-probe categories, immediate and delayed testing, the null on memory and on state–retention correlations, and the extra non-preregistered perceptual condition are all accurately reported. Blondé/Jagiellonian is correct. The headline is appropriately hedged ("does not seem to").

**Issue A. "Overturns prior research" novelty tag** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** "Previous examinations of the relationship between mind wandering (MW) during post-encoding periods and subsequent memory consolidation has led to mixed results… These findings add to a growing body of mixed evidence."
- **Problem:** The authors position the result as another entry in an already-mixed literature, not as an overturning of an established effect. The body text ("Not a definitive refutation, but a solid data point") is accurate, so the tag is the outlier.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** Well-powered null in a contested literature"

**Issue B. Magnitude of the mind-wandering difference** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the easy task produced far more mind wandering"
- **Abstract says:** "The 1-back condition elicited more MW, particularly involving mental time travel, while the 3-back condition increased on-task focus."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a direction, not an effect size; "far more" adds a magnitude the abstract does not support.
- **Suggested fix:** "the easy task produced more mind wandering, particularly mental time travel, while the demanding task increased on-task focus"

*Note (not counted as an issue):* the entry contains a stray duplicated field — "**The media check:** —" above "**Media check:**" — which should be deleted before circulation.

### Study 3: The promise of a reward sharpens memory in older adults just as much as in the young
**PMID:** 42849577 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Group sizes (29 aged 18–35; 30 aged 56–73), the two tasks, the skin conductance measure, the equivalence of reward effects across age groups, money > social reward, and the mediation of the speed gain by anticipatory SCR in older adults are all accurate. Yeung/Education University of Hong Kong is correct.

**Issue A. Headline generalizes a monetary-only memory effect to "a reward"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The promise of a reward sharpens memory in older adults just as much as in the young"
- **Abstract says:** "Regarding memory, both age groups showed improved visual recognition and a greater ability to mentally re-experience past events when monetary reward was available."
- **Problem:** Speed and arousal responded to both monetary and social reward; the memory benefit is reported only for monetary reward. The body text gets this right ("on monetarily rewarded trials"), but the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "The promise of money sharpens memory in older adults just as much as in the young"

**Issue B. "Exactly like" overstates an absence of an age difference** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the data showing a 70-year-old's memory responds to a small cash incentive exactly like a 20-year-old's"
- **Abstract says:** "reward enhances older adults' processing speed and episodic memory similarly to those of younger adults, despite potentially different contributions of sympathetic arousal."
- **Problem:** "Exactly like" claims equivalence from a non-significant age interaction in a 59-person study, and the abstract explicitly notes the underlying physiology may differ. The reward amount ("small cash") is also not given in the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "the data showing older adults' memory responds to a monetary incentive about as much as younger adults' does — though the authors note the underlying arousal contributions may differ"

**Issue C. Wider angle asserts real-world efficacy** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "why adherence programs for older adults so rarely use the motivational machinery that demonstrably still works"
- **Abstract says:** Findings are from a laboratory incentive delay task and visual recognition task in 59 healthy volunteers; the stated contribution is to "deepen our understanding of the three-way relationship among reward, psychophysiological functioning, and aging."
- **Problem:** "Demonstrably still works" projects a small single-session lab effect onto adherence programs and dementia-prevention trials. That is an overgeneralization beyond the study population and setting.
- **Suggested fix:** "why adherence programs for older adults rarely exploit incentives, when lab work like this suggests the motivational machinery is still responsive in later life"

**Issue D. "Single-centre" claim not supported by the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Small N (59 total across two groups); single-center"
- **Abstract says:** Author affiliations span the University of Sydney, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University and The Education University of Hong Kong; no recruitment or testing site is stated.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only — the number of testing sites is not reported.
- **Suggested fix:** "Small N (59 total across two groups); testing site(s) not specified in the abstract"

### Study 4: Making people feel lonely in the lab does not make them read hostility into others
**PMID:** 42831817 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N (142), age range (18–29), undergraduate sample, random assignment to the three conditions, the successful manipulation checks, the baseline loneliness–hostile attribution association, the null for induced change and the absence of an interaction are all reported accurately. Norman/University of Chicago is correct.

**Issue A. "Overturns prior research" novelty tag** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** "While loneliness has been associated with increased hostile attribution biases, empirical studies have yet to assess whether loneliness directly causes increased hostile attributions."
- **Problem:** There was no prior causal finding to overturn — this is the first causal test of an assumption, and the correlational literature (replicated here at baseline) is unaffected.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** First causal test of a widely assumed mechanism — and it comes up null"

**Issue B. "Stable trait-level feature" goes beyond a null result** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "This suggests the cognitive bias is a stable trait-level feature, not something that rides on how lonely you feel this afternoon"
- **Abstract says:** "These findings have implications for the design of loneliness interventions, suggesting that targeting momentary feelings of loneliness may have limited effects on the hostile attribution biases associated with feelings of loneliness."
- **Problem:** A single-session null on an induced state cannot establish that the bias is trait-stable; that would require longitudinal or test–retest data the study does not report. The defensible claim is narrower: shifting momentary loneliness did not shift the bias.
- **Suggested fix:** "This suggests the bias does not track how lonely you feel this afternoon — the authors conclude that interventions aimed at momentary feelings may have limited effect on it"

**Issue C. "Vignette-based measure" not described in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "vignette-based measure of hostile attribution rather than real social behavior"
- **Abstract says:** Only that participants' "tendency to assume hostile intent behind others' ambiguous actions" was assessed; the instrument format is not specified.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "hostile attribution measured by self-report ratings of ambiguous scenarios rather than real social behaviour (instrument not specified in the abstract)"

### Study 5: Words about food resist deliberate forgetting — words about death do not
**PMID:** 42844925 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Four experiments, item-method directed forgetting, Spanish words rated for intrinsic survival value across the obtaining-food and avoiding-death domains, robust overall directed forgetting, and the free-recall and recognition patterns are all reported accurately in the body text. Alonso/Universidad de La Laguna is correct.

**Issue A. Headline contradicts the recognition result** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Words about food resist deliberate forgetting — words about death do not"
- **Abstract says:** "In the avoiding death domain, high survival-value words showed greater recognition following forget instructions, reflected in higher recognition sensitivity."
- **Problem:** Death-related words *did* resist forgetting — just on the recognition test rather than in free recall. The headline states the opposite as a flat fact, and a reader who stops at the headline takes away a finding the study does not report. The body text handles the interaction correctly, which makes the headline the sole problem.
- **Suggested fix:** "Food words slip past 'forget' in recall, death words in recognition — survival value resists deliberate forgetting, but not in one way"

**Issue B. "Student-heavy" sample description not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Student-heavy Spanish-language word lists"
- **Abstract says:** Nothing about who the participants were; it reports only that "Spanish words rated for intrinsic survival value were selected from two adaptive domains."
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only — participant characteristics are not described, and the phrasing conflates the sample with the stimuli.
- **Suggested fix:** "Spanish-language word lists and a Spanish laboratory sample (participant details not given in the abstract), which may not generalise across languages or cultures"

### Study 6: Parents' and teachers' verdicts on a child's self-control only partly match what tests show
**PMID:** 42166276 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N (244), age (5–6), Hong Kong kindergartens, 97.5% Chinese, the four direct tasks, the Childhood Executive Functioning Inventory, the teacher-report pattern across all four tasks and the narrower parent-report pattern are all accurate. Liu/University of Hong Kong is correct.

**Issue A. Moderation by parental education is broader in the digest than in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "parental education moderated the fit: reports from more educated parents lined up with children's task performance, whereas reports from less educated parents correlated with nothing"
- **Abstract says:** "Parental education moderated the association between parent reports and direct assessment **for working memory**. Among more educated parents, parent-reported working memory difficulties were negatively associated with all EF tasks, and parent-reported inhibition difficulties were negatively associated with the inhibitory control task."
- **Problem:** The formal moderation is reported for the working memory scale only, and among more-educated parents the inhibition reports tracked just one task, not performance generally. The second half of the sentence (less-educated parents' reports associated with nothing) is accurate.
- **Suggested fix:** "parental education moderated the fit for working memory: among more educated parents, reported working memory difficulties tracked all four tasks (and reported inhibition difficulties tracked inhibitory control), whereas reports from less educated parents were associated with no task at all"

**Issue B. Publication date sits outside the stated coverage window** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Journal:** *Psychological Assessment* | **Published:** October 2026"
- **Abstract says:** "Psychol Assess. 2026 Oct;38(10):744-757. doi: 10.1037/pas0001480. **Epub 2026 May 21.**"
- **Problem:** The paper first appeared online on 21 May 2026, nearly five months before the 9 October run date and well outside the "Last 30 days" coverage window advertised at the top of the digest. An editor needs to know this is an issue-date pick-up, not new research.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Published:** October 2026 issue (online ahead of print 21 May 2026 — note this falls outside the 30-day window)"

### Study 7: A deep thalamic region only starts telling danger from safety a week after the lesson
**PMID:** 42843708 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The Day 1 / 7–10 day design, n = 28 for the delayed recall fMRI sample, the absence of CS+/CS− discrimination in PVT during acquisition despite differential EDA and expectancy ratings, the discrimination during delayed recall, and the independent n = 56 sample showing no discrimination during acquisition or immediate extinction are all accurately reported. Stout/UC San Diego and VA San Diego is correct, and the Caveats correctly note the histology-informed, "PVT-centered" ROI and the authors' own "preliminary" framing.

**Issue A. "Storage circuitry is not formation circuitry" overreads the data** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "it suggests the circuitry that stores a fear long-term is not the circuitry that forms it"
- **Abstract says:** "a human PVT-centered dorsal midline thalamic region is sensitive to prior threat reinforcement during delayed recall, **while also showing engagement during acute threat-learning contexts**" — and in the n = 56 sample "overall PVT activation was greater during acquisition than extinction, regardless of CS type."
- **Problem:** The PVT *was* active during acquisition; what it failed to do was discriminate CS+ from CS−. The digest converts a difference in cue selectivity over time into a claim about anatomically separate storage and formation systems, which the abstract explicitly complicates.
- **Suggested fix:** "it suggests this region is engaged during learning but only starts separating threat from safety once the memory is old — hinting that cue-selective retrieval recruits circuitry differently from initial encoding"

**Issue B. Clinical relevance claim not supported by the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Relevant to why exposure therapy gains can fade."
- **Abstract says:** The findings "support future investigation of PVT-centered thalamic circuitry in the persistence, retrieval, and updating of threat memories, as well as in salience-related processes." No clinical population was studied and relapse after therapy is not discussed.
- **Problem:** The link to exposure-therapy relapse is the writer's extrapolation from a healthy-volunteer imaging study; it should be marked as speculation.
- **Suggested fix:** "The authors flag it as a target for future work on how threat memories persist and are updated — a question that bears on why exposure therapy gains sometimes fade, though this study did not test that."

**Issue C. "First human imaging evidence" not stated as a priority claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this is the first human imaging evidence pointing the same way"
- **Abstract says:** "whether the human PVT shows cue-related discriminability during delayed recall remains unclear… These findings provide preliminary evidence…"
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only — the authors describe a gap, not a priority claim. Priority assertions are easy to get wrong and invite correction.
- **Suggested fix:** "the authors say it is unclear whether the human PVT behaves this way, and this is the first imaging evidence that it does"

### Study 8: Angry and sad faces compete for different mental resources when you try to hold them in mind
**PMID:** 42845027 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The Experiment 1 design (one vs. three faces; happy/angry/sad; concurrent auditory monitoring at low vs. high load; neutral probe identity match), both dissociated effects in the correct direction, and the Experiment 2 controls (featural similarity ruled out; attentional costs specific to active maintenance) are reported accurately. Garcia/University of São Paulo is correct, and the Caveats rightly note that sample size is absent from the abstract.

**Issue A. Participant and stimulus details not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "undergraduate laboratory paradigm with static posed faces"
- **Abstract says:** Only "participants performed a face working memory (WM) task"; neither participant status nor the face stimulus set is described.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only — the undergraduate characterization and the "static posed faces" description are assumptions about an unreported method.
- **Suggested fix:** "laboratory face-memory paradigm (participant characteristics and stimulus set not reported in the abstract)"

**Editor's note on priorities:** nothing here reverses a direction of effect or would mislead a reader into acting on bad information, so no Major flags. The four Moderate items are the ones to fix before any of these go to a commissioning editor: the Study 5 headline (contradicts the study's own recognition finding), the Study 7 "storage vs. formation circuitry" line, the Study 4 "trait-level feature" leap from a null, and the Study 3 wider angle's "demonstrably still works."
