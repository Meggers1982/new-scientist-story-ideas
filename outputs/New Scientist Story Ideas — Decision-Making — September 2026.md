### 1. People reject the utilitarian answer when asked to settle a group's disagreements

**Journal:** *Cognition* | **Published:** September 18, 2026
**PMID:** 42759227 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cognition.2026.106694
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 8/10 — a direct empirical challenge to the default assumption behind most welfare economics and AI alignment work, with a ready-made LLM hook.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** When a group has to pick one option and members want different things, the standard fix is to add up everyone's utility and take the biggest total. Moore and colleagues systematically sampled a wide range of social decision scenarios and pitted three aggregation algorithms — utilitarian, egalitarian and contractualist (what rational parties would have agreed to) — against participants' actual intuitions. People strongly preferred the contractualist solutions, which beat egalitarian ones across parameter settings and beat the utilitarian default outright. Large language models tested on the same scenarios did not match human preferences.

**Why it matters:** The "sum the utilities" rule is baked into cost-benefit analysis, public health triage and the reward functions being written for AI systems — and it appears not to be what humans actually think is fair.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the mismatch: the algorithm we hand to machines to make fair decisions on our behalf is the one humans like least. Open on a mundane group decision — four housemates choosing a holiday, one of whom hates beaches — and show how the three algorithms diverge. Call Sydney Levine (NYU), who works on moral cognition and machine alignment, and ask what a contractualist AI would actually look like.
- **Wider angle:** A features piece for a policy or economics outlet on the quiet dominance of utilitarian arithmetic in public decision-making — QALYs, transport appraisals, disaster triage — and what would change if institutions aggregated preferences the way citizens apparently want them to.

**Caveats:** Vignette-based judgments rather than consequential decisions; participant demographics and sample sizes not specified in the abstract; LLM comparison is a snapshot of rapidly changing models.

### 2. Saying "um" doesn't dent your reputation — but it does cost you the conversation

**Journal:** *Cognition* | **Published:** September 3, 2026
**PMID:** 42691919 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cognition.2026.106697
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a well-known "social cost of disfluency" claim gets substantially narrowed, and everyone has an opinion about filled pauses.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Previous work on the penalty for saying *um* and *uh* relied on scripted speech recorded in labs. Lee and Papafragou used clips of genuine spontaneous speech across four experiments with an expanded battery of social trait ratings. Filled pauses had no effect on how competent, trustworthy or warm the speaker seemed as a person — but within a given conversation they lowered trustworthiness, made speakers seem less ready and less certain, and made listeners more likely to avoid them in future. Notably, experts who used filled pauses were rated as more careful than novices who did.

**Why it matters:** It separates a reputational judgment ("what kind of person is this?") from a moment-to-moment one ("do I believe this right now?"), and suggests the same hesitation reads as bumbling or as conscientious depending on who is speaking.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the public-speaking industry's war on *um*. The surprise is that filled pauses don't damage how you are seen as a person at all — the cost is local and specific, and it flips to a bonus if the listener already thinks you know your stuff. Call Jon Lee or Anna Papafragou at the University of Pennsylvania, and press them on the expert/novice reversal.
- **Wider angle:** For a language or tech outlet: synthetic voices and AI assistants are being deliberately engineered with hesitations to sound more human. This work suggests which disfluencies buy warmth and which quietly erode trust — a design story about the sound of machine speech.

**Caveats:** Ratings of recorded clips rather than live interaction; English filled pauses only, so cross-linguistic generalization is unclear; sample details not given in the abstract.

### 3. Being told you're wrong when you felt sure makes you doubt yourself for a long while afterward

**Journal:** *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* | **Published:** September 14, 2026
**PMID:** 42734967 | **DOI:** 10.1037/xge0001997
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a genuinely new phenomenon ("metacognitive conflict") with a computational account and obvious everyday resonance; limited by the abstract perceptual task.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Across a series of perceptual decision experiments, participants rated their confidence and then received performance feedback. When the two clashed — high confidence followed by "wrong", or low confidence followed by "right" — people reported markedly lower confidence on the next trial. The adjustment shrank in blocks where such clashes were frequent, and overall confidence was depressed throughout those blocks. An evidence-accumulation model with post-decision accumulation located the effect in a biased starting point: after a clash, people become systematically more conservative about claiming certainty.

**Why it matters:** It shows that feedback doesn't just correct what you believe, it recalibrates how much you trust your own sense of knowing — and repeated contradiction produces a generalized dip in confidence rather than better accuracy.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is that the damage from being corrected isn't to your answer, it's to your confidence system — and it spills over to unrelated decisions. Open on a student or junior employee whose work is repeatedly marked wrong despite feeling right. Call Kobe Desender's lab at KU Leuven, which specializes in modeling confidence, and ask whether the effect ever resolves.
- **Wider angle:** An education or workplace-management feature on feedback design: why frequent correction can flatten confidence without improving judgment, and what that means for training regimes in medicine, aviation and machine-assisted diagnosis, where calibrated confidence matters more than raw accuracy.

**Caveats:** Laboratory perceptual tasks with monetary-free stakes; sample size not reported in the abstract; short-term effects only, no follow-up beyond the session.

### 4. People who have attempted suicide quit faster after a setback — and everyone with depression persists too long

**Journal:** *Behaviour Research and Therapy* | **Published:** October 2026 (online September 5, 2026)
**PMID:** 42704954 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.brat.2026.105148
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a well-powered four-group design that reframes a muddled literature on impulsivity and suicide, though the task is far from a real crisis.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Tsypes and colleagues tested 277 adults from a mid- to late-life depression cohort — 87 who had attempted suicide, 59 with ideation only, 63 depressed non-suicidal and 68 controls — on a Willingness-to-Wait task where waiting up to three seconds maximized reward but longer waits were counterproductive. Everyone was more likely to quit immediately after a loss than a win, but this loss-triggered disengagement was largest in attempters. Once they had begun waiting, however, both attempters and depressed non-suicidal participants overpersisted relative to controls. An earlier age at first attempt — not attempt lethality — predicted stronger loss-triggered quitting, and results survived adjustment for impulsivity, cognition and personality.

**Why it matters:** It splits two things that delay-discounting research has been conflating — deciding whether to re-engage after failure, versus deciding when to stop — and suggests depression and suicide attempt history sit on different sides of that split.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the double dissociation: depression makes people wait too long for things that aren't coming, while a history of attempts adds a hair-trigger for walking away after a loss. Open on the decision-by-decision texture of a difficult day rather than the crisis itself. Call Alexandre Dombrovski at the University of Pittsburgh, who has built much of the computational psychiatry of suicidal decision-making. Do not frame the task as a screening tool.
- **Wider angle:** For a specialist psychiatry or long-read outlet: the shift from treating suicide as a single catastrophic act to modeling it as a sequence of small, reversible decisions about persisting or quitting — and what that reframing means for crisis-line and safety-planning practice.

**Caveats:** Observational group comparison, cannot establish causation; mid- to late-life cohort may not generalize to younger people; laboratory task with small monetary stakes is a distant proxy for suicidal crisis; secondary analysis of an existing cohort.

### 5. Autistic people use rewards to guide their decisions exactly as much as anyone else

**Journal:** *Cognition* | **Published:** September 10, 2026
**PMID:** 42721846 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cognition.2026.106716
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a clean quantitative test that contradicts both the "reduced reward sensitivity" story and the "autistic people are more rational" story at once.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Thirty-two autistic and 48 non-autistic adults performed an orientation categorization task in which the monetary payoff for a correct answer differed between categories. Using signal detection theory, the researchers estimated where each person placed their decision boundary while accounting for sensory uncertainty and prior expectation. Both groups understood the reward structure equally well and had comparable perceptual sensitivity — and autistic participants shifted their boundaries toward reward in the same suboptimal way as everyone else.

**Why it matters:** Claims of blunted reward processing in autism come mainly from neuroimaging; this shows that at the level of actual behavior, reward is integrated normally — and that autistic decision-making is no more coldly optimal than anyone's.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Two popular ideas fall at once — that autistic brains are less moved by reward, and that autistic people are the rational ones who don't fall for framing effects. Both groups were equally, identically irrational. Call Amit Yashar or Bat-Sheva Hadad at the University of Haifa and ask how a normal behavioral result squares with the neuroimaging literature.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-and-meaning feature for a psychology or neuroscience outlet on the growing gap between what brain-imaging differences in autism seem to show and what carefully quantified behavior shows — part of a broader reckoning about inferring cognition from BOLD signal.

**Caveats:** Small autistic sample (n=32), single task and single reward modality (money), laboratory setting; null results require careful interpretation even with good quantitative modeling.

### 6. Telling women what antibiotics do to their gut cuts demand as effectively as invoking superbugs

**Journal:** *British Journal of Health Psychology* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42702911 | **DOI:** 10.1111/bjhp.70109
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — large, replicated, randomized experiments on a real public health lever, but hypothetical consultations limit the punch.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Across three online experiments with 3057 women in the UK, participants imagined a physician consultation for an uncomplicated urinary tract infection. The doctor either explained an individual consequence of antibiotics (disruption of the gut microbiome), a societal one (antimicrobial resistance), or neither. An internal meta-analysis found both messages significantly reduced expectations of receiving antibiotics; neither damaged trust in the physician, and both reduced decisional conflict.

**Why it matters:** Antibiotic stewardship messaging leans heavily on the collective threat of resistance; this suggests a purely self-interested message works just as well — and that raising the downsides doesn't make patients trust their doctor less, which is the usual clinical worry.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is the absence of the expected trade-off — the assumption that a doctor who talks a patient out of antibiotics will be seen as fobbing them off doesn't hold, and self-interest is as persuasive as saving the world. Open in a GP surgery on a Monday morning. Call Cornelia Betsch at the University of Erfurt, who works on health communication and vaccination decisions.
- **Wider angle:** For a global health outlet: the wider evidence on whether "planetary" framings — resistance, emissions, biodiversity — actually move individual behavior compared with personal-consequence framings, and what that implies for the entire architecture of public health persuasion.

**Caveats:** Hypothetical consultations and self-reported expectations, not prescription data; UK women only, so generalization to other health systems and to men or complicated infections is untested; no clinical follow-up.

### 7. AI teammates can be drawn into sharing what only they know — if someone asks

**Journal:** *Human Factors* | **Published:** September 8, 2026
**PMID:** 42708610 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00187208261477411
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a classic team-psychology paradigm adapted to human-AI teams for the first time; strong hook, moderate sample and exploratory secondary findings.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (1 hits)

**The study:** Kerstan and colleagues ran the "hidden profile" task — a staple of team research, in which the right answer only emerges if members share information nobody else has — with 98 participants in human-AI three-member teams and 65 in human-only teams, manipulating whether information inquiry was prompted and what teams expected of their own performance. Prompting inquiry increased information sharing and improved decision quality in both team types, regardless of performance expectations. Exploratory analyses hinted that the patterns of who asks what may differ when a teammate is an AI.

**Why it matters:** Human-AI decision-making has mostly been modeled as "AI recommends, human disposes"; this shows that language models can be slotted into genuinely collaborative paradigms, and that the classic fix for groupthink — asking questions — still works.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Groups are famously bad at surfacing the one fact only one member holds. Add an AI to the team and the same failure appears — along with the same cure. Open on a hospital case conference or a hiring panel with an AI in the room. Call Sarah Kerstan (University of Freiburg) and ask whether people interrogate an AI teammate differently from a human one.
- **Wider angle:** A workplace-technology piece for a business or management outlet on redesigning meeting norms for mixed human-AI teams — where the risk isn't the AI being wrong, but nobody thinking to ask it the right question.

**Caveats:** Moderate sample of teams, single laboratory task, differences in inquiry patterns between team types were exploratory rather than confirmatory; results depend on the specific LLM used.

### 8. Playing the villain pulls you deeper into a game — but only when the choice is really yours

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** September 22, 2026
**PMID:** 42784909 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107861
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a crossover interaction that explains contradictory findings in a very relatable domain; self-report measures hold it back.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** 320 Chinese gamers played moral dilemma scenarios drawn from *Detroit: Become Human* and *BioShock* in a 2×2 design crossing high- versus low-morality choices with high versus low autonomy over the choice. Guilt, shame and pride all mediated the link between the choice made and reported narrative immersion. Crucially, when players felt the decision was genuinely theirs, immoral choices produced stronger immersion than moral ones — a crossover interaction that may reconcile the inconsistent previous results.

**Why it matters:** It suggests immersion is driven by the emotional cost of a decision you own, not by the decision's content — which is why railroaded morality systems in games so often fall flat.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Why does the nasty option feel more gripping? Because guilt is a stronger engagement engine than pride — provided the game let you choose freely. Open on the hardest choice in a game most readers will recognize. Call the authors at Shaanxi Normal University, and pair with a games designer who has built a morality system, for the practitioner's view.
- **Wider angle:** For a culture or games outlet: the design history of morality meters, from *Fable* to *Disco Elysium*, and the evidence that binary good/evil scoring undermines the very immersion it is meant to create.

**Caveats:** Self-reported immersion and emotion; single cultural sample of Chinese players; short lab exposures rather than extended play; borrowed commercial stimuli limit experimental control.

### 9. Time perception in autistic traits looks fine — it's the decision rule that differs

**Journal:** *Consciousness and Cognition* | **Published:** September 10, 2026
**PMID:** 42721950 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.concog.2026.104123
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — Bayesian evidence for the absence of a perceptual deficit is a useful corrective, but the sample is small and non-clinical.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Sixty-five non-clinical adults varying in autistic traits judged sub-second durations (100 and 500 ms) while the researchers used signal detection theory to separate perceptual sensitivity from decision bias. Sensitivity was unrelated to autistic traits, with Bayesian analyses actively supporting the null. Instead, higher scores on the Imagination subscale of the Autism-Spectrum Quotient predicted a shift in decision bias: rather than building a stabilized internal reference from the repeated standard stimulus, these participants anchored on whichever stimulus came first.

**Why it matters:** Reported timing "deficits" across the autistic spectrum may be a strategy difference — favoring the immediately available over the accumulated — rather than a broken clock.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The recurring finding that autistic people experience time differently may be about how they decide, not how they perceive. Open on the everyday version: relying on what just happened rather than on an averaged sense of "how long things usually take". Call Vincenzo Romei at the University of Bologna.
- **Wider angle:** A methods feature for a psychology-focused outlet on how many "perceptual deficits" in clinical and subclinical groups dissolve once sensitivity and criterion are properly separated — a signal-detection audit of neurodivergence research.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=65), non-clinical participants with self-reported trait scores rather than diagnosed autism, single timing paradigm, subscale-level finding that invites replication.

### 10. The brains of anxious, depressed gamblers barely flinch at a loss

**Journal:** *Psychological Medicine* | **Published:** September 24, 2026
**PMID:** 42779459 | **DOI:** 10.1017/S0033291726105534
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a decent clinical sample and a plausible resolution of a contradictory EEG literature, but the subtypes come from data-driven clustering that needs replicating.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** EEG was recorded from 120 people with gambling disorder and 52 healthy controls during the Balloon Analogue Risk Task. Clustering on impulsivity, anxiety and depression scores split the patients into an "impulsive type" and an "emotionally vulnerable type" — the latter with more severe gambling. Only the emotionally vulnerable group showed blunted feedback-related negativity and feedback-P3 responses to losses; the impulsive group was indistinguishable from controls. Within the vulnerable group alone, a smaller feedback-P3 predicted worse gambling severity.

**Why it matters:** It suggests gambling disorder isn't one condition with one neural signature, and that the group whose brains register losses least are the ones doing worst — which could explain why aggregate studies of loss processing in gambling have never agreed.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the counterintuitive pairing: it's not the reckless thrill-seekers whose brains ignore losses, it's the anxious and depressed gamblers. Open on the moment a bet goes wrong and nothing registers. Call the group at Shanghai Mental Health Center. Be clear this is a group difference, not a test anyone can take.
- **Wider angle:** For a health-policy or investigative outlet: gambling products are designed around loss feedback — near misses, losses disguised as wins. What does an industry built on feedback do to players whose feedback response is already blunted?

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational, cannot establish causation; subtypes derived by k-means clustering in a single sample and not independently validated; single center; unequal group sizes.

### 11. People with more autistic traits take the money now

**Journal:** *Behavioural Brain Research* | **Published:** September 10, 2026
**PMID:** 42722131 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bbr.2026.116466
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a careful, honestly hedged result on impulsive choice, but modest sample and the authors explicitly decline to claim a mechanism.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Eighty-one participants, split by autistic trait level, chose between sooner-smaller and later-larger rewards while also completing inhibitory control, working memory and cognitive flexibility tasks. The high-trait group discounted delayed rewards more steeply and chose immediate rewards more often across reward differences — but rated the rewards as equally attractive, ruling out a simple valuation difference. Trial-level modeling confirmed a reduced probability of choosing later rewards without any change in sensitivity to reward size or delay.

**Why it matters:** It locates the difference in the choice itself rather than in how rewards are valued, and sits interestingly alongside evidence (see entry 5) that reward integration in autism is otherwise intact.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Pair this with the Haifa reward study for a short piece on where autistic decision-making does and doesn't differ — rewards are valued normally, but the pull of "now" is stronger. Call Shu Zhang at Bengbu Medical University, and note the authors' own caution that the cognitive mechanism remains unidentified.
- **Wider angle:** For a personal-finance or neurodiversity outlet: the practical consequences of steeper delay discounting for saving, subscriptions and debt — and whether financial tools designed for neurodivergent users make any difference.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=81), subclinical trait groups rather than diagnosed autism, cross-sectional, hypothetical monetary choices; authors explicitly state no mechanism is established.

### 12. Money worries don't stop people giving — they change how they want to give

**Journal:** *The Journal of Social Psychology* | **Published:** September 9, 2026
**PMID:** 42713986 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00224545.2026.2730401
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a neat inversion of the scarcity-reduces-generosity story with three experiments, but the mediator and moderator analyses are the fragile kind.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Across three experiments, Liang and colleagues found that people who perceived themselves as financially constrained preferred donation setups offering multiple causes to choose from over a single one. The preference was mediated by "face consciousness" — concern with social image under economic stress — and moderated by self-construal: people with an independent self-construal leaned toward single-category giving under constraint, while interdependent people leaned toward multiple.

**Why it matters:** Scarcity research has mostly asked whether poorer people give less; this asks how they want the choice framed, which is a question charities and platforms can actually act on.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the reframing: hard-up donors aren't less generous, they're more strategic about how their giving looks. Open on a supermarket checkout charity prompt. Call the Guangxi University team, and get a fundraising researcher to say whether donation platforms would change anything on the back of this.
- **Wider angle:** For a business or nonprofit-sector outlet: a piece on choice architecture in the giving economy — round-ups, matched donations, multi-cause portals — and the evidence base, or lack of one, behind how those menus are built.

**Caveats:** Experimental manipulations of perceived constraint rather than real financial hardship; mediation and moderation analyses are exploratory in character; likely student or online samples with limited cultural range; sample sizes not stated in the abstract.

### 13. Nobody knows why finding your way together is so much harder than doing it alone

**Journal:** *WIREs Cognitive Science* | **Published:** September–October 2026
**PMID:** 42767709 | **DOI:** 10.1002/wcs.70040
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a review rather than new data, but it stakes out a genuinely neglected question that every reader has lived through.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Fernandez Velasco and Sutton note that almost all navigation research studies lone wanderers, despite wayfinding being overwhelmingly a joint activity — and despite GPS conspicuously failing to end navigational arguments. They critically review the handful of recent experiments and import tools from five mature fields (social ontology, distributed cognition, transactive memory, group problem-solving and collaborative recall), proposing designs to measure shared spatial knowledge, collaborative process gains and losses, and the division of cognitive labor between people with matching or clashing navigational strategies.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The evergreen hook — why couples argue in the car — with a real intellectual spine: navigation science has almost no account of navigating together, and a shared history of routes may matter more than either person's sense of direction. Open on a specific failed journey. Call Pablo Fernandez Velasco at the University of Stirling.
- **Wider angle:** A design story for a technology outlet: satnavs and mapping apps are built for a single user holding a single phone, and this review sketches what a genuinely multi-person navigation interface would need to do — including deciding whose mental map wins.

**Caveats:** Narrative review with no new data; proposals are programmatic; the evidence base it draws on is small and methodologically heterogeneous.

### 14. Letting advertising change what you care about might be a rational strategy

**Journal:** *Topics in Cognitive Science* | **Published:** September 22, 2026
**PMID:** 42773691 | **DOI:** 10.1111/tops.70084
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a provocative argument that inverts a standard assumption about manipulation, but it is theory rather than evidence.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Guzman and Aronowitz argue that theories of rationality have no place for the way our environment quietly reshapes what we value — the ad that makes you care about a food, the friend who converts you to a genre. Drawing on research into persistent attentional biases, they propose a two-step route: attention captured in the moment becomes a durable attentional habit, and durable attentional habits shape how we structure our tasks and therefore what we come to value. On their account, being open to this is not a bug but a resource-rational solution to the problem of choosing values with limited cognitive resources.

**Why it matters:** It reframes susceptibility to influence as adaptive rather than defective, which cuts against most of the popular literature on manipulation and nudging.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The uncomfortable argument: your values are partly outsourced to your environment, and that may be the smart way to run a finite mind. Open on something the reader started liking without deciding to. Call Sara Aronowitz at the University of Toronto and push her on where the line between rational openness and exploitation falls.
- **Wider angle:** For a philosophy or ideas outlet: what this does to the ethics of attention-economy regulation. If openness to influence is rational, arguments for protecting people from persuasive design need a different foundation than "manipulation corrupts your true preferences".

**Caveats:** Theoretical paper with no empirical test; the attentional-bias literature it builds on was not designed to address value change; the framework is explicitly a first step.

### 15. Parents rarely regret the surgery that disconnects half their child's brain

**Journal:** *Epileptic Disorders* | **Published:** September 12, 2026
**PMID:** 42730552 | **DOI:** 10.1002/epd2.70394
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 5/10 — extraordinary numbers on both sides of the trade-off, tempered by a small retrospective caregiver-report registry.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Using caregiver survey data from the Global Pediatric Epilepsy Surgery Registry, researchers followed 37 children with Rasmussen's encephalitis who underwent hemispheric disconnection. Seizure freedom reached 84.8% and correlated with improved quality of life. The costs were severe: hemiparesis rose from 22% to 97%, visual deficits from 16% to 84.4%, and around a third lost the ability to run or roll unassisted. Even so, 86% of caregivers reported improved quality of life, 54.8% wished the surgery had been done sooner, and only 12.5% expressed regret. Left-sided surgery predicted language deficits; older age at surgery predicted greater motor loss.

**Why it matters:** It is a rare quantification of how families actually evaluate an irreversible trade-off that looks, from outside, almost impossible — and it suggests the usual clinical instinct to delay may itself carry a cost.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The decision science of the unthinkable: more parents regret waiting than regret operating. Open on one family's timeline from first seizure to surgery. Call Taylor Abel at UPMC Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh. Frame it around counseling and expectation-setting, not as evidence that earlier surgery is better — that comparison hasn't been made.
- **Wider angle:** For a long-form health or disability outlet: a piece on how families and clinicians weigh function against seizure control across pediatric neurology, and on the adaptation literature suggesting people consistently mispredict how they will feel about severe disability.

**Caveats:** Small retrospective cohort (n=37); caregiver-reported outcomes only, with no direct child-reported quality of life; self-selected registry participants likely skew toward engaged families; no comparison group of children who did not have surgery; recall bias over up to five years.

### 16. Where a seizure starts shapes how a person gambles

**Journal:** *Epilepsy Research* | **Published:** September 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42710371 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.eplepsyres.2026.107906
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — an intriguing left/right dissociation in risky choice, but very small groups and the authors themselves call the evidence suggestive.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** No recent NS.com coverage found ✓ (0 hits)

**The study:** Forty-three adults with drug-resistant focal epilepsy (15 right temporal, 18 left temporal, 10 frontal) and 15 controls completed the Cambridge Gambling Task, analyzed with Bayesian regression models tailored to each outcome's distribution. Decision quality and risk adjustment tended to be poorer across all epilepsy groups. The temporal groups diverged: left temporal patients took 1.73 times longer than controls to decide, while right temporal patients showed heightened delay aversion alongside unusually risk-averse betting. Frontal patients fell in between.

**Why it matters:** If replicated, it would suggest that risky decision-making is disrupted differently depending on which temporal lobe is involved — relevant to how neuropsychologists interpret decision tasks in epilepsy.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** A short piece on lateralized decision styles: slow and deliberate on the left, impatient but cautious on the right. Call Eduardo Verche at Universidad Complutense de Madrid and ask how much weight the Bayesian framework can bear with a dozen patients per group.
- **Wider angle:** For a statistics-literate or specialist audience: a methods story on Bayesian analysis as a response to the small-N problem in clinical neuropsychology — what probabilistic characterization buys you, and where it can mislead.

**Caveats:** Very small groups (10–18 per arm), single center, case-control design that cannot establish causation, medication effects not addressed in the abstract, findings described by the authors as suggestive and requiring replication.

### 17. Parents of autistic children show subtle differences in attention and memory

**Journal:** *Autism Research* | **Published:** September 11, 2026
**PMID:** 42728861 | **DOI:** 10.1002/aur.70363
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 4/10 — adds to the broad autism phenotype literature with a standardized battery, but the pattern is mixed and the design cannot separate genetics from the stress of caregiving.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)
**NS.com check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** Tekeli Uçar and Eyüboğlu tested 124 parents — mothers and fathers of autistic children aged 2 to 12, against parents of neurotypical children — on CANTAB tasks measuring reaction time, spatial working memory and paired associates learning. The autism-parent group performed worse on reaction time and on visual episodic memory; spatial working memory results, which tap strategy and decision-making, varied by subtest. Five-choice reaction time was the only significant predictor of group membership in regression, and parental performance did not correlate with the child's autism severity.

**Why it matters:** It supports the idea of measurable cognitive endophenotypes in first-degree relatives, though the absence of any link to child severity complicates a simple genetic dosage story.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** A cautious piece on the broad autism phenotype — the traits that run in families without ever meeting a diagnostic threshold — anchored on why the researchers found no relationship with how severely the child is affected. Call Murat Eyüboğlu at Eskisehir Osmangazi University. Avoid any framing that pathologizes parents.
- **Wider angle:** For a genetics or family-health outlet: what endophenotype research is actually for, and the unresolved confound running through all of it — caregiving a child with high support needs is exhausting, and exhaustion slows reaction times too.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational; single center in Turkey, may not generalize; mixed and subtest-dependent findings; no measure of parental sleep, stress or mental health to rule out caregiving burden as an explanation; group sizes per parent sex not reported.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** A cluster of studies in this batch takes phenomena long described as perceptual or motivational "deficits" in clinical and neurodivergent groups and, using signal detection theory and evidence-accumulation modeling, relocates them in the decision rule instead — "Autistic people use rewards to guide their decisions exactly as much as anyone else" (PMID 42721846) finds reward integration intact, "Time perception in autistic traits looks fine — it's the decision rule that differs" (PMID 42721950) reports Bayesian support for the null on sensitivity while decision bias shifts, and "People with more autistic traits take the money now" (PMID 42722131) pins steeper discounting to the choice rather than to how rewards are valued. The same decomposition logic is doing clinical work elsewhere in the batch: "People who have attempted suicide quit faster after a setback" (PMID 42704954) splits re-engagement after failure from when to stop, and "The brains of anxious, depressed gamblers barely flinch at a loss" (PMID 42779459) shows a blunted loss signal in only one data-derived subtype, which may explain why aggregate studies never agreed.

**Why pitch this now:** Several of these are explicit overturnings of established claims published within three weeks of each other, and they converge on a methodological point — that much of what has been reported as altered perception or blunted reward in autism, depression and addiction may be a difference in where people set their criterion, not in what their brains register.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the quiet re-analysis of neurodivergence and psychiatric research, following researchers who are pulling apart "sensitivity" and "criterion" and asking how many textbook deficits survive the operation — written for readers who follow mental health science and are used to the deficit framing, with autistic and clinician voices on what it changes in practice.

**Potential headlines:**
- Separating perception from judgment dissolves several reported deficits in autism
- The clock is fine; it is the decision about the clock that differs
- What your diagnosis is said to do to your brain may be a statement about where you set the bar
