### 1. Teenagers' hearts slow down when they scroll before bed — so screens may not be keeping them awake the way we think

**Journal:** *Journal of Sleep Research* | **Published:** August 2026 (epub January 20, 2026)
**PMID:** 41556268 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jsr.70288
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — objective wearable-camera plus heart-rate data directly contradict the "screens cause physiological arousal" story, though the sample is small.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)

**The study:** Researchers in New Zealand filmed the evenings of 70 young people aged 11–14.9 (37% Māori, 42% female) using wearable cameras across four nights, while Fitbit Inspire 2 devices logged heart rate. Median heart rate was substantially *lower* during screen use than during non-screen activity (83 vs. 93 bpm), and social media use closest to bedtime was associated with a further small drop (−3 bpm), while messaging and calls nudged heart rate up (+3 bpm). Pre-bed heart rate was unrelated to sleep outcomes except sleep latency, which rose by about 9 minutes per 10 bpm.

**Why it matters:** The dominant public explanation for screens harming teen sleep — that devices leave kids physiologically wound up — gets little support here; the more plausible culprit is simple displacement of bedtime.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on a 13-year-old lying in bed on TikTok with a camera on her chest and a heart-rate monitor on her wrist — and a pulse that is calmer than when she was doing homework. The hook: scrolling may be a relaxation ritual, and the sleep problem is about clocks, not cortisol. Call Kim Meredith-Jones and Rachael Taylor at the University of Otago, who have run several objective screen-use studies.
- **Wider angle:** A methods-and-evidence feature for a parenting or education outlet on how wearable cameras are quietly dismantling a decade of self-reported screen-time research — and why the policy debate has run far ahead of the measurements.

**Caveats:** Small N (70), single center, observational within-person design, short monitoring window (four nights), Fitbit-derived heart rate is a proxy for arousal, and findings may not generalize beyond this age band or region.

### 2. Unusual mental experiences come first, phone dependence follows — via daytime sleepiness

**Journal:** *Psychiatry Research* | **Published:** August 18, 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42636649 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117401
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 7/10 — a large three-wave cohort reverses the assumed arrow of causation, but everything is self-reported.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)

**The study:** A total of 6,153 Chinese college students (79.7% female; mean age 20.1) reported problematic smartphone use, psychotic-like experiences and excessive daytime sleepiness three times over 12 months. Conventional cross-lagged models showed the usual tangle of associations — but once stable between-person differences were removed, the within-person evidence for a two-way street vanished. What remained was a single pathway: more psychotic-like experiences at wave 1 predicted more daytime sleepiness at wave 2, which predicted heavier problematic smartphone use at wave 3.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that in some young people, heavy phone use is downstream of distress and disrupted sleep rather than its cause — a direct challenge to the simplest version of the "phones are driving youth mental illness" narrative.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the reversal: the phone may be the symptom, not the disease. Frame around one student whose odd perceptual experiences and unrelenting daytime fog left the phone as the only thing that fit. The mediation via sleepiness is the mechanistic hook. Call Dongfang Wang at South China Normal University. Be explicit that this is observational and cannot license clinical advice.
- **Wider angle:** A statistics story for a general-interest or data-journalism outlet on how the switch from CLPM to RI-CLPM has been erasing "bidirectional" screen findings across the field — a quiet methodological correction with big public consequences.

**Caveats:** Observational (cannot establish causation), entirely self-reported measures, heavily female sample of Chinese university students that may not generalize, six-month intervals may miss faster dynamics.

### 3. Feeling short of time hollows out life's meaning — and the fix is what you aim for, not what you do

**Journal:** *Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42541341 | **DOI:** 10.1111/aphw.70200
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 7/10 — five studies including two huge naturalistic datasets, an experiment and a successful brief intervention; unusually complete evidence chain.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Across five studies, researchers linked the chronic feeling of "time poverty" to lower meaning in life: archival data (N=54,568), natural-language analysis of 126,424 social media posts, a validated survey (N=383), an experiment manipulating time-poor context (N=160), and a brief reflection task (N=396). The mechanism was a shrinking of *intrinsic* goal aspirations — growth, relationships, community. Extrinsic goals were also associated with time poverty but had no relationship with meaning; a short reflection on intrinsic goals blunted the effect.

**Why it matters:** It reframes busyness as a goal-content problem rather than a scheduling problem, and offers a testable, cheap countermeasure.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the language of hurry in social media posts — what 126,000 people actually write when they feel out of time — then the payoff: time scarcity doesn't just crowd out activities, it quietly rewrites what you want. Call Yan Mu at the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Note the experimental studies are lab-scale and short-term.
- **Wider angle:** A work-and-economics feature for a business or long-read outlet on time poverty as a distributional issue — who gets to hold intrinsic goals, and what four-day-week and caregiving policy would need to do to protect meaning rather than just hours.

**Caveats:** Studies 1–3 are cross-sectional/observational; experimental effects are short-term with no follow-up; social media language analysis is a proxy measure; samples are largely Chinese and may not generalize.

### 4. What you see in your feed pushes your body image around — but your body image barely steers what you look at

**Journal:** *Body Image* | **Published:** July 31, 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42537570 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bodyim.2026.102157
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 7/10 — intensive real-time sampling in a large sample kills the popular "you only see it because you seek it out" defense; still self-reported exposure.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 858 undergraduates (78.3% women) reported six times a day for a week on their momentary exposure to thinspiration, fitspiration and fatspiration content, plus body dissatisfaction, objectification, appreciation, and urges to restrict or exercise. Viewing thinspiration predicted greater body dissatisfaction; fitspiration predicted *lower* body appreciation; fatspiration predicted stronger urges to exercise. In the reverse direction, only one path was significant — higher body objectification predicted *less* subsequent thinspiration viewing. Gender did not moderate any of it.

**Why it matters:** The selectivity argument — that vulnerable people simply seek out this content — gets almost no support in real time, and "fitspiration" looks no safer than thinspiration. The null gender moderation also undercuts the framing of this as a girls' issue.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is asymmetry: content moves the person far more than the person moves the content. Open on a student pinged at 3pm mid-scroll. Ask why fitspiration erodes body *appreciation* even while looking wholesome. Call Isabel Krug at the University of Melbourne.
- **Wider angle:** A platform-accountability piece for a technology or policy outlet: if exposure effects are largely one-directional and gender-neutral, what does that imply for age-gated content rules and recommender audits currently written around teenage girls?

**Caveats:** Undergraduate sample may not generalize; exposure and outcomes both self-reported at the same prompts; associations are momentary and observational; effect sizes not reported in the abstract; one-week window.

### 5. Watching yourself through the camera's eye may make you less able to feel hunger

**Journal:** *Body Image* | **Published:** July 31, 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42537569 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bodyim.2026.102156
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a plausible, specific mechanistic pathway with longitudinal support, but only two time points and one gender.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 762 women-identifying college students (353 at follow-up) completed measures of appearance-related social media consciousness (ASMC — the habit of monitoring how you'd look to an online audience), interoceptive sensibility, and eating pathology one month apart. ASMC predicted rising body dissatisfaction but not binge eating or restraint. Crucially, ASMC predicted *reduced acceptance of bodily sensations*, and that reduced acceptance in turn predicted more restrictive eating, with a significant indirect path.

**Why it matters:** It offers a concrete route from social media habits to restriction that isn't about comparison or thin-ideal internalization — it's about tuning out the body's own signals.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The hook is a strange trade-off: the more attention you pay to how your body *looks* to an imagined feed, the less you accept how it *feels*. Open on the moment of checking a photo before posting. Call April Smith at Auburn University. Frame as mechanism, not clinical advice.
- **Wider angle:** A broader mind-body feature for a health or psychology outlet on interoception as an emerging common currency across anxiety, eating disorders and chronic pain — and whether interoceptive training does anything useful.

**Caveats:** Observational half-longitudinal design (cannot establish causation), women-only college sample that may not generalize, one-month follow-up, self-reported outcomes, substantial attrition between waves.

### 6. People recovering from anorexia describe a phase of relentless hunger that clinical textbooks don't mention

**Journal:** *International Journal of Eating Disorders* | **Published:** August 2026 (epub April 15, 2026)
**PMID:** 41983301 | **DOI:** 10.1002/eat.70109
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a genuinely undocumented clinical phenomenon surfaced from patient forums; qualitative and non-representative.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Researchers pulled 987 posts from r/AnorexiaRecovery (July 2023–January 2024) and thematically analyzed the 92 (9.3%) that described "extreme hunger" during refeeding — a phenomenon absent from the clinical literature. Three themes emerged: anxiety that the hunger was abnormal and signaled binge eating disorder; extreme hunger as a threat to recovery, prompting distress, suicidal thoughts, renewed restriction and relapse; and a perceived vacuum of professional guidance, driving people to online sources instead.

**Why it matters:** A predictable, biologically plausible stage of recovery is going unexplained by clinicians, and the resulting fear appears to push people back into restriction — a fixable gap.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead on the vacuum: thousands of people are asking a subreddit a question their clinicians have never named. Open on a single anonymized post. The story writes itself as patient-generated knowledge outpacing the literature. Call Léa Langanay or Antoine Lamer in Lille. Do not present forum accounts as prevalence data.
- **Wider angle:** A media/health-information feature for a specialist outlet on Reddit as an unlicensed clinical evidence source — how researchers are now mining forums for symptoms medicine has failed to record, and the ethics of doing so without consent.

**Caveats:** Qualitative analysis of public posts, no demographic or diagnostic verification, self-selected online population that may not generalize, no prevalence estimate, no control group.

### 7. Ozempic has been absorbed into pro-eating-disorder Twitter as everyday, normalized talk

**Journal:** *International Journal of Eating Disorders* | **Published:** August 16, 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42604953 | **DOI:** 10.1002/eat.70190
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — highly topical and the first systematic look at GLP-1 discourse in this community, but a small, descriptive corpus.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Using netnography, researchers retrieved public posts on X containing both "Ozempic" and "edtwt" (the eating-disorder Twitter tag) from August 2024 to July 2025, analyzing 255 after exclusions. The single largest theme was normalization and cultural saturation (28.6%) — Ozempic treated as unremarkable background. Personal narratives covered curiosity (19.2%), intent to use (11.8%), active use or experimentation (11.4%) and access (9.0%). Risk warnings and information sharing made up just 3.5%.

**Why it matters:** GLP-1 drugs are entering the most vulnerable online communities almost entirely without countervailing risk information, and access appears to be a live, discussed problem rather than a hypothetical one.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The number to lead on is 3.5% — the share of posts containing any risk warning, against 28.6% treating the drug as normal. Frame as: a prescription medication has been culturally absorbed by a community organized around restriction. Call Jennifer Couturier at McMaster. Avoid amplifying access routes.
- **Wider angle:** A pharmaceutical-regulation investigation for a health-policy or business outlet: how telehealth prescribing, compounded semaglutide and grey-market supply intersect with online communities that platforms have repeatedly failed to moderate.

**Caveats:** Small corpus (255 posts), single platform, descriptive content analysis with no user characteristics, no way to verify who posted or whether stated intentions became behavior, time-limited sampling window.

### 8. Classmates can reliably name the school's "influencers" — and those teens are both kinder and more aggressive than their peers

**Journal:** *The Journal of Genetic Psychology* | **Published:** August 4, 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42552601 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00221325.2026.2708120
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a new peer-nomination method that sidesteps self-reported screen time, though single-school and cross-sectional.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 331 seventh and eighth graders in a small Midwestern US community nominated which classmates were most visible on social media. These peer nominations corresponded moderately with the nominees' own reports of social media use — and predicted higher levels of prosocial behavior, higher physical, verbal and relational aggression, and more self-reported substance use.

**Why it matters:** It validates a way of measuring adolescent social media prominence that doesn't depend on teens estimating their own screen time, and it complicates the picture of the visible teen: social capital and social harm travel together.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The surprise is that the most-followed kid in year 8 is simultaneously the most helpful and the most aggressive — visibility looks like a form of social power, with everything that implies. Open on the nomination exercise itself. Call Lara Mayeux at the University of Oklahoma.
- **Wider angle:** A schools feature for an education outlet on peer-nomination methods generally — what happens when researchers ask children to map their own social hierarchies, and what teachers could learn from those maps.

**Caveats:** Single school in one small community (population may not generalize), cross-sectional and observational, substance use and social media use self-reported, no measure of what the visible teens actually posted.

### 9. A substantial minority of people press "like" specifically to hurt someone

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** August 2026 (epub July 9, 2026)
**PMID:** 42424914 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107413
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a delightfully counterintuitive four-study package on an unstudied behavior; evidence is mostly survey-based.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Four studies examined "counter-valenced liking" — using the like button on bad news about a rival. Study 1 used five years of archival data on likes attached to sports teams' negative posts, showing the behavior increasing over time. Study 2 (qualitative) found a substantial minority admitting to it, most citing schadenfreude. Study 3 confirmed a correlation between trait schadenfreude and liking negative outcomes, including injuries. Study 4 found that people most bothered by receiving such likes are more likely to do it themselves, and a notable number said they did it explicitly to hurt the other party.

**Why it matters:** A button designed to signal approval has been repurposed as a low-cost weapon — a neat case study in how platform affordances get inverted by users, and how content-moderation systems that read likes as positive signals are misreading them.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Open on the moment a fan sees a rival's star player carried off the pitch — and taps the heart. The hook: "engagement" metrics can't tell joy from cruelty. Call Devin Lunt at Morehead State University.
- **Wider angle:** A technology feature for a general-interest outlet on the semantics of reaction buttons — why platforms keep adding emoji and keep failing to capture what users mean, and what that does to recommendation algorithms trained on those signals.

**Caveats:** Largely self-report and cross-sectional, sports-fandom context may not generalize to political or personal contexts, no behavioral measure of actual liking in Studies 2–4, sample sizes and demographics not specified in the abstract.

### 10. Adding a social network to an eating-disorder app didn't help teens — and unguided self-help worked about as well as coaching

**Journal:** *International Journal of Eating Disorders* | **Published:** August 2026 (epub April 16, 2026)
**PMID:** 41992076 | **DOI:** 10.1002/eat.70105
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 6/10 — a randomized three-arm test of a widely assumed "peer support helps" premise, though a pilot without a no-treatment arm.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)

**The study:** 148 teens with or at high risk for eating disorders, recruited via social media and an online screen, were randomized to app-based self-help, guided self-help with a coach, or guided self-help plus a peer social networking component, with follow-up to 6 months. There were no significant between-group differences on global eating disorder psychopathology; all groups improved substantially (d = −0.93 from baseline to 6 months). Binge eating fell in all arms, but fastest in the guided self-help arm — the one *without* the social network.

**Why it matters:** Peer-networking features are routinely bolted onto youth mental health apps on the assumption they boost engagement and outcomes; this pilot finds no benefit, and hints the simpler product may be better for binge eating.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the null that matters commercially: the social feature didn't add anything. Open on a teen using a stripped-down app alone at midnight. Call Patricia Cavazos-Rehg or Ellen Fitzsimmons-Craft at Washington University in St. Louis, and press on why peer contact might be neutral or counterproductive in this population.
- **Wider angle:** A digital-health business piece for a specialist or trade outlet on the "engagement feature" arms race in youth mental health apps, and how rarely those features are tested against a plain-vanilla control.

**Caveats:** Pilot RCT, small N (148) for a three-arm design, no inactive control so improvement over time cannot be attributed to the apps, several authors declare consultancy relationships with digital health companies (Woebot, Kooth, Google Health, Rissana, PredictView), recruitment via social media may skew the sample.

### 11. Four in five adolescents who self-harm or restrict say they sometimes do it to feel *worse*

**Journal:** *International Journal of Eating Disorders* | **Published:** August 2026 (epub April 20, 2026)
**PMID:** 42010304 | **DOI:** 10.1002/eat.70111
**Novelty:** Overturns prior research
**NS fit:** 6/10 — directly challenges a foundational assumption in the emotion-regulation model of self-harm, but the sample is small and online-recruited.
**Media check:** Not verified — SerpAPI lookup failed

**The study:** 137 adolescents (mean age 15.1, 75% female), recruited online via social media into a longitudinal study, reported on the functions of restrictive eating, binge eating, purging and non-suicidal self-injury. The field has assumed these behaviors are pro-hedonic — done to reduce negative feeling. Instead, 81% endorsed at least one *contra*-hedonic function: doing it to increase or sustain negative affect, or to dampen positive affect. Contra-hedonic motives were strongest for non-suicidal self-injury and consistently tied to a desire to self-punish.

**Why it matters:** If a large share of self-destructive behavior is aimed at feeling worse rather than better, treatments built entirely around teaching alternative distress-reduction skills are targeting the wrong function for many teens.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead on the inversion of a textbook assumption: the goal isn't relief, it's punishment. Open on the survey item that adolescents kept endorsing. Call Shirley Wang at Yale. Frame carefully — this is a description of motives, not a treatment claim, and needs Samaritans-style signposting.
- **Wider angle:** A clinical-practice feature for a psychology or medical outlet on functional analysis in adolescent mental health — how therapies get built on an assumed function, and what happens to dialectical behavior therapy's skills modules if self-punishment is the real driver.

**Caveats:** Small N (137), predominantly White and female sample recruited via social media (may not generalize), cross-sectional self-report of motives within a larger study, secondary analysis, no clinical verification of behaviors.

### 12. Identifying with an online group makes you more likely to start measuring yourself against others

**Journal:** *Acta Psychologica* | **Published:** September 2026 (epub August 3, 2026)
**PMID:** 42546360 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107491
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 6/10 — five-country longitudinal panel with within-person analysis; a rare piece of evidence on *why* people compare, though self-reported.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Using longitudinal survey data from adults aged 18–75 in Finland (N=1,541), France (N=1,561), Germany (N=1,529), Ireland (N=1,112) and Poland (N=1,533), researchers tracked whether identifying with online groups and networks predicted a person's orientation toward comparing their abilities with others. It did — both between and within individuals over time — and the relationship was partly mediated by the motive to verify one's own self-concept.

**Why it matters:** Social comparison is usually treated as a fixed personality trait or as a passive consequence of exposure to feeds; this suggests it is actively cultivated by belonging to online groups, which reframes the target for any "healthy social media" intervention.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The counterintuitive bit is that belonging — usually cast as the protective factor — is what ramps up comparison. Open on someone joining a running or parenting group online for support and finding themselves ranked. Call Atte Oksanen's group at Tampere University, which runs the panel.
- **Wider angle:** A social-psychology feature for a longer-form outlet revisiting Festinger's 1954 comparison theory at 70 — and how digital group life has made a supposedly egocentric process into a fundamentally social one.

**Caveats:** All self-reported, observational (cannot establish causation), European adult samples only, effect sizes not given in the abstract, mediation inferred from panel data rather than experiment.

### 13. The strongest predictor of compulsive short-video use isn't the pull of the videos — it's overestimating your own self-control

**Journal:** *Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking* | **Published:** August 17, 2026 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42608804 | **DOI:** 10.1177/21522715261479173
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a nice metacognitive twist on the flow literature, weakened by a cross-sectional self-report design.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Survey data from 842 short-form video users were analyzed with partial least squares structural equation modeling, treating "flow" not as the classic optimal experience but as a passive state with maladaptive components (affective reliance, loss of self-reflection, telepresence). Overestimation of one's own self-control emerged as the strongest driver of compulsive use — exceeding the total effect of flow itself — and flow raised compulsive use partly by inflating that overconfidence.

**Why it matters:** It relocates the problem from the content to a metacognitive illusion: the belief that you could stop whenever you wanted is itself what keeps you scrolling.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead on "I can stop any time" as the mechanism, not the excuse. Open with a reader mentally estimating how long they've been on TikTok, then the gap between estimate and reality. The reframing of flow — psychology's poster child for the good life — as something that can be hollow and passive is the second hook. Call Chei Sian Lee at Nanyang Technological University.
- **Wider angle:** A design-critique piece for a technology outlet on whether screen-time dashboards and app timers work at all if the core problem is a confidence illusion rather than ignorance of the numbers.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional (cannot establish causation), all self-reported including the outcome, sample demographics unspecified, latent-variable model heavily dependent on the novel reconceptualization of flow, no behavioral usage data.

### 14. Short-form video use predicts disordered eating four months later — in working nurses, not teenagers

**Journal:** *International Journal of Eating Disorders* | **Published:** August 2026 (epub April 13, 2026)
**PMID:** 41968956 | **DOI:** 10.1002/eat.70108
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — usefully extends media-effects research beyond students to a working adult profession; modest sample with attrition.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (1 news hits)

**The study:** 400 registered female nurses at a hospital in Wuhan, China were surveyed at baseline, with 270 retained at four-month follow-up. Baseline short-form video use predicted disordered eating at follow-up indirectly, through increased body dissatisfaction. **Story angles:**

- **Wider angle:** An occupational-health feature for a nursing or workplace outlet on appearance pressure inside healthcare — a workforce that is majority female, heavily shift-based, and rarely studied as a body-image risk group.

**Caveats:** Single hospital, female-only, two waves with 32.5% attrition, self-reported exposure and outcomes, observational design (cannot establish causation), findings may not generalize outside China.

### 15. One capacity — the ability to get started — sits underneath anxiety, depression, drinking and compulsive social media use

**Journal:** *Behaviour Research and Therapy* | **Published:** August 2026 (epub June 9, 2026)
**PMID:** 42285011 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.brat.2026.105099
**Novelty:** New mechanism
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a large, well-analyzed transdiagnostic study, but cross-sectional self-report and a technical framing that needs work to sell.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** 1,997 community adults completed measures of emotion regulation, dispositional self-regulation, psychological flexibility, mentalization, attachment and childhood maltreatment, alongside symptoms of anxiety, depression, alcohol use disorder and problematic social media use. Bayesian model averaging identified *initiatory* self-control — the ability to start goal-directed behavior, as distinct from inhibiting unwanted behavior — as the only predictor credibly associated with all four outcomes. Emotional abuse and anxious attachment tracked anxiety, depression and problematic social media use; psychological flexibility mattered for the internalizing conditions but not the behavioral ones.

**Why it matters:** The dominant framing of problematic social media use as a failure of restraint may be backwards — the shared deficit looks like difficulty initiating, and that is a different intervention target.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Lead with the distinction most people have never heard: stopping and starting are separate abilities, and it's the starting that's broken. Open on someone who can't put the phone down and equally can't begin the task waiting behind it. Call Tania Moretta at eCampus University.
- **Wider angle:** A conceptual feature for a psychiatry or science-policy outlet on the RDoC project a decade on — whether process-based, dimension-first psychiatry is actually replacing diagnostic categories, and what studies like this one do and don't demonstrate.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional (cannot establish causation), all self-reported including retrospective childhood maltreatment, community sample recruited without stated sampling frame, no clinical diagnoses.

### 16. A four-stage map of how recommender systems and AI companions can escort someone toward violence

**Journal:** *Personality and Social Psychology Review* | **Published:** August 2026 (epub March 23, 2026)
**PMID:** 41869924 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10888683261430089
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — timely and rigorous synthesis with concrete governance proposals, but it is a theoretical review with no new data.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (2 news hits)

**The study:** A synthesis in a leading review journal integrates radicalization process models with research on personality and social risk factors, recommender systems and generative AI into a four-stage framework: Exposure (virality and recommendation create initial attraction to extreme content), Reinforcement (filter bubbles and AI bot swarms manufacture a synthetic consensus), Group Integration (ideologically homogeneous clusters plus AI companions supply belonging and readiness to act), and Violent Extremist Action. The authors specify which known vulnerability factors bite at which stage and propose stage-matched policy measures.

**Why it matters:** It gives regulators and researchers a stage-specific vocabulary, and flags AI companions and bot swarms — not just recommendation — as an underrated part of the pipeline.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** The novel element is "synthetic consensus": bot swarms making a fringe view feel like the majority to one user. Open there rather than on the familiar rabbit-hole imagery. Call Jonas Kunst at BI Norwegian Business School. Be clear this is a framework awaiting empirical test, not evidence that AI is radicalizing people at scale.
- **Wider angle:** A regulatory feature for a policy or technology outlet on whether the EU Digital Services Act and AI Act contain anything that touches the Reinforcement and Group Integration stages, or whether they only regulate Exposure.

**Caveats:** Theoretical review with no new empirical data; the framework is untested as a whole; causal claims about AI's contribution remain inferential; risk of overstating a still-rare outcome.

### 17. Real grooming conversations from court files reveal two very different kinds of offender

**Journal:** *Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking* | **Published:** August 2026 (epub July 9, 2026)
**PMID:** 42427109 | **DOI:** 10.1177/21522715261458454
**Novelty:** First-in-class
**NS fit:** 5/10 — a genuinely new dataset that replaces two-decade-old decoy transcripts, though the analysis is preliminary.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (0 news hits)

**The study:** Almost all research on online child grooming rests on Perverted Justice Foundation transcripts collected nearly 20 years ago, in which the "minors" were adults posing as children. Polish researchers assembled a new corpus of conversations between suspected offenders and real minors, drawn from criminal cases reviewed by Polish courts, and compared two offender profiles — hypersexual and intimacy-seeking — on communication strategy, concealment technique, requests made, and the responses those requests drew from the children.

**Why it matters:** The evidence base underpinning detection tools and prevention advice was built on simulated conversations from a pre-smartphone internet; this is the first serious attempt to check whether it holds with real interactions.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Half a dozen studies in this batch are not new findings about social media so much as new *measurements* of it — and the better the measurement, the more the standard causal story bends. Wearable cameras plus heart-rate logging find teenagers are calmer, not aroused, while scrolling before bed ("Teenagers' hearts slow down when they scroll before bed", PMID 41556268); a random-intercept cross-lagged model in 6,153 students erases the usual "bidirectional" tangle and leaves phone use as the downstream consequence of distress and daytime sleepiness (PMID 42636649); six-times-daily sampling of 858 undergraduates shows feed content moves body image while body image barely steers what people look at, killing the self-selection defense (PMID 42537570); and peer nomination replaces teens' own screen-time guesses entirely (PMID 42552601). Meanwhile the mechanism studies relocate the problem away from "can't stop": overconfidence in one's own self-control beats content pull as a predictor of compulsive short-video use (PMID 42608804), initiatory rather than inhibitory self-control is the shared thread across anxiety, depression, drinking and problematic use (PMID 42285011), and a new "digital coercion" scale reframes distress as entrapment in systems you cannot opt out of (PMID 42585824).

**Why pitch this now:** Legislation on age gates, phone bans and recommender audits is being written on top of a decade of self-reported screen-time research, exactly as the objective and within-person methods arrive to tell us which of those effects survive scrutiny — and which reverse.

**Angle:** A methods-and-evidence feature for an intelligent general reader: follow the instruments (chest cameras, Fitbits, experience-sampling pings, peer nominations, RI-CLPM) and show what each one adds or subtracts from the harm case, ending on what actually holds up — content exposure effects on body image, and the metacognitive illusion of control. Written for parents, teachers and policymakers who have absorbed the headline version and not the corrections.
