### Study 1: Most people have intrusive fears of "going crazy" — and OCD makes them stick
**PMID:** 42800306 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Core numbers check out: N = 154 university students at Memorial University of Newfoundland; 83.1% endorsed at least one psychosis-related intrusion or compulsion; fear of losing control of thoughts/actions most frequently endorsed (50.6%); fear of developing a psychotic illness (18.3%) rated most distressing; 53.2% endorsed replacing "bad thoughts" with better ones; health anxiety and negative beliefs about losing control predicted intrusions over and above OCD severity. The "four in five" gloss is a fair rounding of 83.1%.

**Issue A. Headline and "Why it matters" generalize a student analogue sample to the whole population** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Most people have intrusive fears of 'going crazy'" / "It suggests that fearing you are going mad is close to a universal human experience rather than a rare symptom"
- **Abstract says:** "This preliminary study examined the prevalence and associated factors of fears of psychosis among a university student sample (N = 154)."
- **Problem:** The Caveats correctly note the population "unlikely to generalize beyond young, educated adults," but the headline and the "Why it matters" line contradict that caveat by extending an 83% figure from one Canadian student convenience sample to "most people" and "a universal human experience." No prevalence claim about the general population is supported here.
- **Suggested fix:** "Most students report intrusive fears of 'going crazy' — and OCD symptoms travel with them" / "It suggests that fearing you are going mad may be a common experience among young adults rather than a rare symptom, and hints that what distinguishes a clinical problem is the meaning attached to losing control."

**Issue B. "OCD makes them stick" asserts causation and persistence the study did not measure** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "and OCD makes them stick" / "what turns it into a clinical problem is not the thought itself but what you believe losing control would mean"
- **Abstract says:** "individuals meeting OCD symptom cut-offs were more likely to endorse psychosis-related fears" and health anxiety and negative beliefs about losing control "predicted psychosis-related unwanted intrusive thoughts" in hierarchical regression.
- **Problem:** This is a cross-sectional questionnaire study with OCD defined by cut-off scores. It shows association, not that OCD causes the thoughts to persist, and nothing about persistence over time was measured. "Turns it into" is likewise causal.
- **Suggested fix:** "...and people with OCD symptoms report them more often" / "...and that beliefs about what losing control would mean are associated with how strongly the fears are endorsed."

**Issue C. Researcher first name not verifiable from the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call Emily Fawcett's group at Memorial University"
- **Abstract says:** "Fawcett EJ ... Electronic address: efawcett@mun.ca" (note two Fawcetts on the byline: Fawcett JM and Fawcett EJ)
- **Problem:** The given name is an inference. Unverifiable from abstract only, and there are two Fawcetts, so a misattribution risk exists.
- **Suggested fix:** "Call senior author E. J. Fawcett at Memorial University (confirm given name before pitching)."

*NS fit:* 6/10 is at the top of what a student analogue sample with self-report measures supports; 5/10 would be safer, and the "first prevalence estimate" claim in the fit note is unverifiable from the abstract (the authors say only "preliminary").

### Study 2: People in early psychosis say romance is central to recovery — their clinics almost never ask
**PMID:** 41891676 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 20, early psychosis intervention programs in Ontario, reflexive thematic analysis, Queen's University affiliation, journal and dates all match. Framing is appropriately descriptive and the Caveats are accurate.

**Issue A. "Goal of recovery" slightly recasts the authors' wording** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Participants described intimacy, romance and sexuality as both a goal of recovery and a yardstick for measuring it"
- **Abstract says:** "Many people experiencing early psychosis see romance, intimacy, and sexuality as facilitators and indicators of recovery"
- **Problem:** "Facilitator" means a contributor to recovery, not a goal of it; the "future goals" sub-theme offers partial support, but the headline construct in the abstract is facilitator/indicator.
- **Suggested fix:** "Participants described intimacy, romance and sexuality as both something that helps recovery along and a yardstick for measuring it."

**Issue B. Unsourced participant quotation and "warnings"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Open on a participant describing being told relationships were something to postpone until they were 'stable'." / "when they did, it often came with stipulations or warnings"
- **Abstract says:** "how intimacy, romance, and sexuality are addressed in mental healthcare (e.g., barriers, stipulations, strategies for approaching these topics)"
- **Problem:** "Stipulations" is in the abstract; "warnings" is not. The quoted "stable" scenario appears nowhere in the abstract and reads as if it came from the paper. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "...and when they did, it often came with stipulations. Pitch angle: open on the theme of clinicians attaching conditions to relationships — ask the authors for an illustrative participant quote from the full text."

*NS fit:* 6/10 is defensible for a well-framed qualitative study, given the digest explicitly says it "can only describe, not quantify."

### Study 3: Women's verbal memory advantage vanishes after a first episode of psychosis
**PMID:** 42843653 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 486 across FEP patients, unaffected siblings and controls; RAVLT; rs9896243 in NSF; female C-allele carriers in control and sibling groups outscored males on immediate, short-delay and long-delay recall; advantage absent in FEP; male FEP C-carriers showed the most pronounced recognition deficits. All accurate. Caveats correctly capture the hypothesis-generating functional annotation.

**Issue A. Headline drops the genotype condition and implies a temporal change** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Women's verbal memory advantage vanishes after a first episode of psychosis"
- **Abstract says:** "Female C-allele carriers in the control and sibling groups showed significantly higher scores... However, this female advantage was not observed in FEP patients."
- **Problem:** Two problems. The advantage reported is specific to C-allele carriers, not women generally; and the design is cross-sectional group comparison, so nothing was observed to change "after" anything — the advantage was simply absent in a different group of people. The body text is accurate, so the headline contradicts it.
- **Suggested fix:** "A memory-linked gene variant favours women — but not women with first-episode psychosis"

**Issue B. "Disappeared entirely" overstates a null result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "In patients with first-episode psychosis, that female advantage disappeared entirely"
- **Abstract says:** "this female advantage was not observed in FEP patients"
- **Problem:** "Not observed" means the sex difference did not reach significance in that group; "disappeared entirely" asserts a zero effect, which a non-significant finding does not establish.
- **Suggested fix:** "In patients with first-episode psychosis, that female advantage was not seen."

**Issue C. "New mechanism" novelty label overstates what was shown** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** New mechanism"
- **Abstract says:** Functional annotations "are exploratory and should be considered hypothesis-generating."
- **Problem:** This is a candidate-SNP association with an interaction term, plus in-silico annotation. No mechanism was demonstrated.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** Interaction finding / incremental"

*NS fit:* 5/10 is appropriate.

### Study 4: Genetic evidence points to clozapine itself driving diabetes risk
**PMID:** 42472444 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Adelaide University team, Mendelian randomization, higher plasma clozapine and clozapine-norclozapine ratio associated with higher T2D risk and blood pressure, 28 colocalizing markers, 16 with cardiometabolic associations including haematological markers and excretory traits — all accurate. The pitch angle's explicit instruction to avoid implying dose changes is good practice and matches the authors' own caution.

**Issue A. Headline asserts causation the authors explicitly disclaim** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Genetic evidence points to clozapine itself driving diabetes risk"
- **Abstract says:** "These findings are hypothesis-generating and do not, in the absence of prospective clinical validation, establish causal relationships between clozapine and the identified cardiometabolic traits."
- **Problem:** "Driving" is causal language applied to a finding whose authors state in the conclusions that causation is not established. The body and Caveats hedge correctly, so the headline contradicts the rest of the entry — and a headline is what an editor reads first.
- **Suggested fix:** "Genetic study strengthens the case that clozapine metabolism is tied to diabetes risk"

**Issue B. Ancestry limitation not supported by the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "relies on existing GWAS summary data whose ancestry composition limits generalizability"
- **Abstract says:** Nothing about data sources, cohorts or ancestry composition.
- **Problem:** Plausible for MR work, but unverifiable from abstract only. Stating it as fact in the Caveats invites a correction if the underlying GWAS are more diverse than assumed.
- **Suggested fix:** "relies on genetic summary data; check the source cohorts' ancestry composition before pitching generalizability claims."

*NS fit:* 5/10 is well judged for a hypothesis-generating pipeline. Note the abstract carries no funding or competing-interest statement, so nothing to flag as omitted.

### Study 5: There may be no single recipe for recovering from schizophrenia
**PMID:** 42734987 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N = 101 with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder; finite mixture regression; three-class solution with one class too small to interpret; one class associated with social functioning, depressive symptoms, positive symptoms and metacognition; the other showing no single dominant predictor despite similar symptom and functioning levels; negative symptoms associated in both larger classes. All accurate, and the Caveats are unusually thorough. Headline is appropriately hedged ("may be").

**Issue A. "Overturns prior research" misstates the contribution** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** "findings regarding the strength and direction of these associations have been inconsistent, suggesting that different association structures may exist within the population... one class... reflect[s] a multidimensional association pattern consistent with previous findings."
- **Problem:** The study reconciles inconsistent prior findings rather than overturning them, and one class explicitly replicates the existing literature.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** Reframes existing findings"

**Issue B. "Whether life feels worth living" glosses a defined construct** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "for whom completely different things determine whether life feels worth living"
- **Abstract says:** "Personal recovery was treated as the dependent variable."
- **Problem:** Personal recovery is a specific measured construct (hope, identity, meaning, empowerment, depending on instrument), not a global judgement about life's worth. "Determine" also imports causation into a cross-sectional association, which the Caveats correctly disclaim.
- **Suggested fix:** "for whom completely different factors are associated with how far they feel they are personally recovering."

*NS fit:* 5/10 is defensible; the digest names the data-hungriness of mixture modelling at N = 101 itself.

### Study 6: Two ways reality can go wrong: the philosopher's case for comparing borderline and schizophrenia
**PMID:** 42853770 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. Framing is consistent with the abstract. "Evanescence of reality," "excess of reality," "ontic gravity," the oblique-versus-direct gaze contrast and the epoché framing all match the source, the entry states plainly that this is conceptual work with no data, the pitch angle instructs the writer to be explicit that it is theory, and the 4/10 fit score is appropriate for a non-empirical paper.
