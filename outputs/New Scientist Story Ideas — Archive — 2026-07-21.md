### 1. Your heartbeat shapes how much social rejection hurts — and this holds true even in older age

**Journal:** *Biological Psychology* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 17
**PMID:** 42468769 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.biopsycho.2026.109348
**Novelty:** Counterintuitive
**NS fit:** 8/10 — Highly counterintuitive (heartbeat timing shapes social pain, and this persists despite age-related declines in body awareness), strong topic fit for NS Mind (emotion, social behaviour, ageing), pre-registered design adds credibility, though self-reported social pain and unspecified N limit the score.
**Media check:** Not widely covered ✓ (SERPAPI verified)

**The study:** In a pre-registered study, younger and older adults played a computerised ball-tossing game (Cyberball) in which social exclusion events were timed to occur either during cardiac systole or diastole, allowing researchers to test whether the phase of the heartbeat modulates the emotional sting of being left out. Despite older adults showing measurably lower heart rate variability and reduced body awareness, both age groups reported less social pain when exclusion happened during systole than during diastole — replicating the effect previously seen only in younger adults. The study did not report a combined N in the abstract.

**Why it matters:** The result suggests that the body's internal signalling system — specifically the baroreceptor signals sent during each heartbeat — continues to buffer emotional pain well into older adulthood, even as other markers of interoception decline with age.

**Story angles:**
- **Pitch angle:** Proposed headline — "The moment your heart contracts determines how much being left out hurts" Social rejection stings less when it lands at a specific moment in your heartbeat cycle — and remarkably, this biological buffer works even in old age. In a pre-registered experiment, people playing a virtual ball-tossing game felt less hurt by being excluded when the snub coincided with their heart's systolic phase than its diastolic phase. The finding replicates earlier work — but the surprise is that older adults showed exactly the same effect, even though their interoceptive abilities had measurably declined with age. It raises a striking possibility: that the heart's moment-to-moment signalling acts as an emotional shock absorber that resists the ageing process. At a time when loneliness in older adults is a public-health crisis, understanding the biology of social pain feels urgent. Why it fits: NS Mind readers are fascinated by the hidden ways the body shapes emotion; a study showing that the cardiac cycle modulates the pain of social exclusion — and does so across the adult lifespan — hits the sweet spot of counterintuitive science with real human relevance.
