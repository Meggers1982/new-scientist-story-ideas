### Study 1: Immigrants start later life less depressed than natives — then overtake them by their mid-60s
**PMID:** 42851008 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. Sample (HRS 1992–2020, 28 years), the mid-60s crossover, the "holds across ethnoracial and gender groups" claim, the null role of childhood environments and the ~80% attributable to joblessness/financial insecurity/discrimination/reduced social support/neighborhood alienation all match the abstract. The "expiry date in the mind that doesn't match the one in the body" line is supported by the abstract's statement that mental and physical health erosion "appears unrelated." Author/affiliation (Zheng H, University of Toronto) correct. Framing is associational throughout ("accounted for," "explained"), and the Caveats name the observational design. NS fit 8/10 is defensible for a 28-year nationally representative panel, despite the self-reported depression scale.

### Study 2: Ukrainians who fled abroad report worse PTSD than those who stayed in the war zone
**PMID:** 42411615 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. NS fit score rests on cross-sectional self-report** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — a genuinely unexpected result from a very large sample with careful matching; cross-sectional design and a small abroad group hold it back."
- **Abstract says:** "A cross-sectional propensity score-matched design was used among 7,065 war-affected participants; 287 lived abroad... One-to-one matching yielded 235 pairs."
- **Problem:** The headline comparison rests on 235 matched pairs of self-reported (PCL-5, self-report moral injury and growth scales) data collected at a single time point, with no way to rule out reverse selection (people with worse symptoms being more likely or able to leave). A 7 implies a finding sturdy enough to carry a feature on its own; the design can only support a well-hedged one.
- **Suggested fix:** "**NS fit:** 6/10 — an unexpected result from a very large, carefully matched sample, but cross-sectional self-report with only 235 matched pairs means selection effects cannot be excluded."

Everything else checks out: 7,065 participants, 287 abroad, matching variables (gender, age, education, war exposure), higher scores on all PTSD clusters and probable PTSD (PCL-5 ≥ 33), elevated depreciation, moral-injury shame and trust, core-belief disruption, similar total post-traumatic growth with "new possibilities" slightly higher abroad. Neumann CS is at the University of North Texas as stated.

### Study 3: Disgust lingers longer than fear after a disturbing experience
**PMID:** 42060450 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Headline and pitch hook state unconditionally what the study found only under one condition** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Disgust lingers longer than fear after a disturbing experience" / "The hook is that revulsion outlasts terror."
- **Abstract says:** Title: "Disgust reactions to a remembered trauma analogue are similarly persistent to fear reactions." Results: "Disgust and fear faded similarly when the intensity of emotional reactions was maximized at encoding, but when these emotional reactions were matched at an initial moderately intense level, disgust persisted more than fear over 24 hr and 1 week."
- **Problem:** The authors' own framing is "similarly — if not more — persistent." Disgust outlasted fear in only one of two encoding conditions. The digest body states this correctly, but the headline and pitch hook assert the unconditional version, which is the more exciting result a reader would assume rather than the one obtained. An editor reading only the headline would commission the wrong story.
- **Suggested fix:** "Disgust can outlast fear after a disturbing experience — when the two start out equally strong" / "The hook is that at matched intensity, revulsion outlasted terror."

**Issue B. Clinical implication drawn from an analogue study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "if disgust is the stickier emotion, exposure-based therapies may be targeting the wrong reaction for some patients."
- **Abstract says:** "highlighting disgust as a trauma-relevant emotion."
- **Problem:** The participants viewed negative images, not real trauma, and none were patients. The abstract makes no treatment claim. The conditional hedge ("may") helps, but the sentence still invites a treatment-implication read that the design cannot bear.
- **Suggested fix:** "if disgust is the stickier emotion in at least some conditions, that raises a question — currently untested in patients — about whether fear-focused exposure therapy addresses the full reaction."

**Issue C. NS fit score** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 7/10 — three experiments including a replication at a longer delay, testing an emotion that trauma science has largely ignored; limited by the analogue design."
- **Problem:** Three lab analogue experiments on ~110 participants each with self-rated emotion as the sole outcome, and a result that only appears in one encoding condition. A 7 overstates how much weight this can carry, particularly as the headline claim is conditional.
- **Suggested fix:** "**NS fit:** 6/10 — three controlled experiments including a one-week replication, but a trauma analogue with self-rated outcomes and a condition-dependent effect."

Ns (111, 112, 118), delays, and the Takarangi lab at Flinders University are correct.

### Study 4: Public distress over Israel's hostages fell for six months, then froze for a year
**PMID:** 42828449 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Claim that participants had no personal connection to the hostages** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "explained 34–44% of the variance in general psychological distress at every wave, in people with no personal connection to the hostages."
- **Abstract says:** "Israeli adults were surveyed at three (T1; N = 740), six (T2; N = 336), and eighteen (T3; N = 254) months post-kidnapping" and concludes the consequences extend "far beyond directly affected families."
- **Problem:** The abstract describes a general public sample; it never states participants were screened for having no personal connection to a hostage. In a country of Israel's size many respondents would have indirect ties. The digest asserts a sample characteristic that is not in the abstract and that carries the whole "collective, not personal" argument. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "explained 34–44% of the variance in general psychological distress across waves, in a general sample of Israeli adults rather than hostages' families."

**Issue B. Pitch angle contrasts ambiguity against an exposure variable the abstract does not report** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the thing predicting it was not exposure to violence but not knowing."
- **Abstract says:** "ALI+-SD and BAS each significantly predicted DASS beyond time effects, with multilevel mediation indicating that ALI+-SD mediated the BAS-DASS association."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports no measure of personal exposure to violence and no comparison showing ambiguity outperformed it. The pitch presents a head-to-head result that the abstract does not contain.
- **Suggested fix:** "the things predicting it were separation distress and boundary ambiguity — not knowing — which each predicted distress over and above the passage of time."

**Issue C. Variance figures come from the longitudinal subsample** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Together, these two ambiguity measures explained 34–44% of the variance in general psychological distress at every wave"
- **Abstract says:** "In the longitudinal sample, ALI+-SD and BAS together explained 44% (T1), 34% (T2), and 41% (T3) of the variance in general distress."
- **Problem:** The 34–44% range applies to the N = 220 subsample who completed all three waves, not to each full cross-sectional wave. "At every wave" blurs this.
- **Suggested fix:** "Together, these two measures explained 34–44% of the variance in general distress at each wave among the 220 people who completed all three."

### Study 5: Parents who were sexually abused as children have children reporting very different kinds of maltreatment
**PMID:** 42842484 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Explicit null for offspring sexual abuse is not stated in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "but not offspring sexual abuse specifically."
- **Abstract says:** "Parental (G1) childhood sexual abuse emerged as a significant risk factor for offspring (G2) trauma across multiple domains, including emotional abuse, emotional neglect, physical abuse, and overall CTQ-SF scores... Findings support heterotypic intergenerational continuity."
- **Problem:** Sexual abuse is absent from the list of affected offspring domains and "heterotypic" continuity is the stated conclusion, so the inference is reasonable — but the abstract never reports a tested null for offspring sexual abuse. Since this null is the digest's entire novelty claim ("the 'cycle of abuse' isn't a cycle of the same abuse"), it needs verification in the full text before pitching.
- **Suggested fix:** "and sexual abuse was notably absent from the affected offspring domains — the authors describe the pattern as 'heterotypic' continuity (confirm the specific null in full text)."

**Issue B. Mechanism stated more firmly than the abstract allows** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "likely via attachment and relational pathways"
- **Abstract says:** "transmission may operate through internalized, relational, and contextual pathways rather than through direct replication"; the plain-language summary flags "disrupted attachment" as a target for intervention.
- **Problem:** "Likely" upgrades the authors' "may," and no pathway was measured in this study.
- **Suggested fix:** "possibly via internalized, relational and contextual pathways, though none were measured here"

N (68 G1, 111 G2), the CTQ-SF, the 5.7-fold odds figure, and affiliations (Hock, Duke Kunshan; Galler, Barbados Nutrition Study) are all correct, and the Caveats appropriately flag the small N and retrospective reporting.

### Study 6: A VR walk through an ordinary street can pick out soldiers with hidden PTSD symptoms
**PMID:** 42826675 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Street" is an invented scene detail** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A VR walk through an ordinary street" / "soldiers who won't tick boxes on a mental health form will walk through a virtual street. Open on what the headset actually shows — a mundane scene"
- **Abstract says:** "Volunteers navigated an everyday-life virtual reality environment designed to highlight multisensory integration impairments associated with trauma-related fear conditioning."
- **Problem:** The abstract specifies only an "everyday-life" environment. A street is a plausible guess but is not in the source, and the pitch builds a scene-setting opener on it. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "A VR walk through an everyday scene" / "check with the authors what the environment actually depicts before writing the opener."

Everything else verifies: 84 active-duty participants, CAPS-5 four-group classification, HRV/skin conductance/respiration/temperature/EEG/behavior, 76–88% accuracy and AUC 0.73–0.88 for moderate sub-PTSD vs no-PTSD, HRV and skin conductance most discriminant, Trousselard at IRBA. The declared competing interest (lead author is president of a company that received part of the funding) is correctly carried into Caveats, and the pitch explicitly says "validation, not a deployable test."

### Study 7: Treating Inuit silence as a symptom may be the mistake
**PMID:** 42578805 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Novelty label "Overturns prior research" is too strong for a conceptual case study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**Novelty:** Overturns prior research"
- **Abstract says:** "I draw on pandemic-era public communication and professional narratives collected at a large health event in Greenland to critically reflect on cultures of silence and confession... It argues that such models may misrecognize culturally grounded practices of quiet."
- **Problem:** This is an interpretive, argument-based contribution to debate, not an empirical refutation of prior findings. "Overturns prior research" implies data that supersede earlier data. The digest's own NS fit line acknowledges this, so the label contradicts the body.
- **Suggested fix:** "**Novelty:** Challenges a dominant framework (conceptual)"

**Issue B. Overgeneralization in "Why it matters"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The assumption that talking about trauma is universally healing underpins most of global mental health practice"
- **Abstract says:** "hegemonic knowledge frameworks, which prioritize disclosure-centred models of care, shape contemporary mental health governance in Greenland."
- **Problem:** The abstract's claim is about Greenlandic mental health governance and about debates in transcultural psychiatry, not about "most of global mental health practice."
- **Suggested fix:** "Disclosure-centred models dominate how mental health care is delivered and governed in settings like Greenland — and here they are argued to misread a functioning cultural practice as a deficit."

The three recurring patterns, the data source (pandemic-era public communication and professional narratives at a large health event in Greenland), the author and affiliation (Schwalbe, University of Copenhagen), and the Caveats are all accurate.

### Study 8: For Nova festival survivors, the institutional response became a second injury
**PMID:** 42847151 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Peer communities upgraded from "a key source" to "the dominant source," with an added comparison to formal services** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "survivor-led peer communities — not formal services — were the dominant source of recovery capital."
- **Abstract says:** "Institutional betrayal emerged as an ongoing secondary injury, whereas survivor-led peer communities served as a key source of recovery capital."
- **Problem:** "A key source" becomes "the dominant source," and the parenthetical "not formal services" asserts a direct comparison that a 15-person reflexive thematic analysis does not quantify. The wider angle then generalizes to "survivor networks outperforming official support structures," which this study cannot establish.
- **Suggested fix:** "survivor-led peer communities emerged as a key source of recovery capital, while institutional betrayal functioned as an ongoing secondary injury."

N = 15, the ~18-month interval, reflexive thematic analysis, the three themes, and Elisha's affiliation (Max Stern Yezreel Valley College) are all correct, and the Caveats name the small N, self-selection and retrospective design.

### Study 11: Being a sad child — not an abused one — tracks with worse romantic relationships in your twenties
**PMID:** 42852960 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Headline implies a comparison with abuse that the study did not make** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Being a sad child — not an abused one — tracks with worse romantic relationships in your twenties"
- **Abstract says:** "most studies have focused on trauma or abuse... research examining childhood sadness in relation to romantic relationship satisfaction (RRS) in emerging adulthood remains limited."
- **Problem:** The study measured childhood sadness, emotion dysregulation, adult wellbeing and RRS. It did not measure abuse and did not compare the two. The em-dash construction reads as "sadness matters, abuse doesn't," which reverses nothing in the data but asserts a tested contrast that does not exist. The "Why it matters" paragraph gets this right ("may also leave a relational trace"), so the headline contradicts the body.
- **Suggested fix:** "Ordinary childhood sadness — not just abuse — tracks with worse romantic relationships in your twenties"

N = 217 (82% women, mean age 21.13), six qualitative interviewees, the wellbeing-mediated association, the non-independent role of emotion dysregulation and the exploratory serial pattern are all reported correctly, as is Moran's affiliation (Royal Holloway). The Caveats appropriately mark the mediation as exploratory.

### Study 12: Transgender women report rarely seeking formal help after violence — and rarely getting it when they do
**PMID:** 42848018 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Headline turns "mixed" attainment into "rarely getting it"** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Transgender women report rarely seeking formal help after violence — and rarely getting it when they do"
- **Abstract says:** "Quantitative findings revealed low levels of formal help-seeking for victimization-related needs and mixed reports of experiences attaining help."
- **Problem:** "Low" help-seeking is accurate; "rarely getting it" is not — the abstract reports *mixed* attainment experiences, meaning some participants did obtain help. The digest's own body text says "experiences of actually obtaining help were mixed," so the headline contradicts the entry's own summary and would mislead an editor about the second half of the finding.
- **Suggested fix:** "Transgender women rarely seek formal help after violence — and report mixed experiences when they do"

**Issue B. Informal networks upgraded from "critical" to "dominant"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "chosen families and community groups emerged as the dominant source of support."
- **Abstract says:** "Informal networks, including chosen families and community groups, emerged as critical sources of support."
- **Problem:** "Critical sources" indicates importance; "the dominant source" implies a ranking against formal services that the abstract does not report.
- **Suggested fix:** "chosen families and community groups emerged as critical sources of support."

Sample details (103 trans women aged 18–35 in Milwaukee and Chicago; 25 in-depth interviews), the life history calendar method, the barriers listed, and Hereth's affiliation (University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee) are correct.

### Study 13: The people sent instead of police are burning out as "the face of a broken system"
**PMID:** 41940892 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Pitch treats the sample size as the size of the workforce and adds an unsupported descriptor** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The surprise is who is carrying the reform: a few hundred underpaid workers arriving at the worst moment of someone's life with no clear mandate."
- **Abstract says:** "this qualitative study draws on first-hand perspectives on the challenges of MC work expressed by a national sample of MC workers (n = 364)" and lists challenges including "resources" and "the workplace and workforce."
- **Problem:** Two issues in one sentence. "A few hundred" reads as the national mobile crisis workforce when 364 is simply the study's sample. And "underpaid" is not a term the abstract uses — the resource and workforce domains are described generically.
- **Suggested fix:** "The surprise is who is carrying the reform: mobile crisis workers arriving at the worst moment of someone's life with thin resources and no clear mandate — 364 of whom described the job in this study."

The five challenge domains, the compounding role of structural characteristics, the authors' recommendations (trauma-informed work environment, clarified agency policies, training standards, role clarity), Gupta's affiliation (University of Chicago) and the publication details all check out, and the Caveats correctly flag the qualitative, self-selected, US-only design and the absence of client outcome data.

### Study 14: Sleep may be the bridge between childhood trauma and emotional numbness in depression
**PMID:** 42785231 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. N = 269 depressed patients, CTQ/PSQI/SHAPS/TAS-20, serial mediation (PROCESS Model 6), indirect paths accounting for 75.38% of the total association, non-significant direct path — all correct. The digest consistently uses "statistically accounted for" rather than causal language, the "Why it matters" explicitly states that only longitudinal or intervention work could establish whether improving sleep moves anything, and the pitch angle says "No clinical recommendation from these data" — which is exactly right given the abstract's own cautious "Clinicians may consider jointly assessing these symptoms." Li Z is at Guangzhou Medical University as stated. NS fit 4/10 is well calibrated.

### Study 15: Shame, not avoidance itself, may be what links trauma to suicidal thoughts
**PMID:** 42815005 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. N = 185 trauma-exposed community adults, 76.76% female (rounded to 77%), mean age 35.58 (rounded to 36), significant indirect effects of experiential avoidance on both suicidality and PTSD severity through trauma-related shame, controlling for trauma load, age and biological sex. Albanese is at Texas A&M as stated. The headline hedge ("may be"), the "pending prospective evidence" qualifier and the pitch instruction to "state plainly that the design cannot establish sequence" all match the abstract's call for future prospective work. NS fit 4/10 is appropriate.

**Cross-cutting note for the editor:** four of the five Moderate issues (Studies 3, 11, 12, and the pitch line in 4) follow the same pattern — the body text summarizes the abstract correctly, but the headline or pitch hook asserts a stronger, cleaner version of the finding.
