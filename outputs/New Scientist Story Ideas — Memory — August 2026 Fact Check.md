### Study 1: Semaglutide is linked to fewer cognitive complaints in people with psychiatric illness
**PMID:** 42624612 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Cohort description implies everyone started a drug** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "researchers compared 13,007 adults with a psychiatric diagnosis who started an antidiabetic drug"
- **Abstract says:** "Semaglutide initiation was compared against four comparator strategies including sitagliptin, empagliflozin, glipizide or no antidiabetic treatment."
- **Problem:** One of the four comparator strategies is *no* antidiabetic treatment, so the cohort cannot be described as uniformly drug initiators.
- **Suggested fix:** "researchers compared 13,007 adults with a psychiatric diagnosis across four treatment strategies — semaglutide (n=1261), sitagliptin, empagliflozin, glipizide or no antidiabetic treatment"

**Issue B. Omits the null comparison with sitagliptin** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "clinician-rated cognitive signs/symptoms … were lower with semaglutide than with no antidiabetic treatment (mean ratio 0.75), glipizide or empagliflozin"
- **Abstract says:** "lower but not significantly different from sitagliptin (12.52; MR, 0.89 (0.77–1.02); p=0.50)"
- **Problem:** Three of four comparisons were significant and one — against the other incretin-adjacent agent — was not. Omitting it makes the signal look more uniform than it is, and the sitagliptin null is the comparison most relevant to whether this is GLP-1-specific.
- **Suggested fix:** Add: "…but not significantly different from sitagliptin (MR 0.89, p=0.50)."

**Issue C. Major-depression finding recast as a stronger effect** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "with the strongest signal in memory and in people with major depression"
- **Abstract says:** "Similar effect sizes were seen across disorders but were only consistently significant in major depression."
- **Problem:** Effect sizes were comparable across diagnoses; major depression stood out only in statistical significance (plausibly a power artefact of the larger subgroup). "Strongest signal in people with major depression" implies a differential biological effect the study did not find.
- **Suggested fix:** "with the strongest signal in memory; effect sizes were similar across diagnoses but reached significance consistently only in major depression, likely reflecting subgroup size."

*NS fit 7/10 is defensible — very large cohort, clinician-rated (not self-reported) outcome, observational status clearly flagged. Industry conflicts are correctly carried into Caveats.*

### Study 2: Sleep sharpens memory for who told you what
**PMID:** 42492165 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Word-reduction effect attributed to both experiments** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Sleepers showed better item recognition *and* better source memory … and used fewer words to describe previously discussed items"
- **Abstract says:** "The Sleep group also used fewer words to describe old vs. new items in Experiment 2"
- **Problem:** The recognition/source-memory effect held across Experiments 1 and 2; the communicative-efficiency effect is reported only for the face-to-face Experiment 2. As written it reads as a general finding.
- **Suggested fix:** "…and, in the face-to-face experiment only, used fewer words to describe previously discussed items."

*NS fit 7/10 is defensible: three experiments, an experimental sleep/wake manipulation, and the null mechanism result is honestly flagged in both the score rationale and Caveats.*

### Study 3: Eye-movement therapy works even when you are not consciously thinking about the memory
**PMID:** 42531952 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline asserts a clinical claim the Caveats disown** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** Headline: "Eye-movement therapy works even when you are not consciously thinking about the memory"
- **Abstract says:** Participants "acquired conditioned fear to snake/spider images"; findings "suggest a promising noninvasive strategy" — i.e. an analogue paradigm in non-clinical volunteers.
- **Problem:** The Caveats correctly state "no clinical claim can be made", but the headline states flatly that a therapy "works". Nothing in this study tested therapy in patients; it tested a fear-conditioning analogue.
- **Suggested fix:** "Eye movements dampened lab-induced fear even when the cue was shown below awareness"

**Issue B. NS fit score** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "NS fit: 7/10"
- **Problem:** The headline-grabbing arm (subliminal re-exposure) rests on self-reported intrusive thoughts over a week, with a *null* on the objective physiological measure. That is thinner evidence than a 7 implies for a claim about trauma therapy.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 6/10, with the rationale noting the objective outcome was null in the subliminal condition.

### Study 4: Wait 27 seconds and your short-term memory starts inventing words
**PMID:** 42277491 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline and "Why it matters" locate onset later than the data do** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** Headline: "Wait 27 seconds and your short-term memory starts inventing words that were never there"; "this places its onset within the first half-minute"
- **Abstract says:** "it can be observed even with no delay and no intervening distraction between study and test"
- **Problem:** The digest's own body text says the illusion appeared with no delay at all, which contradicts the headline's implication that 27 seconds is the trigger point. The delay effect is about *growth* in false recognition, not onset.
- **Suggested fix:** Headline: "Your memory invents words within seconds — and warning people doesn't stop it." Why it matters: "false recognition is present immediately and grows over the first half-minute."

*Sample size and true/false direction are correctly reported; non-preregistration is confirmed in the abstract's declarations.*

### Study 5: To remember to do something later, physically rehearse it
**PMID:** 42627881 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Null age interaction reported as a per-group effect** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Enactment beat both other conditions across every age group"
- **Abstract says:** "participants in the EE condition outperformed those in both the EFT and standard encoding conditions, with no evidence that this benefit differed by age group"
- **Problem:** A significant main effect plus a non-significant interaction is not the same as a demonstrated benefit within each of the four age groups.
- **Suggested fix:** "Enactment beat both other conditions overall, with no evidence the benefit differed by age."

**Issue B. Age range in pitch not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a crude physical run-through worked, from age 8 to 60"
- **Abstract says:** Group labels only — children, adolescents, young adults, middle-aged adults; no ages given.
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only, and stated as fact.
- **Suggested fix:** "…worked from childhood to middle age (confirm exact age bands from full text)."

### Study 6: Taking notes you expect to keep makes you encode less deeply
**PMID:** 42622798 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Pitch overstates the encoding effect** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Your phone isn't just holding your memories — it's stopping them forming."
- **Abstract says:** "performance in the unexpected loss of access offloading conditions was poorer than performance in the shallow encoding conditions"
- **Problem:** Recall was reduced relative to a shallow-processing benchmark, not abolished. "Stopping them forming" is hype beyond a graded recall difference in a word-list task.
- **Suggested fix:** "Your phone isn't just holding your memories — it's thinning out how well they form."

*Experiment structure (3 encoding experiments + 1 remember/know experiment) and the remember-judgement result are reported accurately.*

### Study 7: Eye-movement therapy dampens shame, anger and sadness memories
**PMID:** 42622123 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Therapy" in the headline for an online analogue task** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** Headline: "Eye-movement therapy dampens shame, anger and sadness memories, not just fear"
- **Abstract says:** "the present online proof-of-principle study"; outcomes were self-rated vividness, unpleasantness and accessibility of everyday autobiographical memories.
- **Problem:** Caveats flag this, but the headline still frames a single-session online dual-task manipulation as therapy delivered to trauma memories.
- **Suggested fix:** "The eye-movement trick in EMDR dulls shame, anger and sadness memories too — at least in the lab"

**Issue B. Novelty label and strength of conclusion** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Novelty: Overturns prior research"; "this suggests the dual-task mechanism is emotion-general"
- **Abstract says:** "These findings tentatively suggest that dual-task interventions can be applied to emotionally diverse autobiographical memories."
- **Problem:** A non-significant moderator is absence of evidence, not evidence of emotion-generality, and the authors hedge with "tentatively". "Overturns prior research" overstates a null moderation result.
- **Suggested fix:** Novelty: "Challenges a prediction"; Why it matters: "this tentatively suggests the dual-task effect is not specific to fear."

### Study 9: Notetaking rescues an overloaded working memory
**PMID:** 42607389 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Error rate framed as load-dependent** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "But people also made more offloading-specific errors at high load"
- **Abstract says:** "Participants offloaded more frequently at higher loads. However, they also made more offloading-related errors by incorrectly copying information or incorrectly reading and/or reporting notes when tested."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not explicitly state that offloading errors *increased with set size* — only that they occurred. The load-dependence is inferred. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "But people also made offloading-specific errors — copying items down wrong, or misreading their own notes at recall (check full text for whether these scaled with load)."

### Study 10: A week of brain stimulation improves both sleep and overnight skill consolidation
**PMID:** 42537590 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Overnight" consolidation not specified in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** Headline: "…improves both sleep and overnight skill consolidation in insomnia"
- **Abstract says:** "offline consolidation of implicit sequence learning", assessed before and after the 7-day intervention.
- **Problem:** "Offline consolidation" is not stated to be an overnight (sleep-bracketed) interval in the abstract; the digest converts a generic term into a sleep-dependent one, which is also the more headline-friendly reading. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "…improves sleep and offline consolidation of a motor sequence skill in insomnia" (confirm the consolidation interval from full text before pitching the sleep link).

*N=42, randomisation, double-blinding and the sham null are all accurately reported, and the small N is properly flagged in Caveats and the NS score.*

### Study 11: Acting out an instruction helps you remember it
**PMID:** 42622513 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Group-wise effect inferred from a null group difference** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Enactment improved recall in all three groups with no significant difference by visual acuity"
- **Abstract says:** "enactment produced higher recall accuracy than spoken input alone, but no significant differences were found across visual acuity groups"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports an overall enactment advantage and no group differences; it does not report a significant within-group enactment effect for each of the three groups separately (n≈17 per group).
- **Suggested fix:** "Enactment improved recall overall, with no significant differences between the three visual-acuity groups."

*N=51, the three-group structure, and the residual-vision oddity are reported accurately, and the small, uneven groups are flagged.*

### Study 12: You remember cheaters' faces
**PMID:** 42625545 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Used the same photograph" stated absolutely** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Previous studies showing that people have better source memory for individuals who behaved dishonestly used the *same* photograph at learning and test"
- **Abstract says:** "in most of these experiments, the same facial images were used during both the learning and testing phases"
- **Problem:** "Most" becomes "used" — a small hardening of the methodological critique.
- **Suggested fix:** "…most of which used the same photograph at learning and test."

*The main result — source-memory advantage for cheaters in both the changed-perspective and identical-photo conditions — is reported accurately, and the missing N is flagged.*

### Study 13: Handwriting a new word shapes how strongly it lodges in the mental lexicon
**PMID:** 42607453 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal verb in headline for a predictive relationship** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** Headline: "Handwriting a new word by hand shapes how strongly it lodges in the mental lexicon overnight"
- **Abstract says:** "the magnitude of exposure-induced neural modulation during learning predicted the strength of subsequent lexical competition … after overnight consolidation"
- **Problem:** The relationship is predictive/correlational (neural modulation → later lexical competition); handwriting itself was not manipulated against a non-handwriting condition. Caveats note the sleep link is correlational, but the headline still reads causally.
- **Suggested fix:** "How much the brain changes while you handwrite a new word predicts how well it lodges in the lexicon overnight"

*N=63, native Spanish speakers, the four-exposure orthographic asymptote and the protracted motor trajectory are all accurate.*

### Study 15: Daily diaries show mood and PTSD symptoms move together within a day

### Study 17: Harder-looking Chinese characters are easier to pull out of short-term memory
**PMID:** 42612440 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Significant framing issue

**Issue A. Headline reverses the stroke-count result** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** Headline: "Harder-looking Chinese characters are easier to pull out of short-term memory"
- **Abstract says:** "accuracy for simple characters was higher than that for complex characters … simpler characters with fewer strokes are more readily retrievable from STM"
- **Problem:** "Harder-*looking*" reads as visual/graphic complexity — exactly the dimension on which the study found the *opposite* result. The counterintuitive findings concern phonetic regularity and semantic transparency, not visual complexity. The body text gets this right ("Simpler characters were retrieved more accurately, as expected"), so the headline contradicts the entry it sits above.
- **Suggested fix:** "Chinese characters that don't tell you how they sound are easier to hold in short-term memory"

*Experiments 2 and 3 (irregular > regular; non-transparent > transparent) are reported accurately, and the missing sample details are flagged.*

### Study 19: A nine-week course improves how older adults read between the lines
**PMID:** 42629485 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Significant framing issue

**Issue A. Omits two null/partial outcomes on targeted modalities** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Pragmatic comprehension and production improved from pre- to post-training and were maintained at three months, in both linguistic and extralinguistic channels."
- **Abstract says:** "A trend towards enhancement was also observed in paralinguistic ability, although the changes did not reach statistical significance, while no significant change emerged in sensitivity to the context."
- **Problem:** The training explicitly targeted four things — linguistic, extralinguistic, paralinguistic and context sensitivity. Two of the four did not improve significantly, including context sensitivity, which is arguably the closest thing to "reading between the lines" in the headline. Reporting only the two positives makes the intervention look broader in effect than it was.
- **Suggested fix:** "Pragmatic comprehension and production improved pre- to post-training and were maintained at three months in the linguistic and extralinguistic channels; paralinguistic gains were a non-significant trend and sensitivity to context did not change."

*N=24, ages 65–86, nine-week group format, three-month follow-up, stable cognitive/ToM measures and the authors' own "preliminary" label are all accurate. NS fit 4/10 with an explicit "no control group" caveat is appropriately conservative.*

### Study 20: Training working memory with emotional material curbs risky decisions
**PMID:** 42590932 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Hype in the pitch headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** Pitch angle: "Brain training's best shot yet — with an emotional twist."
- **Abstract says:** "These findings highlight eWMT as a promising cognitive-affective intervention"
- **Problem:** "Best shot yet" is a superlative comparative claim across the whole working-memory-training literature that this 30-person single-site trial cannot support — and it sits awkwardly against the digest's own line that "at this sample size it is a hypothesis, not a finding."
- **Suggested fix:** "A small trial gives working memory training an emotional twist — and a rare transfer signal"

*N=30 (15/15), 20 sessions, active feature-matching comparator, one-month follow-up, and the adaptive-strategy null are all accurate; underpowering is flagged in both the NS rationale and Caveats.*

### Study 21: Personalised symptom networks reveal four recurring "shapes" of PTSD
**PMID:** 42625545 → correct PMID **42607016** | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Sample composition and a mislabelled model component** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Fifty-five trauma-exposed people (31 with PTSD) chose personalised items representing the components of Ehlers and Clark's cognitive model — trauma memory, triggers, negative appraisals, sense of current threat, coping strategies."
- **Abstract says:** "Fifty-five participants (31 with PTSD, 24 with clinical-level symptoms) selected personalised items reflecting core model components: negative appraisals, trauma memory, triggers, sense of current threat, and *maladaptive* strategies."
- **Problem:** Two small drifts: the comparison group is not "non-PTSD" but people with clinical-level symptoms (worth stating, as it affects how distinct the clusters can be), and "maladaptive strategies" is a specific model construct that "coping strategies" flattens.
- **Suggested fix:** "Fifty-five trauma-exposed people (31 with PTSD, 24 with clinical-level symptoms) … negative appraisals, trauma memory, triggers, sense of current threat and maladaptive strategies."

**Issue B. "Confirmed" overstates the model test** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Individual networks confirmed the model's central claim that 'sense of current threat' is the hub"
- **Abstract says:** "Findings provided empirical support for key assumptions of the E&C model, particularly the central role of sense of current threat"
- **Problem:** "Confirmed" implies a decisive test; the authors claim support for assumptions in an exploratory idiographic framework.
- **Suggested fix:** "Individual networks supported the model's central claim that 'sense of current threat' sits at the hub."

*Note: the Tim Dalgleish/MRC CBU affiliation in the pitch is unverifiable from abstract only — the PubMed record carries no affiliations. Confirm before pitching. The 41-completer EMA subsample, 2,594 observations and four subtypes are all accurate, and the exploratory status of the clusters is flagged.*

**Cross-cutting note for the editor:** the most consistent pattern in this digest is a *headline* that makes a stronger or slightly different claim than the entry's own body text and Caveats — Studies 3, 4, 7, 13 and especially 17. The Caveats sections are generally strong and honest; the headlines are running ahead of them. A single pass rewriting headlines to match the body would clear most of the flagged issues. The second pattern is treating a non-significant group-by-condition interaction as evidence of an effect within every subgroup (Studies 5 and 11) — worth a standing rule.
