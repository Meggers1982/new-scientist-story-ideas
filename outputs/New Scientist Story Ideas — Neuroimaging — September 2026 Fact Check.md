### Study 1: People who donate a kidney to a stranger show a reversed brain map of social closeness
**PMID:** 42667790 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Core numbers check out: 75 percent of social-circle-sensitive volume favoring the closest circle in controls, 30/70 reversal in altruists, temporoparietal junction as the focus, demographically matched controls, and the absence of a reported N are all accurately conveyed.

**Issue A. Organ type over-specified** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "people who had donated a kidney or liver lobe to an anonymous stranger"
- **Abstract says:** "extraordinary altruists, who had donated a vital organ to an anonymous recipient"
- **Problem:** The abstract says only "a vital organ." Whether liver-lobe donors were included is unverifiable from abstract only, and the headline commits to kidneys specifically.
- **Suggested fix:** "people who had donated a vital organ — such as a kidney — to an anonymous stranger"

**Issue B. "Treats that stranger roughly as most of us treat a sibling"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the finding that their brain treats that stranger roughly as most of us treat a sibling"
- **Abstract says:** "only 30% of this brain volume preferred the closest circle, while 70% showed stronger responses to more distant social circles… the closest vs. distant response contrast was significantly smaller in extraordinary altruists"
- **Problem:** The data show reduced selectivity for the innermost circle and a majority of voxels favoring distant circles — not that a stranger is processed equivalently to a named close relative. The paper's own title ("A sibling to all") invites this reading, but the pitch should not present it as a measured equivalence.
- **Suggested fix:** "the finding that their brains show markedly less preference for their closest ties, allocating more of the social-distance signal to people further out"

**Issue C. NS fit score of 8 rests on an unreported sample size** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "**NS fit:** 8/10"
- **Abstract says:** No N is reported for either group.
- **Problem:** An 8 is defensible only if the sample is adequate; the digest's own caveat concedes it "is almost certainly small," and percentage-of-voxels metrics are threshold-sensitive. A 7+ score on an unknown, probably small fMRI sample needs hedging.
- **Suggested fix:** Score 7/10 pending confirmation of group sizes from the full text.

### Study 2: A brain marker for loneliness shows up across depression, schizophrenia and Alzheimer's
**PMID:** 42044556 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Sample sizes (N=167, N=152; mega-analyses N=317, N=291), subgroup range (26–44), the replicated dmPFC connectivity association, the extension to MDD, the failed replication of the emotional-faces result, and the industry disclosures are all correctly represented.

**Issue A. Author affiliation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call Simone Braak or Martien Kas (University of Groningen), who led the PRISM consortium work."
- **Abstract says:** Braak S — "Amsterdam UMC, location Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, Department of Psychiatry"; Kas MJH — "Groningen Institute for Evolutionary Life Sciences, University of Groningen."
- **Problem:** The parenthetical attaches Groningen to both names; Braak is at Amsterdam UMC. The PRISM consortium attribution is also unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Call Simone Braak (Amsterdam UMC) or Martien Kas (University of Groningen)."

**Issue B. Social dysfunction and loneliness treated as two outcomes** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "was associated with higher social dysfunction and loneliness scores across all groups"
- **Abstract says:** "higher mean SFS+LON social dysfunction scores"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a single composite of the Social Functioning Scale and the De Jong-Gierveld Loneliness scale, not two separately tested outcomes. The headline's "brain marker for loneliness" inherits the same slippage.
- **Suggested fix:** "was associated with higher scores on a combined social-functioning-and-loneliness measure across all groups"

### Study 3: People with epilepsy in rural China are now dying of strokes and heart disease, not accidents
**PMID:** 42704311 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Cohort size (17,515), person-years (25,922), 381 deaths, SMR 3.51, circulatory 47.8 percent with cerebrovascular 28.9 percent and heart disease 18.9 percent, and brain malignancy SMR 20.30 are all accurate.

**Issue A. A study co-author pitched as outside commentary** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Call Ley Liu or Dong Zhou at West China Hospital, or Josemir Sander at UCL's Queen Square Institute of Neurology for international context."
- **Abstract says:** Author list includes "Sander JW(1)(8)(9)" with affiliations "Queen Square Institute of Neurology, London, UK" and "Chalfont Centre for Epilepsy."
- **Problem:** Sander is a co-author on this paper. Offering him as a source "for international context" reads as an independent outside voice and would waste a writer's credibility if pitched that way.
- **Suggested fix:** "Call Ley Liu or Dong Zhou at West China Hospital; note that Josemir Sander (Queen Square Institute of Neurology) is a co-author and cannot serve as the independent voice — source that separately."

**Issue B. Author first name not supported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Call Ley Liu or Dong Zhou at West China Hospital"
- **Abstract says:** "Liu L" (last author), "Zhou D"
- **Problem:** "Ley Liu" is an expansion of "Liu L" that cannot be verified from the abstract; check the full text before printing a name.
- **Suggested fix:** "Call corresponding author L. Liu, or Dong Zhou, at West China Hospital"

**Issue C. Cause-of-death comparison overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "rather than the drownings, burns and injuries that dominated earlier studies in low-resource settings" / headline: "not accidents"
- **Abstract says:** "Earlier studies in resource-limited settings suggested that accidental deaths were the main causes"
- **Problem:** The abstract says "accidental deaths" generically — drownings and burns are not specified — and it does not report what proportion of this cohort's deaths were accidental, so "not accidents" is an inference rather than a reported contrast.
- **Suggested fix:** "rather than the accidental deaths that earlier studies in resource-limited settings identified as the leading cause"; headline: "…are now dying mostly of strokes and heart disease"

### Study 4: Sleep apnea leaves a fingerprint in the fine wiring of grey matter
**PMID:** 42687385 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

Group sizes (24/26/23 plus 24 controls), the NODDI method, ODI changes being more widespread than NDI, and the bidirectional pattern are all accurate, and the pitch explicitly warns against inferring causation for cognitive symptoms.

**Issue A. Causal claim about prior imaging literature** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Conventional MRI shows that sleep apnea shrinks brain tissue"
- **Abstract says:** "cortical structural alterations have been detected via conventional neuroimaging techniques"
- **Problem:** The abstract describes detected structural alterations, not demonstrated tissue shrinkage, and certainly not sleep apnea *causing* it. The sentence sets up the "Why it matters" with a causal premise the cited work doesn't supply.
- **Suggested fix:** "Conventional MRI has detected structural differences in the brains of people with sleep apnea; this suggests the underlying cytoarchitecture differs too, rather than tissue being simply lost"

**Issue B. Prevalence figure not from this study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Around a billion people have obstructive sleep apnea and most are undiagnosed"
- **Abstract says:** "Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is a common chronic multisystem disorder"
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only. The billion figure is widely cited from separate epidemiology and needs its own citation in any pitch.
- **Suggested fix:** "OSA is extremely common — [cite Benjafield et al. 2019 for the ~936 million estimate] — and most cases go undiagnosed"

### Study 5: Bilingual children's brains predict the next word in the language they don't learn at school
**PMID:** 42667920 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=88, ages 7–12, Spanish heritage language, fNIRS, the successful surprisal-tracking result, the uninterpretable individual-differences trends, and the Brookes royalty disclosure are all correctly reported.

**Issue A. Comparison to monolingual brains is not in the study** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "this shows children's brains are doing the same predictive work in Spanish that monolingual brains do in English" / "predicting upcoming Spanish words as fluently as any listener"
- **Abstract says:** "The children demonstrated successful neural tracking of word predictability in their heritage language, suggesting they can form rich linguistic representations in Spanish."
- **Problem:** There was no monolingual group and no English condition; no equivalence was tested. The digest's own caveat notes the absence of a monolingual comparison, but the body text and pitch angle assert exactly that comparison, contradicting the caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "this shows children's brains are doing predictive work in Spanish of the kind documented in majority-language listening — though this study included no monolingual or English comparison"

**Issue B. "Large language model" / GPT framing** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a large language model is being used as a measuring instrument for a child's brain" / "surprisal estimates from GPT-style systems"
- **Abstract says:** "a novel computational approach to quantifying linguistic predictions"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not name the model or identify it as an LLM. Unverifiable from abstract only — confirm the model class before building the methodological hook on it.
- **Suggested fix:** "a computational language model is being used as a measuring instrument for a child's brain (confirm model type with authors)"

### Study 6: Brain scans of "digital addiction" converge on the same three regions — and on serotonin
**PMID:** 42190596 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The three GMV-reduction clusters (bilateral ACC, left MFG, left mOFC), the serotonergic/GABAergic spatial correspondence, the 534 genes and their enrichment for synaptic and ion-channel function are all accurate, and the cross-sectional direction-of-causation caveat is well handled.

**Issue A. "It's probably not dopamine" overstates the abstract** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "implicated serotonergic and GABAergic systems rather than the dopamine story usually told about screens" / "The counterintuitive line is 'it's probably not dopamine.'"
- **Abstract says:** "The findings provide convergent evidence for the involvement of serotonergic, inhibitory, and reward-related neurobiological systems"
- **Problem:** The authors explicitly retain reward-related systems in their conclusion. Spatial correspondence with serotonergic and GABAergic maps does not exclude dopaminergic involvement, and the abstract does not frame the result as a rebuttal of dopamine accounts. The pitch angle's whole hook rests on an exclusion the study does not make.
- **Suggested fix:** "implicated serotonergic and GABAergic systems alongside reward-related circuitry, broadening the dopamine-centric story usually told about screens" — and reframe the pitch as "it's not *only* dopamine."

### Study 7: In frontotemporal dementia, poor memory scores don't mean a damaged memory system
**PMID:** 40995692 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=300, the group composition, the correlation gradient (MCI r=−.58 > AD r=−.36 ≈ controls r=−.35 > non-significant bvFTD r=−.21), and the loss of several group differences after propensity score matching are all accurately reported, as is the absence of a stated bvFTD subgroup size.

**Issue A. "Memory architecture relatively intact" goes beyond the data** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "People with bvFTD can fail memory tests while their memory architecture is relatively intact"
- **Abstract says:** "Significant differences in memory tests and MTL volumes followed the pattern: controls > MCI > bvFTD > AD… AD is primarily marked by MTL-related impairments, which are less pronounced in bvFTD."
- **Problem:** bvFTD participants had significantly smaller MTL volumes than controls and MCI — their medial temporal structures were not intact, only less affected than in AD. The absent correlation means MTL volume doesn't explain their memory scores, which is a different claim from structural preservation.
- **Suggested fix:** "In bvFTD, memory scores are not explained by medial temporal lobe volume at all — MTL atrophy is less pronounced than in Alzheimer's, and frontal contributions appear to drive the test failures"

### Study 8: Your brain treats "will they cheat me?" completely differently from "could I lose?"
**PMID:** 42341477 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=31, the parametric trust game, the three manipulated factors, the null behavioral effect of agency, and the dmPFC encoding of agency despite that null are all accurate.

**Issue A. Behavioral effect of temptation misstated** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Behaviorally, people responded almost entirely to their own risk and largely ignored the partner's incentive or agency."
- **Abstract says:** "trust declined with increasing risk and with the risk × temptation interaction, owing to self-centered heuristics, while agency had no reliable effect."
- **Problem:** Partner temptation *did* influence behavior, in interaction with risk. Only agency had no reliable behavioral effect. Lumping temptation in with agency misstates which factor produced the brain-behavior dissociation the whole pitch is built on. The body's "all three were encoded separately" is also loose — temptation was encoded in interaction with risk.
- **Suggested fix:** "Behaviorally, people responded to their own risk and to risk-by-temptation interactions, but a partner's agency had no reliable effect on choices. Neurally, agency was nonetheless distinguished in dorsomedial prefrontal cortex."

**Issue B. Study population and setting not stated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Thirty-one participants played a parametric trust game in an fMRI scanner" / Caveats: "single-center"
- **Abstract says:** Author affiliations are University of Tehran, Iran University of Medical Sciences, George Mason University and University of Mannheim; the abstract gives no participant demographics or recruitment site.
- **Problem:** The digest asserts a single center without support and omits that the scanning was most likely done in Iran, which is relevant for a study about trust norms. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** Add to Caveats: "Recruitment site and participant demographics are not stated in the abstract; author affiliations suggest data collection in Tehran — confirm before generalizing about 'trust decisions' broadly."

### Study 9: A model using genes, autism and EEG predicts which delayed children will have intellectual disability
**PMID:** 42634999 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=2,453, the 2014–2023 window, minimum follow-up to 60 months, 28 candidate predictors, AUC 0.783, the 0.015 AUC band, the three strongest risk factors, the protective adaptive DQ, the 83.0 percent ID rate, the 31.7 percent NPV and the authors' own triage-not-screening caveat are all accurate.

**Issue A. Miscount of the non-regression algorithms** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the quiet embarrassment that four machine learning methods couldn't beat logistic regression"
- **Abstract says:** "Five algorithms—L2- and L1-regularised logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost and LightGBM"
- **Problem:** Two of the five are logistic regressions. Only three tree-based methods failed to beat the calibrated L2 model, not four.
- **Suggested fix:** "the quiet embarrassment that three tree-based machine learning methods couldn't beat a calibrated logistic regression"

### Study 10: Learning a language in later life helps — but only a narrow slice of thinking
**PMID:** 42684539 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. The "small but reliable" pooled effect, its confinement to higher-order executive processes (executive coordination, attentional regulation, working memory), the explicit absence of broad general-cognition enhancement, the frontal-parietal and memory-network imaging findings, and the review-not-primary-data status are all correctly represented. Author affiliations (Wu at Jiangsu Normal University; Centre for Cognitive and Brain Sciences, University of Macau) check out. Framing is appropriately deflationary and the caveats about small, short, unblinded training studies are consistent with a review-level claim.

### Study 11: Schizophrenia's motivation symptoms track how rigidly the cortex is linked together
**PMID:** 42176687 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

No factual errors found. N=203 across three independent sites, the ENIGMA Lifespan normative modeling, the MAP/EXP structural covariance networks, the selective MAP finding (β = 0.085, p = 0.03) with no corresponding EXP effect, and the "over-coupling / network rigidity" interpretation all match the abstract. The NIH/DOE/NSF funding disclosure is carried into Caveats, and the pitch explicitly labels the result correlational and not yet a treatment target. A 4/10 fit score is defensible for a small effect in a technical framework.

### Study 12: The cerebellum turns up as an unexpected marker of problem gaming
**PMID:** 42727812 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

N=784 (453 IGD, 331 controls), ages 17–32, balanced accuracy 62.60 vs 57.65 percent, the convergence of CAM, Grad-CAM and SVM on the cerebellum, and the failure of any IAT correlation to survive correction are all accurate. The "this is a clue, not a test" framing is appropriately blunt.

**Issue A. Lateralization dropped** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Nodal efficiency in cerebellar lobule X, vermal lobule IX and left thalamus correlated negatively with DSM-5 symptom severity"
- **Abstract says:** "nodal efficiency was significantly and negatively correlated with DSM-5 severity in the left cerebellar lobule X, vermal lobule IX, and left thalamus, with a marginally significant correlation in the right hippocampus"
- **Problem:** The cerebellar finding is specifically left lobule X; the digest drops the side. The marginally significant right hippocampus result is also omitted, which matters because the paper's title frames a cerebello-thalamo-hippocampal network.
- **Suggested fix:** "Nodal efficiency in the left cerebellar lobule X, vermal lobule IX and left thalamus correlated negatively with DSM-5 symptom severity, with a marginal effect in the right hippocampus"

### Study 13: Mitochondria may shape how fast time feels — but the evidence is thin
**PMID:** 42669931 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

The task battery, the physiological and neuroimaging measures, the null group comparison, the differential association patterns and the authors' own "weak evidence" characterization are all faithfully reported, and the digest correctly leads with the null.

**Issue A. Contradictory sample size for the healthy group** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Sixty-five healthy adults (n=59) and 36 people carrying rare mitochondrial DNA mutations"
- **Abstract says:** "a cohort of healthy adults (n = 59) and individuals carrying rare mitochondrial DNA mutations affecting mitochondrial energy transformation (n = 36)"
- **Problem:** The sentence gives two different numbers for the same group. "Sixty-five" appears nowhere in the abstract; the healthy group is n = 59 and the total cohort is 95.
- **Suggested fix:** "Fifty-nine healthy adults and 36 people carrying rare mitochondrial DNA mutations"

### Study 14: A genetic epilepsy comes with a second kind of seizure that scans can't see
**PMID:** 42704296 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

27 individuals with genetically confirmed PCDH19-clustering epilepsy, eight (29.6 percent) with functional seizures, median FDS onset at 16, six initially misdiagnosed with consequent ASM escalation and hospitalizations, and improvement in all treated individuals are all accurate. The "functional does not mean fake" framing is well judged and the caveats cover the retrospective, referral-biased design.

**Issue A. Headline says "scans" for an EEG finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** Headline: "A genetic epilepsy comes with a second kind of seizure that scans can't see"
- **Abstract says:** Diagnosis was per ILAE criteria using "neurophysiological" among other data; the distinguishing feature of functional seizures is the absence of ictal epileptiform activity on EEG.
- **Problem:** EEG is not a scan, and the study collected neuroimaging data separately. For a neuroimaging-focused digest this risks a reader assuming MRI findings that the abstract does not report.
- **Suggested fix:** "A genetic epilepsy comes with a second kind of seizure that doesn't show up on EEG"

**Issue B. Claim about clinician reasoning not tested** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "having a proven genetic epilepsy makes clinicians *less* likely to consider that a given seizure is functional, and that assumption leads to years of unnecessary medication"
- **Abstract says:** "Six patients were initially misdiagnosed with epileptic seizures, resulting in unnecessary antiseizure medication (ASM) escalation and hospitalizations."
- **Problem:** The abstract documents that misdiagnosis happened in six of eight cases; it does not measure clinician reasoning, compare against a group without a genetic diagnosis, or report duration ("years") of inappropriate treatment.
- **Suggested fix:** "Six of the eight were initially misdiagnosed with epileptic seizures and had their medication escalated — raising the question of whether a confirmed genetic diagnosis makes clinicians less likely to consider a functional cause"
